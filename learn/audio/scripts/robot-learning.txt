Supervised learning is told the right answer. Reinforcement learning is only told how well it did, later, and has to work out which of its actions deserve the credit. That one difference produces the whole subject, from a dot learning to cross a grid, to the P P O that aligns language models, to the policies that now fold laundry. This episode follows the chapter's road from the math of reward, through why robots break it, to vision language action models.

The formal object is the Markov decision process: states, actions, transition dynamics, a reward, and a discount gamma. The Markov property, that the next state depends only on the current state and action, makes everything tractable, and it is exactly what real robots violate. The goal is to maximize the return, the discounted sum of future rewards. The state value function says how good it is to be in a state and then follow the policy; the action value function, Q, says how good it is to take a particular action first. Both satisfy the Bellman equation, which you should be able to say in one sentence: the value of now equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of next. The optimal policy is greedy with respect to Q star, and everything from tabular Q learning to G R P O is a way of estimating these quantities when the dynamics are unknown.

Let me do the chapter's three state example out loud, because the fixed point in it is the heart of the method. States A, B, and C, with C terminal. From A or B you can stay for reward zero, or advance for reward minus one, except that advancing from B to C pays plus ten. Gamma is zero point nine. Work backward. C is terminal, value zero. In B, advancing pays ten; staying pays zero point nine times the value of B, which is nine. So B is worth ten. In A, advancing pays minus one plus zero point nine times ten, which is eight; staying pays zero point nine times the value of A, which is seven point two. So A is worth eight. Notice the stay rows reference the value of the very state we are solving. That self reference is why Bellman equations are solved by iteration, value iteration or its sample based cousin Q learning, rather than in closed form. The chapter's gridworld animation is that iteration made visible: the star pays plus one, the pits pay minus one, every step costs a little, and you watch value propagate backward from the goal one cell per visit while arrows show the greedy policy forming.

Three distinctions to keep straight. Exploration versus exploitation: epsilon greedy pays a short term cost for information, and in the gridworld the whole difference between the short path and a long one is epsilon. On policy versus off policy: P P O learns about the policy that generated the data; Q learning and SAC learn about the greedy policy from any behavior data, which is what lets them reuse a replay buffer and logged demonstrations. The max in Q learning's target is exactly what makes it off policy. And model free versus model based: learn values from experience, or learn the dynamics and plan inside them, which is far more sample efficient when the model is good and catastrophic when it is not. Simulators are models, so robotics lives on that boundary.

Tables die at the first camera image. D Q N in twenty fifteen made networks work in Q learning with a replay buffer and a frozen target network. Policy gradient methods instead parameterize the policy directly, essential when actions are continuous and argmax is impossible. One identity does the work: the gradient of a probability equals the probability times the gradient of its log, which turns the gradient of an expectation into something you can sample. REINFORCE reads: raise the log probability of actions followed by high return, lower those followed by low return. Unbiased, and horribly high variance. Subtract the state value as a baseline and you are weighting by the advantage; learn that value with a second network and you have an actor critic. The chapter's launch angle animation shows this literally: above average shots in green get upweighted, below average ones in red get downweighted, and the spread shrinks. Variance is the whole story.

P P O, from Schulman and colleagues in twenty seventeen, fixes the step size problem: one large update can move a policy where its own data is useless. Compute the ratio of the new policy's probability to the old one's for each action, and take the minimum of ratio times advantage and clipped ratio times advantage, clipped at about twenty percent. Past the clip the objective goes flat, so you can take several epochs of updates on the same rollouts without the policy running away. The chapter's P P O update is twenty-five lines of PyTorch, and the comment at the bottom is the important one: swap the observation for a prompt plus partial response, the action for the next token, and the reward for a reward model score with a K L penalty, and you have R L H F. G R P O is P P O with the critic removed and the group mean as baseline. Read that code once and you have read the heart of both fields. On real robots, where every sample costs time and wear, the off policy choice is SAC, from Haarnoja and colleagues in twenty eighteen, which maximizes return plus entropy and is ten to a hundred times more sample efficient.

Why do robots break the clean M D P. Actions are continuous and high dimensional. Observations are partial, so the state is really a history. Control runs at ten to fifty hertz. Data is expensive and unsafe: a simulator gives a million steps an hour, a real robot gives thousands and needs a human to reset the scene. And the sim to real gap, answered by domain randomization and system identification, is far easier for walking than for contact rich manipulation, which is why manipulation went the imitation route. One idea softens several of these at once: action chunking. Emit the next fifty actions per inference instead of one. It cuts inference frequency, smooths motion, and, the surprising finding from the ACT paper, reduces compounding error, because the policy commits to a coherent short plan. The chapter's two link arm animation lets you raise the chunk size and watch inference load fall.

Imitation learning treats demonstrations as a supervised dataset. Its classic failure is covariate shift: the demonstrator never visited the slightly off states the learner drifts into, so errors compound, quadratically in the horizon at worst. DAgger, from Ross and colleagues in twenty eleven, has the expert label the states the learner actually visits, but needs an expert on call. The modern remedies are architectural. ACT, from Zhao and colleagues and the ALOHA paper in twenty twenty-three, outputs a chunk of actions from a transformer wrapped in a conditional variational autoencoder, so two valid ways to grasp do not average into a useless middle, and reached eighty to ninety percent success on fine bimanual tasks from fifty demonstrations. Diffusion Policy, from Chi and colleagues in twenty twenty-three, samples instead of regressing: train a network to predict the noise added to demonstration action chunks, then start from pure noise and denoise for a handful of steps. Denoisers represent multimodal distributions, which is why they handle left or right around the obstacle where a mean squared error head drives straight into it. The chapter's denoising animation resolves noise into a smooth path, and resampling the noise may commit it to the other route. Flow matching, which pi zero uses, learns a straight line velocity field from noise to data instead.

Generalist policies repeat the story of language models: pool data across tasks and robots, put a pretrained backbone in front, and let language specify the task. R T two in twenty twenty-three put a vision language model in front and emitted actions as text tokens. Open X Embodiment pooled a million episodes from twenty-two robots and showed cross embodiment transfer is real. OpenVLA, from Kim and colleagues in twenty twenty-four, is a seven billion parameter Llama two with binned actions. Pi zero, from Physical Intelligence in twenty twenty-four, pairs a three billion parameter PaliGemma backbone with a small action expert that emits continuous chunks of fifty actions by flow matching, and SmolVLA is LeRobot's open four hundred fifty million parameter V L A. Two lessons: action representation moved from discrete tokens to continuous flow heads, and the V L M is the pretraining. A V L A is a V L M whose output modality is motion.

Here is the latency budget, worked, because it decides architecture. Fifty hertz control means twenty milliseconds per tick. Two camera frames at about two hundred fifty-six tokens each plus a short instruction is about five hundred forty tokens of prefill on a three billion parameter backbone, forty to sixty milliseconds on a consumer GPU. A flow matching expert with ten steps over a fifty action chunk: thirty to fifty milliseconds. Total about one hundred milliseconds, five ticks, while the chunk covers fifty ticks, so the next chunk is ready long before the current one runs out. With token actions instead, seven action dimensions times one decode step each at twenty-five milliseconds is one hundred seventy-five milliseconds for a single action, barely five hertz. That arithmetic is why every recent V L A has a continuous action expert and why asynchronous inference matters. The chapter's pipeline animation lets you switch models and watch inference time against the ticks the chunk covers.

R L for robots and for language models is the same math with different reward sources. In R L H F the reward arrives once at the end, which is why G R P O can drop the critic. Language R L moved to verifiable rewards because learned reward models get exploited; robotics has the mirror problem, success is cheap to verify in simulation and expensive in the real world. So the frontier is R L post training of V L As: imitation gets you to seventy percent, R L on the robot's own rollouts gets you to ninety-five, DAgger without the human. Offline R L and learned world models are the two bets on getting there with less real data.

Data comes from teleoperation, a leader arm you move by hand and a follower that mirrors it, which LeRobot's S O one hundred kits reproduce for a few hundred dollars. The LeRobot dataset format is the open standard: a Parquet table of per frame state, action, and timestamps plus camera video, with action chunks assembled at load time. Evaluation is success rate over twenty to fifty episodes on LIBERO or SimplerEnv in simulation, and on real hardware with fixed initial configurations and video, because the same checkpoint on another lab's robot can lose thirty points.

Two interview questions. Why is Q learning off policy: the max in its target. Why did manipulation go to imitation and diffusion rather than R L: rewards, resets, and safety make R L on a real arm painful, and diffusion heads solve the multimodal demonstration problem that broke plain behavioral cloning.

Before the deep R L algorithms, the chapter now walks the ladder that leads to them, because every modern method is one rung above something in the first six chapters of Sutton and Barto. The bottom rung is the multi-armed bandit, a slot machine problem with no state at all, solved with epsilon greedy, upper confidence bounds, or Thompson sampling. Add a context the agent cannot change and you have a contextual bandit, and here is the bridge that matters: R L H F for language models is literally a contextual bandit. A prompt arrives, the policy writes one whole response, a reward model scores it once, and the episode ends. Nothing the policy does changes a next state. Keep that in mind, because it explains why so much of P P O's machinery can be thrown away for language models.

The next rung is Monte Carlo. To estimate the value of a state, run whole episodes, record the return that followed each visit, and average. First visit Monte Carlo prediction is unbiased, because the sampled return is exactly the quantity you want, but it is high variance, because every return sums dozens of random rewards, and it cannot update until an episode ends. Monte Carlo control does the same for action values and keeps an epsilon soft policy so that every action is still tried. The REINFORCE policy gradient from the next section is Monte Carlo applied to policies, and inherits exactly the same variance problem.

Temporal difference learning fixes the waiting. T D zero updates after every single step by replacing the unknown return with one reward plus the discounted value estimate of the next state, which is called bootstrapping. That trade is the heart of the subject: temporal difference is biased, because the target leans on possibly wrong estimates, but it is low variance and learns online. N step returns and T D lambda with eligibility traces interpolate between the two extremes, and generalized advantage estimation inside P P O is that same interpolation applied to advantages. For control, sarsa and Q learning differ by one symbol in the target. Sarsa uses the action the policy actually takes next, so it is on policy and learns the safe path away from the cliff edge. Q learning uses the max over next actions, so it is off policy, learns the optimal edge hugging path, and falls off the cliff during training. The chapter's random walk interactive, straight out of Sutton and Barto, runs Monte Carlo and T D zero on the same episodes of a five state chain; watch T D's error drop faster at a moderate learning rate and Monte Carlo stay noisier. That one picture is the bias variance trade.

Tables die at a few thousand states, so the next rung replaces them with a neural network, and theory gets nervous. Sutton and Barto's deadly triad says that bootstrapping plus off policy learning plus function approximation can diverge, and D Q N is all three at once. Its two famous additions are exactly the stabilizers: a replay buffer that breaks the correlation between consecutive samples so the updates look like supervised learning, and a target network, a lagged copy of the weights, so the target stops chasing the estimate it is computed from. Double D Q N, dueling networks, and prioritized replay each patch one named flaw.

From values to policies is three variance reduction steps and one stability step. Subtract a baseline, use the advantage, estimate it with a critic, and you have an actor critic. Then the stability problem: one large policy gradient step can change the policy so much that the data it came from no longer describes it, and performance collapses. Schulman and colleagues answered that first with T R P O in twenty fifteen, a K L trust region around the old policy, and then with P P O in twenty seventeen, which gets the same protection with a first order trick: compute the ratio of new to old probability for each action, clip it to a band around one, and take the minimum of the clipped and unclipped objectives. Beyond the band, in the direction the advantage pushes, the curve goes flat and the gradient vanishes, so the policy cannot keep moving on that sample. The P P O clipping interactive on the page plots that surrogate against the ratio with an epsilon slider, and you can drag the current ratio into the flat region and watch the gradient read zero.

Finally the language model branch, which is where the bandit bridge pays off. R L H F with P P O needs a learned reward model, a frozen reference model with a K L penalty, a critic, and expensive rollouts, four models in memory for what is formally a one step bandit. D P O, from Rafailov and colleagues in twenty twenty-three, removes the reward model and the rollouts with one observation: under the Bradley Terry preference model, the K L regularized objective has a closed form optimal policy, so the reward can be written in terms of the policy itself and cancelled out. What remains is a classification loss on preference pairs that raises the log probability margin of the chosen response over the rejected one, measured relative to the reference model, with beta setting how far from the reference you may drift. It is cheap and stable, and its weakness is that it only reshuffles probability among responses it has already seen. I P O, K T O, O R P O, and SimPO each trade one of its flaws for another. G R P O goes back to on policy sampling but exploits the bandit structure: sample a group of answers per prompt, score them with a verifier, and use the score minus the group mean as the advantage, so no critic is trained. The systems consequences of that choice are the R L Systems chapter.
