Supervised learning is told the right answer. Reinforcement learning is only told how well it did, later, and has to work out which of its actions deserve the credit. That one difference produces the whole subject, from a dot learning to cross a grid, to the P P O that aligns language models, to the policies that now fold laundry. This episode follows the chapter's road: the math of learning from reward, why robots break it, how imitation took over manipulation, and how vision language action models turned a chatbot backbone into a controller.

The formal object is the Markov decision process: states, actions, a transition function, a reward, and a discount gamma. At each step the agent sees a state, picks an action, the environment samples the next state and pays a reward. The Markov property, that the next state depends only on the current state and action, is what makes everything tractable, and it is exactly what real robots violate. The goal is to maximize the return, the discounted sum of future rewards. The state value function says how good it is to be in a state and then follow the policy; the action value function, Q, says how good it is to take a particular action first. Both satisfy the Bellman equation, which you should be able to say in one sentence: the value of now equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of next. The optimal policy is greedy with respect to Q star, and everything from tabular Q learning to G R P O is a way of estimating these quantities when the dynamics are unknown and the state space is enormous.

Let me do the chapter's three state example out loud, because the fixed point in it is the heart of the method. States A, B, and C, with C terminal. From A or B you can stay for reward zero, or advance for reward minus one, except that advancing from B to C pays plus ten. Gamma is zero point nine. Work backward. C is terminal, value zero. In B, advancing pays ten; staying pays zero point nine times the value of B, which is nine. So B is worth ten. In A, advancing pays minus one plus zero point nine times ten, which is eight; staying pays zero point nine times the value of A, which is seven point two. So A is worth eight. Notice the stay rows reference the value of the very state we are solving. That self reference is why Bellman equations are solved by iteration, value iteration or its sample based cousin Q learning, rather than in closed form. The chapter's gridworld animation is that iteration made visible: the star pays plus one, the pits pay minus one, every step costs a little, and you watch value propagate backward from the goal one cell per visit while arrows show the greedy policy forming.

Three distinctions to keep straight. Exploration versus exploitation: epsilon greedy pays a short term cost for information, and in the gridworld the whole difference between the short path and a long one is epsilon. On policy versus off policy: P P O learns about the policy that generated the data; Q learning and SAC learn about the greedy policy from any behavior data, which is what lets them reuse a replay buffer and logged demonstrations. The max in Q learning's target is exactly what makes it off policy. And model free versus model based: learn values from experience, or learn the dynamics and plan inside them, which is far more sample efficient when the model is good and catastrophic when it is not. Simulators are models, so robotics lives on that boundary.

Tables die at the first camera image. D Q N in twenty fifteen made networks work in Q learning with a replay buffer and a frozen target network. Policy gradient methods instead parameterize the policy directly, essential when actions are continuous and argmax is impossible. One identity does the work: the gradient of a probability equals the probability times the gradient of its log, which turns the gradient of an expectation into something you can sample. REINFORCE reads: raise the log probability of actions followed by high return, lower those followed by low return. Unbiased, and horribly high variance. Subtract the state value as a baseline and you are weighting by the advantage; learn that value with a second network and you have an actor critic. The chapter's launch angle animation shows this literally: above average shots in green get upweighted, below average ones in red get downweighted, and the spread shrinks. Variance is the whole story.

P P O, from Schulman and colleagues in twenty seventeen, fixes the step size problem: one large update can move a policy where its own data is useless. Compute the ratio of the new policy's probability to the old one's for each action, and take the minimum of ratio times advantage and clipped ratio times advantage, clipped at about plus or minus twenty percent. Past the clip, in the direction the advantage favors, the objective goes flat and the gradient is zero, so you can take several epochs of minibatch updates on the same rollouts without the policy running away. The chapter's P P O update is twenty-five lines of PyTorch, and the comment at the bottom is the important one: swap the observation for a prompt plus partial response, the action for the next token, the distribution for the language model's softmax, and the reward for a reward model score with a K L penalty to the reference model, and you have R L H F. G R P O is P P O with the critic removed and the group mean as the baseline. Read that code once and you have read the heart of both fields. On real robots, where every sample costs wall clock time and wear, the off policy choice is SAC, from Haarnoja and colleagues in twenty eighteen, which maximizes return plus policy entropy and is ten to a hundred times more sample efficient.

Why do robots break the clean M D P. Actions are continuous and high dimensional, eight numbers for an arm and gripper, fifty for a humanoid. Observations are partial, so the state is really a history. Control runs at ten to fifty hertz in real time. Data is expensive and unsafe: a simulator gives a million steps an hour, a real robot gives thousands and breaks things and needs a human to reset the scene. And the sim to real gap, friction, latency, lighting, answered by domain randomization and system identification, is far easier for walking than for contact rich manipulation. That is why manipulation went the imitation route. One idea softens several of these at once: action chunking. Emit the next fifty actions per inference instead of one. It cuts inference frequency, smooths motion, and, the surprising finding from the ACT paper, reduces compounding error, because the policy commits to a coherent short plan instead of re deciding every twenty milliseconds. The chapter's two link arm animation puts a random policy beside a trained one and lets you raise the chunk size and watch inference load fall.

Imitation learning treats demonstrations as a supervised dataset and regresses action from observation. Its classic failure is covariate shift: the demonstrator never visited the slightly off states the learner drifts into, so errors compound, quadratically in the horizon in the worst case. DAgger, from Ross and colleagues in twenty eleven, has the expert label the states the learner actually visits, but needs an expert on call. The modern remedies are architectural. ACT, from Zhao and colleagues and the ALOHA paper in twenty twenty-three, outputs a chunk of actions from a transformer wrapped in a conditional variational autoencoder, so two valid ways to grasp do not average into a useless middle; it reached eighty to ninety percent success on fine bimanual tasks from about fifty demonstrations. Diffusion Policy, from Chi and colleagues in twenty twenty-three, samples instead of regressing: train a network to predict the noise added to demonstration action chunks, then at inference start from pure noise and denoise for a handful of D D I M steps. Denoisers represent arbitrarily multimodal distributions, which is why they handle left or right around the obstacle where a mean squared error head would drive straight into it. The chapter's denoising animation resolves a cloud of noise into a smooth path, and if you resample the noise it may commit to the other route. Flow matching is the refinement pi zero uses: learn a velocity field from noise to data along straight lines and integrate it in a few steps.

Generalist policies repeat the story of language models: pool data across tasks and robots, put a pretrained backbone in front, and let language specify the task. R T two in twenty twenty-three put a vision language model in front and emitted actions as text tokens. Open X Embodiment pooled a million episodes from twenty-two robots and showed cross embodiment transfer is real. OpenVLA, from Kim and colleagues in twenty twenty-four, is a seven billion parameter Llama two with binned actions. Pi zero, from Physical Intelligence in twenty twenty-four, pairs a three billion parameter PaliGemma backbone with a small action expert that emits continuous chunks of fifty actions by flow matching, and SmolVLA is LeRobot's open four hundred fifty million parameter V L A. Two lessons: action representation moved from discrete tokens to continuous flow heads, and the V L M is the pretraining. A V L A is a V L M whose output modality is motion.

Here is the latency budget, worked, because it decides architecture. Fifty hertz control means twenty milliseconds per tick. Two small camera frames at about two hundred fifty-six tokens each plus a twenty token instruction is about five hundred forty tokens of prefill on a three billion parameter backbone: forty to sixty milliseconds on a consumer GPU. A flow matching expert with ten steps over a fifty action chunk: thirty to fifty milliseconds. Total about one hundred milliseconds, five ticks, while the chunk covers fifty ticks, a full second, so the next chunk is ready long before the current one runs out. Now do it with token actions: seven action dimensions times one decode step each at about twenty-five milliseconds is one hundred seventy-five milliseconds on top of prefill, for a single action, barely five hertz. That arithmetic is why every recent V L A has a continuous action expert and why asynchronous inference matters on cheap hardware. The chapter's pipeline animation lets you switch models and watch inference time against the ticks the chunk covers.

R L for robots and for language models is the same math with different reward sources. In R L H F the reward arrives once at the end, which is why G R P O can drop the critic. Language R L moved to verifiable rewards because learned reward models get exploited; robotics has the mirror problem, success is cheap to verify in simulation and expensive in the real world. So the frontier is R L post training of V L As: imitation gets you to seventy percent, R L on the robot's own rollouts gets you to ninety-five, DAgger without the human. Offline R L and learned world models are the two bets on getting there with less real data.

Data comes from teleoperation, a leader arm you move by hand and a follower that mirrors it, which LeRobot's S O one hundred kits reproduce for a few hundred dollars. The LeRobot dataset format is the open standard: a Parquet table of per frame state, action, and timestamps plus camera video, with action chunks assembled at load time. Evaluation is success rate over twenty to fifty episodes on LIBERO or SimplerEnv in simulation, and on real hardware with fixed initial configurations and video, because the same checkpoint on another lab's robot can lose thirty points.

Two interview questions. Why is Q learning off policy. Because of the max in its target. And why did manipulation go to imitation and diffusion rather than R L. Because rewards, resets, and safety make R L on a real arm painful, and diffusion heads solve the multimodal demonstration problem that broke plain behavioral cloning.
