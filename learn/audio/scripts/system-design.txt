A system design interview is forty five minutes of structured collaboration, not a monologue, and the candidates who do well share two habits: they quantify before they design, and they volunteer tradeoffs instead of waiting to be cross examined. This chapter compresses Alex Xu's System Design Interview, volumes one and two, into one page: the framework, the back of the envelope math you can do live, the building blocks with the animations that make them stick, and the single trick behind every one of the twenty eight book problems. It is the companion to Step twenty one of the study plan.

Start with the framework and the clock. Step one, understand and scope, takes five to ten minutes. Ask about users, scale in daily actives and queries per second, which features are in and out, the read to write mix, and the latency and consistency needs, and write the agreed requirements down. Step two, high level design, takes ten to fifteen minutes: draw the end to end box diagram, clients to load balancer to services to cache to storage to asynchronous pipelines, and get buy in before going deep. Step three, the deep dive, is another ten to fifteen minutes on one or two components, the data model, the sharding, the hot path, a failure mode. This is where seniority shows. Step four, the wrap up, covers bottlenecks, failure handling, and monitoring. Never say it is done.

The estimation step exists because a three thousand Q P S system and a three hundred thousand Q P S system are different designs. The point is not precision; it is catching order of magnitude errors in ninety seconds. Memorize three anchors. A day is about eighty six thousand four hundred seconds, which you round to one hundred thousand for mental math. One million requests per day is about twelve Q P S. And the latency ladder, which I will come to. Everything else is multiplication. Take the book's Twitter example. Three hundred million daily active users, each posting about two tweets a day, is six hundred million writes a day, and six hundred million divided by roughly a hundred thousand seconds is about seven thousand write Q P S on average. A one hundred to one read to write ratio makes that about seven hundred thousand read Q P S, and you plan for two to three times that at peak. The moment you say, so reads dominate and I will design around caching and fan out, you have earned the next ten minutes. The calculator on the page lets you move those sliders and watch Q P S, yearly storage, and cache size update.

The latency ladder you are allowed to bring. A main memory reference is about a hundred nanoseconds. An S S D random read is about a hundred microseconds, which is your database index hit budget. A round trip inside one datacenter is about half a millisecond, so a request can afford a handful of internal hops, while a round trip from California to the Netherlands is about one hundred fifty milliseconds, which makes cross region synchronous replication a product decision rather than a technical one. Availability nines: three nines is about eight and a half hours of downtime a year, four nines is about fifty two minutes, five nines is about five minutes. And powers of two: two to the tenth is a thousand, two to the twentieth a million, two to the thirtieth a billion, so one billion hundred byte records is about a hundred gigabytes without touching a calculator.

Now the building blocks. Load balancers are the front door. Layer four balances on I P and port, fast and blind to content; layer seven reads the request and can route by path, cookie, or model name, which is why an L L M gateway is a layer seven balancer. Caching follows the eighty twenty rule: a cache sized for twenty percent of the day's objects serves about eighty percent of reads. Cache aside is the default pattern, read the cache, miss to the database, fill the cache. Set T T L's, evict with L R U, and defend against the thundering herd, where one hot key expires and ten thousand requests stampede the database, using request coalescing or staggered expirations.

Sharding splits rows across databases by key when one machine cannot hold the data or the write load. Hash sharding spreads load evenly but kills range queries; range sharding keeps scans cheap but invites hot spots. Replication has a leader taking writes and followers serving reads and taking over on failure. Asynchronous replication is fast but can lose the last writes in a failover; synchronous is safe but slow across regions. Whenever you put a read replica in a design, mention replication lag, because read your own writes breaks the moment a user's read hits a stale follower.

Consistent hashing is the fix for resharding, and it comes from the Dynamo paper from Amazon in two thousand seven, the same paper that gives us quorums and hinted handoff. Keys and nodes hash onto the same circle, and each key belongs to the first node clockwise. Add or remove a node and only the keys in the affected arc change owner; everything else stays put. Compare that with hash modulo n, where changing n remaps almost everything. Real systems give each physical node many virtual nodes to even out the arcs. The ring animation on the page lets you add and remove nodes and watch exactly which keys flash and move.

Message queues are a buffer between producers and consumers. The web tier acknowledges fast, workers process at their own pace, and traffic spikes become queue depth instead of dropped requests, which is back pressure made visible. In an interview, a queue is the answer whenever work can be asynchronous: notifications, feed fan out, video transcoding, analytics.

Rate limiting usually means a token bucket. The bucket holds up to a fixed number of tokens and refills at a steady rate; each request spends one. A burst drains saved up tokens and is allowed, then further requests bounce with a four twenty nine until refill catches up. That is the signature: short bursts permitted, long run average enforced. Unique I D generation at scale means Snowflake: sixty four bits laid out as a sign bit, forty one bits of millisecond timestamp, five bits of datacenter, five bits of machine, and twelve bits of sequence, so each machine mints thousands of time-sortable I D's per millisecond with no coordination.

On storage, be honest about S Q L versus NoSQL. Start relational: transactions, joins, secondary indexes, and fifty years of tooling are features you give up, not defaults you escape. Reach for NoSQL with a concrete reason, write volume beyond what sharded S Q L handles gracefully, genuinely schema free data, or pure key value access at massive scale.

Inside a Dynamo style key value store, the book's deepest chapter, four ideas carry the deep dive. Quorums: each key lives on n replicas, a write succeeds after w acknowledgments, a read queries r replicas, and if w plus r is greater than n the read and write sets overlap, so a read sees at least one up to date copy. Vector clocks: per replica version counters that detect concurrent writes, where the store keeps both versions and the client reconciles, or you accept last write wins and quietly drop data. Merkle trees: hash trees over key ranges, so two replicas compare roots and descend only into differing subtrees, syncing gigabytes of divergence by exchanging kilobytes of hashes.

The storage engine underneath matters too. In an L S M tree, writes go to an in memory sorted memtable, and when it fills it is flushed as an immutable sorted file, sequential I O, the thing disks love. B trees update pages in place instead: faster point reads, slower random writes. L S M means Cassandra, RocksDB, and LevelDB, the write heavy half of every design a chat system or a metrics store answer. Kleppmann's Designing Data Intensive Applications is the book for this layer.

Cap in one breath. When a network partition happens, and it will, you choose: refuse requests to stay consistent, or answer with possibly stale data to stay available. With no partition there is no tradeoff, and a system that is both consistent and available at all times is a fiction. Banks pick consistency for balances; social feeds pick availability and converge later. The useful interview move is choosing per feature: the payment ledger is consistent, the like counter is available, and you say so. Idempotency is how retries become safe: give every mutating request a client generated key, deduplicate on the server, and a retried payment charges once. That is also the honest story behind exactly once delivery. Messaging systems give you at least once, and exactly once processing is at least once delivery plus idempotent consumers. Say that sentence and watch the interviewer relax.

The twenty eight problems each hang on one trick, and the page has the full table. A few to carry in your head. The U R L shortener is a counter encoded in base sixty two. The news feed is fan out on write to followers' feed caches, except celebrities, who are fan out on read and merged at query time. Chat is web sockets plus per user inbox queues with per channel message I D's for ordering. Ad click aggregation is stream processing made exactly once through idempotency and watermarks, plus a nightly batch reconciliation. The payment system is idempotency keys plus an append only double entry ledger plus reconciliation, never updating balances in place.

Increasingly the deep dive is design an L L M serving system, and this notebook has a whole chapter of the internals. The sketch they expect: client to an A P I gateway doing authentication, token bucket rate limiting, and metering; then a prefix aware router; then a prefill pool; then K V cache transfer; then a decode pool running continuous batching with paged attention; then streaming back to the client, with autoscaling driven by goodput under time to first token and time per token objectives.
