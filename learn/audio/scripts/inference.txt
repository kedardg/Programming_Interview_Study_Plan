When you send a prompt to a language model, the hardware does two completely different jobs, one after the other, and almost every design decision in a serving engine comes from the fact that those two jobs stress the machine in opposite ways. This is the inference systems chapter: prefill and decode, the K V cache, continuous batching, paged attention, scheduling against latency targets, and speculative decoding.

Start with the two phases. Prefill runs the whole prompt through the model at once. Every layer sees all the prompt tokens together, so the matrix multiplies are large and the tensor cores stay busy. Prefill is compute bound. It produces the first output token and the keys and values of every prompt token, saved for later. Decode then generates the response one token at a time. Each step feeds only the single newest token through the model, but attention still has to look at every previous key and value. The matrices are skinny, batch by one token, so the G P U spends almost all its time streaming weights and cached K V out of high bandwidth memory rather than multiplying. Decode is memory bandwidth bound.

Those two phases map onto the two latency metrics every serving benchmark reports. Time to first token is dominated by prefill; it is what makes a chat feel responsive. Time per output token is dominated by decode; it is what makes streaming feel fast. Total latency is roughly time to first token plus time per output token times the number of output tokens. Because decode is bandwidth bound, there is a clean floor: one decode step cannot be faster than the time to read every active byte, weights plus cache, from memory once. A seventy B model in F P sixteen is about one hundred forty gigabytes of weights. On an H one hundred with three point three five terabytes per second of bandwidth, that floor is about forty two milliseconds per token at batch one. That single number is why batching and parallelism exist.

Now the K V cache itself. Why cache at all? Attention for the newest token needs the softmax of its query against all previous keys, applied to all previous values. Without a cache, every decode step would recompute every past token's keys and values, turning linear generation into quadratic work. The cost is captured by one formula you should know cold: K V bytes equals two, for keys and values, times the number of layers, times the number of K V heads, times the head dimension, times the number of cached tokens, times the batch size, times the bytes per element.

Let us do the worked example from the melchi dot me article the study plan uses. Take a hypothetical seventy B model with full multi head attention: eighty layers, sixty four K V heads, head dimension one hundred twenty eight, in B F sixteen, two bytes per element. Per token that is two times eighty times sixty four times one hundred twenty eight times two bytes, about two point six megabytes. At thirty two thousand tokens of context that is about eighty gigabytes per sequence, and with sixteen concurrent users it is about one point two five tebibytes of cache. The weights of that same model are about one hundred forty gigabytes. The cache is nine times the model. That is the whole story of why serving side attention variants exist.

Each variant attacks one factor of the product. Multi query attention shares a single K V head across all query heads, dividing the cache by the number of heads. Grouped query attention, used by Llama two and three and Mistral, lets groups of query heads share K V heads, so sixty four query heads over eight K V heads cuts the cache eight times. Multi head latent attention, from DeepSeek V two, compresses keys and values into a small shared latent vector and decompresses on the fly, for a reduction of ten times or more. Open the calculator on the page, pick the full M H A seventy B, set thirty two K context and sixteen users, and then switch to the G Q A Llama seventy B and watch the number drop eight fold.

Next, batching. Batch one decode wastes the G P U: reading one hundred forty gigabytes of weights to produce one token is an arithmetic intensity far below one flop per byte, on hardware that balances at hundreds. The fix is to serve many sequences at once, so each weight read is amortized across the whole batch. But how you batch matters as much as whether you batch. Static batching groups requests, runs them in lockstep, and returns when the longest one finishes. Short requests sit idle, padded, holding memory, while stragglers finish. Continuous batching, introduced as iteration level scheduling in the Orca paper by Yu and colleagues at O S D I twenty twenty two, makes the batch a living thing: after every decode iteration, finished sequences leave and waiting requests join. No padding, no waiting for stragglers. It is the backbone of every modern engine.

There is a subtlety. A newly admitted request still needs its prefill, which is long and compute bound, and running it stalls every decoding request in the batch for that iteration. Sarathi Serve, by Agrawal and colleagues at O S D I twenty twenty four, answers with chunked prefill: split the prompt into chunks and co schedule one chunk alongside the batch's decode steps each iteration, smoothing the stall into a small bounded overhead. That is why chunked prefill is a flag in v L L M and friends.

Continuous batching created a memory problem, and paged attention solved it. If each sequence pre allocates a contiguous K V region sized for its maximum possible length, most of it is never used. The v L L M paper by Kwon and colleagues in twenty twenty three measured only twenty to thirty eight percent of K V memory doing useful work in prior systems; the rest was lost to fragmentation and reservations. Paged attention borrows the oldest trick in operating systems. The cache is carved into fixed size blocks of, say, sixteen tokens. A per sequence block table maps logical positions to physical blocks allocated on demand. Waste is bounded to the last partially filled block per sequence, and the same physical block can back many sequences: a shared system prompt is stored once and referenced by every request that uses it, with copy on write when a sequence diverges.

Prefix sharing generalizes. S G Lang's radix attention keeps finished sequences' K V in a radix tree keyed by token prefix, so few shot prompts, chat histories, and agent loops all reuse cached computation automatically.

Now scheduling. Throughput alone is a bad target, because a scheduler can always raise tokens per second by batching harder at the cost of each user's latency. Production systems optimize goodput: requests served within their latency objectives, a time to first token bound and a time per output token bound, per unit of hardware. The Etalon paper formalizes this. The levers are admission and preemption, where v L L M preempts whole sequences when the cache fills rather than running out of memory; the chunked prefill ratio, where bigger chunks help the new request's first token but hurt everyone else's per token latency; and prefill decode disaggregation. DistServe, by Zhong and colleagues at O S D I twenty twenty four, and Splitwise from Microsoft run prefill and decode on separate G P U pools, each provisioned and parallelized for its own regime, shipping the cache between them. That removes phase interference entirely and lets you scale first token capacity and per token capacity independently. The price is moving K V over the network, which is what N I X L and Nvidia Dynamo are built for.

Then speculative decoding, which spends decode's idle compute to cut latency. Introduced by Leviathan, Kalman and Matias in twenty twenty three, and in parallel by Chen and colleagues at DeepMind, the idea is this. A cheap draft model proposes k tokens. The target model scores all k in one parallel forward pass, which costs barely more than scoring one token because that pass is bandwidth bound. Accepted tokens are kept; at the first rejection the token is resampled from a corrected distribution. The acceptance rule is constructed so the output distribution is mathematically identical to sampling the target model alone. It is a lossless speedup, typically two to three times when draft and target agree often. Medusa adds extra prediction heads on the target model; Eagle drafts from the target's own hidden states and has the best acceptance rates.

Two more pieces. Structured decoding guarantees output shape for agents and A P Is by masking invalid tokens at each step with a grammar compiled ahead of time; X Grammar precomputes the automaton masks so the per token overhead is microseconds, and the Guiding L L Ms the Right Way paper shows how to mask without distorting the model's distribution. And the engines: v L L M, the default with paged attention and the broadest model support; S G Lang, built around radix attention and structured programs, strongest when prefixes are reused; and TensorRT L L M, compiled kernels and F P eight and F P four paths for peak Nvidia performance.

Misconceptions to clear up. Decode is not slow because the model is big in flops; it is slow because every token re reads the weights, which is why a smaller batch does not make it proportionally faster. The K V cache is not a minor detail; at long context it is the memory budget. And speculative decoding is not an approximation; done correctly it is exact. The classic interview question is: walk me through one request from arrival to last token, and where the G P U is idle. Narrate prefill, admission into the running batch, block allocation, the per step cache sweep, and eviction, and you have this chapter.
