Here is a strange fact about the last seven years of language models. If you took GPT-2 from 2019 and set it next to a frontier model from this year, almost every block would look familiar. Tokens go in, they become vectors, those vectors pass through a stack of mixing layers and feed-forward layers wrapped in residual connections and normalization, and a final head predicts the next token. The shape never changed. What changed is every component inside that shape, and each change was a fix for a specific failure that only appears at scale. This chapter is the story of those fixes.

Let's start with the dense lineage, from GPT-2 to Llama 3. The modern dense model differs from GPT-2 by about six targeted substitutions. First, normalization moved from post-norm layer norm to pre-norm R M S norm. Pre-norm keeps the residual stream as a clean identity path, which is why hundred-layer stacks train stably, and R M S norm drops the mean centering and the bias for the same quality with cheaper kernels. Second, positions moved from learned absolute embeddings to rotary position embeddings, or RoPE, which encode relative position by rotating query and key pairs, and which can be stretched to longer contexts with scaling tricks like YaRN. Third, the feed-forward block moved from a four-times-wide GELU M L P to a gated SwiGLU M L P at about eight thirds width, because gating consistently wins at equal compute. Fourth, attention moved from full multi-head attention to grouped query attention, G Q A, which shrinks the key value cache eight times or more. Fifth, biases disappeared everywhere. Sixth, vocabularies grew from fifty thousand tokens to one hundred twenty eight thousand or more, so each document costs fewer tokens.

Here is the lesson behind that table, and it is the single most useful thing to say in an interview about architectures. Architecture changes get adopted when they fix a training stability problem or an inference cost problem. They almost never get adopted because they raise a benchmark score at small scale. G Q A and M L A entered pretraining purely because of serving arithmetic. Remember the formula from the inference chapter: key value cache bytes equal two, times layers, times K V heads, times head dimension, times cached tokens, times batch, times bytes per element. A hypothetical seventy billion parameter model with full multi-head attention, sixty four K V heads, serving sixteen users at thirty two thousand tokens of context, needs about one point two five tebibytes of cache, nine times its own weights. Switch to G Q A with eight K V heads and that drops eight times. That is why Llama 2 and 3 use G Q A. It is a serving decision baked into pretraining.

Now mixture of experts, M O E. A dense model spends every parameter on every token. M O E breaks that coupling. Replace the M L P with E expert M L Ps and a tiny router that sends each token to its top k experts. Total parameters grow with E, which is knowledge capacity. Per-token compute grows only with k. DeepSeek V3, from the DeepSeek V3 technical report of December 2024, is the canonical modern datapoint: six hundred seventy one billion total parameters, thirty seven billion active per token. That is frontier-class capacity at mid-size compute. Mixtral uses top two of eight experts. DeepSeek uses top eight of two hundred fifty six fine-grained experts, plus one shared expert that is always on, handling common knowledge so the routed experts can specialize.

The engineering is all in the router. Left alone, routers collapse. A slight early preference for a few experts means those experts get more gradient, get better, get picked more, and the others starve. The classic fix is an auxiliary load balancing loss that pushes usage toward uniform, but that loss fights quality. DeepSeek V3's answer is auxiliary-loss-free balancing: a per-expert bias term that is adjusted only for routing decisions, leaving the gradients clean. And the systems bill is real. Experts live on different GPUs, so every M O E layer costs two all-to-all communications, dispatch and combine. That is why libraries like DeepEP and the expert placement load balancer exist, and why the AWS Neuron kernel library ships separate M O E kernels for prefill and decode.

Next, state space models. Attention's superpower is also its bill: a cache that grows linearly with context, and a decode step that must re-read all of it. State space models make the opposite trade. They compress the entire past into a fixed size recurrent state, so decode cost and memory are constant in sequence length. The Mamba paper by Gu and Dao, from December 2023, added selectivity: the transition and input gates are functions of the current token, so the model can choose what to write into its state and what to forget, recovering some of attention's content-based addressing. Mamba 2, in 2024, showed that this selective scan can be computed with matrix multiply shaped primitives, making it tensor-core friendly and revealing a formal duality with linear attention. Training parallelism comes from the associative scan, the same parallel scan primitive you build in the kernels chapter.

The honest scorecard: state space models win on long-context throughput and memory. Pure state space models lag attention on exact recall, needle in a haystack, copying, in-context lookup, because a fixed state must lossily compress the past. So the production answer became hybrids. Interleave mostly state-space or linear-attention layers with a few full attention layers. The cheap layers carry bulk sequence mixing; the few attention layers provide exact recall. Jamba, Nemotron H, Falcon H1, IBM Granite 4, and Kimi Linear all follow this recipe. And notice something: Gated DeltaNet, Mamba 2, and selective scans all appear as kernels in the Neuron kernel library's experimental catalog. New architectures become real exactly when someone writes fast kernels for them.

Two more cheap mixers. Linear attention replaces softmax of Q K transpose times V with a feature map applied to Q and K, and associativity turns that into a running d by d state, recurrent at decode and parallel at training. Modern gated variants, gated linear attention and Gated DeltaNet, add forgetting and delta-rule updates that fix the quality gap of the early versions. Sliding window attention keeps exact softmax attention but only over the last w tokens, so cost is n times w and the cache is capped at w. Alone it loses global context. In a hybrid ratio it is now mainstream: Gemma 3 runs five local layers per one global layer, and GPT-OSS alternates banded and dense layers. The pattern to remember is that full attention has become a scarce resource spent on a few layers, with cheap mixers doing everything else.

Multi-token prediction is the clearest example of architectures being co-designed with inference. DeepSeek V3 trains an extra M T P head that predicts the token after next through a small additional block. The training signal gets denser, but the real payoff is serving: that head is a built-in draft model for speculative decoding, with high acceptance and no separate draft model to deploy. M L A exists to shrink the cache. Fine-grained M O E assumes expert parallelism. Window-to-global ratios are tuned against paged key value implementations. Reading a model card now means reading its serving plan.

Multimodal architectures come in three patterns. The LLaVA pattern uses a pretrained vision encoder, a small projector that maps image patches into the language model's embedding space, and fine-tuning on interleaved data. Early fusion models consume text, image, and audio tokens from the start of pretraining, which gives better cross-modal reasoning at much higher training cost. And generation-side decoders bolt image or audio generation onto the same trunk. The serving implication is that images are thousands of prefill tokens, so chunked prefill matters even more.

Now the data. This chapter includes a chart computed from Sebastian Raschka's architecture gallery dataset, one hundred nine open-weight models from 2019 to 2026, showing the share of models released each year that use each component. In 2026, G Q A appears in forty six of seventy models, M O E in forty one, sliding windows in twenty seven, hybrids with state-space or linear-attention layers in twenty three, M T P in twenty three, Q K norm in twenty two, and M L A in twenty. Compare 2025, where hybrids were four out of thirty two. The frontier moved from rare to mainstream in one year.

The 2025 and 2026 developments go further. Gated attention puts a learned sigmoid gate on the attention output. NoPE drops positional encoding on global layers while keeping RoPE on local ones, which helps length extrapolation. DeepSeek sparse attention uses a lightweight indexer to select the top two thousand or so past tokens per query, and GLM 5.2 reuses each indexer result across the next three layers. Manifold-constrained hyper-connections split the residual stream into several parallel streams. Short convolutions preprocess queries, keys, and values before linear-attention layers. Cross-layer K V sharing lets global layers share one key value set, which Gemma 4 uses to fit on edge devices. And looped stacks like Ouro re-apply the same layers several times with a learned exit, buying depth without parameters.

Common misconceptions. First, M O E does not make inference cheap in memory: all experts must be resident, so memory scales with total parameters, only compute scales with active parameters. Second, a bigger context window is not free even with a cache; it is linear in memory and in decode bandwidth. Third, state space models are not simply better than attention; they trade recall for efficiency, which is why hybrids exist. A good interview question is: explain why Llama 3 switched to G Q A, and compute the cache savings. Another is: what breaks when you remove load balancing from an M O E router, and how did DeepSeek V3 solve it without an auxiliary loss?

There is one more axis the chapter adds: how text gets generated at all. Every model so far has used causal language modeling, a decoder-only transformer with a causal mask predicting the next token from everything before it. It won for three plain reasons. One objective covers every task you can phrase as continuation, every position in every sequence is supervised in a single pass, and sampling is just the training computation fed its own outputs. The causal mask is also why the K V cache exists: earlier tokens never look at later ones, so incremental decoding comes for free. Masked language modeling, the BERT family, is the bidirectional alternative, a superb encoder for embeddings and rerankers but awkward for generation, because every pass recomputes every position. Prefix language models and encoder decoder models like T five and U L two sit in between, bidirectional over the input and causal over the output, which is still the right shape for translation and speech to text.

Diffusion language models take the masked idea all the way. In masked diffusion the noise is masking itself: training masks a random fraction of tokens, from none to all, and generation starts from a fully masked canvas and reveals the most confident tokens over a chosen number of denoising steps, sometimes re-masking a weak one so a later step can revise it. The number of forward passes is the number of steps, not the length of the text. LLaDA, Dream, Mercury, and Gemini Diffusion are this family. Continuous diffusion models instead add Gaussian noise to token embeddings and denoise in continuous space like image diffusion; their hard part is rounding back to discrete tokens. Be careful with the acronym C D M: it can mean continuous diffusion models, or conditional diffusion models, any diffusion model conditioned on text or a class, which is about conditioning rather than the data. Open the masked diffusion unmasking interactive and drag the steps slider. At six steps the diffusion model finishes in six passes where the autoregressive baseline needs twenty four, but every diffusion pass is a full bidirectional sweep with no cache, and fewer steps commit more tokens blind. Diffusion shines at infilling, editing, and low latency on short outputs, and still lags on long range coherence, which is why block diffusion, autoregressive across blocks and parallel within them, is the hybrid to watch.

The second axis is how long a model thinks. In Kahneman's terms, system one is fast automatic pattern completion, and a causal model answering directly is exactly that: one pass per token, the same compute for any question. System two is deliberate computation, which for language models means test time compute: chain of thought writes intermediate steps, self consistency samples several chains and votes, search and verification spend more still, and the reasoning models, o one, DeepSeek R one, Qwen three in thinking mode, bake it in by training with reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards to think at length before answering. Architecturally they are ordinary causal models; what changed is the policy and the serving bill, since thousands of decode tokens per query push load toward decode. Thinking budgets cap the private tokens, hybrid thinking modes let one model run either way, and latent reasoning moves deliberation out of the token stream: Coconut feeds the last hidden state back as the next input, and looped models like Ouro iterate the same layers until an exit gate fires, system two inside the forward pass. The loop closes with system two distillation, from Distilling System Two into System One by Yu and colleagues in twenty twenty four and DeepSeek's R one distilled models: run the slow method, keep answers that pass a check, fine tune the model to produce them directly. It transfers the answers the teacher found, not the ability to search for new ones. Open the accuracy versus inference cost interactive and move the self consistency and thinking budget sliders; the curves are illustrative, but their shape is the published shape, including the dashed distilled student that gets most of the way there at a fraction of the cost on in-distribution problems.

Before the state-space section, a word about what came before transformers, because every why transformers answer is really a comparison with recurrent networks. A vanilla R N N carries a hidden state through time, updated as tanh of W x times the input plus W h times the previous state. Training unrolls it and backpropagates through time, and the gradient reaching an early step is a product of one Jacobian per step, each a diagonal of tanh derivatives times W h. The tanh derivative only shrinks, and repeated multiplication by W h either shrinks or explodes depending on its largest singular value, so gradients across long gaps vanish or blow up, and a plain R N N cannot learn dependencies more than a few dozen steps long. Clipping fixes the exploding side; nothing in the vanilla cell fixes vanishing.

The L S T M fixes it by separating memory from output. A cell state flows along a highway touched only by element-wise gates, with no matrix multiply and no squashing nonlinearity between steps, while the hidden state is a filtered view of that cell that serves both as the output and as the query for the next step's gates. The forget gate decides what to erase, the input gate what to write, the output gate what to expose, and because the derivative of the cell state with respect to the previous cell state is just the forget gate, a forget gate near one lets gradients travel back many steps intact. That is the whole trick, and the G R U is its two-gate simplification. The new gradients-through-time animation on this page makes it concrete: drag the spectral radius of the recurrent weight and watch the R N N's gradient die or explode within ten steps, then switch to the L S T M cell and watch the forget gate keep it alive. Transformers won because they train all positions in parallel and give any two tokens a direct, constant-length path, at the price of a K V cache that grows with the sequence, and that price is exactly what brought recurrence back as state-space models and gated linear attention, which are the L S T M's gates in hardware-friendly math.
