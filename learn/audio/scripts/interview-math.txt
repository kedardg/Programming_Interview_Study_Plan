Some companies run a dedicated math round. It can be anything from a logic puzzle to a pen-and-paper derivation, and the same material resurfaces inside M L coding and research discussions: what is the variance of this estimator, how many samples do you need, why is that expectation biased. Alisa Liu wrote a set of math notes for exactly one such interview, and those notes are the spine of this chapter. We will follow her order: the lookup table, the distributions, expectation and inequalities, Bayes, the limit theorems, the moves, the puzzle canon, statistics, and a quick sheet of calculus and linear algebra. Every result gets derived aloud, and every one gets a one-line intuition you can say without paper.

Start with the lookup table, because most interview math questions are a short story hiding a standard object, and the fastest skill to build is pattern matching the story to the tool before touching algebra. You hear how many trials until the first success, or how long until the next event, with that memoryless flavor; reach for the geometric and the exponential. You hear trials that each succeed or fail; Bernoulli and binomial. How many events in a fixed window; Poisson. A large sample and an average; the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, approximately Gaussian with variance sigma squared over n. Bound a probability knowing only the mean, or only the mean and variance; Markov and Chebyshev. Estimate a parameter from data; maximum likelihood. Update a belief after evidence; Bayes. The expectation of a function of a random variable; Jensen. The expected number of things with a property; linearity of expectation over indicators. An expected hitting time or probability of reaching a state; first-step analysis. The future depends only on the present; a Markov chain. Choose, arrange, or split into groups; the combinatorics table.

Now the seven distributions you must own, organized on two axes: discrete versus continuous, and counting versus waiting. A Bernoulli with parameter p is one trial. Its mean is p, and here is the trick that saves you in half of all indicator problems: a zero-one variable squared is itself, so the expectation of X squared is also p, and the variance is p minus p squared, which is p times one minus p. A binomial with parameters n and p is the sum of n independent Bernoullis, so by linearity its mean is n p and, by independence, its variance is n p times one minus p. The probability of exactly k successes is n choose k times p to the k times one minus p to the n minus k. Poisson with rate lambda counts events in a fixed window. Derive it as the binomial limit: slice the window into n tiny pieces each with probability lambda over n, send n to infinity, and the binomial becomes lambda to the k times e to the minus lambda over k factorial. Its mean and variance are both lambda. Geometric with parameter p is the number of trials up to and including the first success. Its tail, the probability of needing more than k trials, is one minus p to the k, and that tail form gives memorylessness for free. Its mean of one over p is where first-step analysis makes its debut: with probability p you are done after one trial, with probability one minus p you have spent one trial and are back where you started, so the mean satisfies m equals one plus one minus p times m, giving one over p. The variance is one minus p over p squared.

On the continuous side, uniform on a to b has mean a plus b over two and variance b minus a squared over twelve. Exponential with rate lambda is the continuous waiting time, with survival function e to the minus lambda x. It is memoryless, and it is the only memoryless continuous distribution. Its mean is one over lambda and its variance is one over lambda squared. The minimum of independent exponentials is exponential with the rates added, because the survival functions multiply, and the probability that a particular one is the minimum is its rate over the total rate. And the Gaussian, mean mu and variance sigma squared, is the limit shape of averages; everything reduces to the standard normal through z equals x minus mu over sigma. Heights normal with mean seventy and variance nine, probability above seventy-six is the probability z exceeds two, about two point three percent.

Expectation is the sum, or integral, of x times its probability, and for any function g, the expectation of g of X is the sum of g of x times the probability, without ever finding the distribution of g of X. The tail formula for non-negative variables, expectation equals the integral of the probability that X exceeds x, is the fastest route to many waiting-time means. Three rules, and exactly when they need independence: linearity always holds, dependence is irrelevant, which is why indicator tricks work on wildly dependent events. The expectation of a product factoring, and variances adding, need independence, or at least zero covariance.

The inequalities, each with a one-line proof. Markov: for non-negative X, the probability that X is at least a is at most the mean over a, because the mean cannot be smaller than the contribution of the values above a. Chebyshev: the probability that X is at least k standard deviations from its mean is at most one over k squared; prove it by applying Markov to the non-negative variable X minus mu squared. So at least seventy-five percent of any distribution's mass lies within two standard deviations. Jensen: for a convex function, the function of the mean is at most the mean of the function, and the inequality flips for concave functions. Remember the direction with x squared and a variable that is plus or minus one: the function of the mean is zero, the mean of the function is one. In machine learning this is why the log of an expectation is at least the expectation of the log, which is the evidence lower bound, and why estimating a function of a mean from samples is biased. Cauchy Schwarz is why correlation lives between minus one and one, and the union bound is the crude but independence-free workhorse.

Bayes turns a prior into a posterior after evidence: the probability of A given B is the probability of B given A times the prior on A, over the probability of B, which you compute with the law of total probability. The classic trap is the rare disease test. Prevalence one percent, sensitivity ninety-nine percent, false positive rate five percent. Positive test, probability of disease: point nine nine times point zero one in the numerator, which is point zero zero nine nine, over that plus point zero five times point nine nine, which adds point zero four nine five. The answer is about sixteen point seven percent. The base rate dominates, and interviewers use it to see whether you reach for the denominator.

The limit theorems. For i i d samples with mean mu and variance sigma squared, the sample mean has variance sigma squared over n, so its standard deviation, the standard error, is sigma over root n. The law of large numbers says the sample mean converges to mu; you can prove the weak form from Chebyshev in one line. The central limit theorem says the standardized sum converges to a Gaussian, and it fails for heavy tails with infinite variance or for strong dependence. The operational lesson to say out loud: error shrinks like one over root n, so cutting it tenfold costs a hundredfold more samples. That is why a benchmark difference of a point or two needs thousands of evaluation items. The C L T simulator on the page averages draws from a very skewed exponential, and by about thirty draws per average the histogram is a bell.

Now the moves, the problem types you can learn as procedures. First-step analysis: the expected number of fair coin flips to see two heads in a row. Let E zero be the expected remaining flips when the last flip was tails or you just started, and E one when the last flip was heads. Condition on the next flip: E zero equals one half of one plus E zero plus one half of one plus E one, and E one equals one half of one plus E zero plus one half of one. Solve: E one is one plus half of E zero, substitute, and E zero equals six. Counting in expectation: define an indicator for each item, the expected count is the sum of the probabilities, and dependence does not matter. Expected fixed points of a random permutation of a hundred items: a hundred times one over a hundred, which is one, for any n. Max and min of n variables: the maximum's distribution function is F to the n, the minimum's survival is one minus F to the n. For n uniforms on zero one, the expected maximum is n over n plus one and the expected minimum is one over n plus one; the picture is n points splitting the interval into n plus one gaps of equal expected length. Combinatorics: order matters and no replacement is permutations, n factorial over n minus k factorial; order does not matter is combinations, divide by k factorial; with replacement and order is n to the k; and splitting k identical items into n groups is stars and bars, n plus k minus one choose k. Markov chains: a transition matrix with rows summing to one, a stationary distribution pi with pi equals pi P, expected return time one over pi of i, and absorption probabilities from first-step recurrences. On a symmetric graph like a cycle the stationary distribution is uniform, so the return time to any node is n and the hitting time to a neighbor is n minus one.

Now the puzzle canon, each with its answer and its one-line intuition. The birthday problem: with d days and n people, the probability of no collision is the product of d minus i over d, which is about e to the minus n squared over two d, so collisions appear around root d and cross fifty percent at about one point one eight root d. For three hundred sixty-five days that is twenty-three people. The follow-up is someone sharing your birthday, which needs two hundred fifty-three. Gambler's ruin: start with a dollars, win or lose one dollar per round, stop at N or zero. For a fair coin the probability of reaching N is a over N and the expected duration is a times N minus a; thirty dollars aiming for a hundred is a thirty percent chance over about twenty-one hundred rounds. For a biased coin the probability is one minus q over p to the a, over one minus q over p to the N, and the simulator on the page shows how fast it collapses when p drops to point four five. Underneath is the simple random walk, whose variance after n steps is n, so the typical distance grows like root n.

The coupon collector: with n coupon types, phase k, when you hold k distinct types, lasts a geometric number of boxes with mean n over n minus k, so the total is n times the harmonic number H n, about n times the log of n plus point five seven seven. Fifty coupons take about two hundred twenty-five boxes, and the histogram has a long right tail from unlucky last coupons. Reservoir sampling: to pick k items uniformly from a stream of unknown length, keep the first k, then accept item i with probability k over i and evict a random slot; by induction every item ends up selected with probability k over n. Monty Hall: switching wins two thirds of the time, because your first pick is right one third of the time and the host's reveal moves all the remaining mass to the other door; with ten doors and eight goats opened, switching wins nine tenths. The secretary problem: skip the first n over e candidates, then take the first one better than all of them, and you hire the best with probability about one over e, thirty-seven percent. The longest run of heads in n fair flips is about log base two of n, with only constant-size fluctuations, so a thousand twenty-four flips give a longest run of about ten. Balls into bins: n balls in n bins leave about a fraction one over e of the bins empty, another one over e with exactly one, and the rest with two or more. Information puzzles: to distinguish k possibilities with tests of m outcomes you need at least log base m of k tests, which is why nine coins with one heavier take two weighings. And lattice paths from the origin to m, n are m plus n choose m, with waypoints multiplying.

Statistics. Maximum likelihood chooses the parameter that makes the data most probable: take the log so the product becomes a sum, differentiate, set to zero. For a Bernoulli the estimate is the sample frequency. For an exponential it is one over the sample mean. For a uniform on zero to theta there is no stationary point; the likelihood is maximized by the smallest feasible theta, the sample maximum, a reminder that calculus is a tool and not the definition. Language models are maximum likelihood: minimizing cross entropy is maximizing the log likelihood of the observed tokens. Bias and variance: an estimator is itself a random variable; bias is how far it is off on average, variance is how much it depends on the particular sample, and mean squared error decomposes exactly into bias squared plus variance. The sample variance divided by n is biased low by n minus one over n, which is why you divide by n minus one.

Taylor series approximate a function near a point using its derivatives there: zeroth order a horizontal line, first order the tangent, second order a parabola matching curvature, and the second-order form is where Newton's method and the learning-rate-versus-curvature argument come from. Derivatives to know cold: powers, exponentials, logarithms, sines and cosines, the chain rule, which is backpropagation, the product rule, and the quotient rule. Handy identities: the harmonic number is about log n plus point five seven seven, the geometric-mean arithmetic-mean inequality, e as the limit of one plus one over n to the n, one over e as the limit of one minus one over n to the n and as the derangement fraction, the sums of the first n integers and squares, and the sum of k times r to the k, which is r over one minus r squared.

The linear algebra quick sheet, in one breath: symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, and positive semidefinite means all eigenvalues non-negative, which is why covariance matrices, P C A, and Hessian curvature all live here; the singular value decomposition exists for any matrix, its truncation is the best low-rank approximation, which is the hypothesis behind lora; the gradient of a transpose x is a, the gradient of x transpose A x is A plus A transpose times x; the softmax Jacobian is s i times delta i j minus s j, so the gradient of cross entropy with softmax is simply the softmax minus the one-hot label, the single most asked backward derivation; log-sum-exp with the max pulled out is the stable form and its gradient is the softmax; and never invert a matrix when you can solve, since inversion is order n cubed.
