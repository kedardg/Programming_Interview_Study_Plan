Welcome to the first chapter of the audio course: math foundations for deep learning. Here is the claim this chapter makes, and it is a strong one. Everything in the whole track, attention, backpropagation, quantization, the roofline model, every one of them reduces to four pieces of math: linear algebra, calculus, probability, and floating point arithmetic. If you own these four, nothing later is magic. It is just these four ideas wearing different clothes.

Let us start with linear algebra, and specifically with the humble vector. A vector is an ordered list of numbers, but deep learning leans entirely on its geometric reading. Think of it as an arrow in a space with one axis per number. A four thousand ninety six dimensional embedding is an arrow in a four thousand ninety six dimensional space. And the sentence "two tokens mean similar things" becomes "two arrows point in similar directions."

The tool that measures that is the dot product. It has two faces, and both matter. The first face is a sum: multiply the matching components of two vectors and add them up. That is what the hardware computes. It is a multiply accumulate, the single operation GPUs are built around. One fused multiply add counts as two flops. The second face is geometric: the dot product equals the length of A, times the length of B, times the cosine of the angle between them. That face tells you what the number means. It is positive when the arrows agree, zero when they are perpendicular, which means unrelated, and negative when they oppose. Divide out the lengths and you get cosine similarity, the standard "how alike are these embeddings" metric in retrieval. And here is the connection to keep: every attention score you will ever meet is exactly this. A query vector dotted with a key vector, asking "how relevant is that token to me?"

Let us do one by hand. Take A equals one and two, and B equals three and one. The dot product is one times three plus two times one, which is three plus two, which is five. The length of A is the square root of five, about two point two four. The length of B is the square root of ten, about three point one six. So the cosine of the angle is five divided by two point two four times three point one six, which is five over seven point oh seven, about zero point seven one. The arrows are forty five degrees apart. Positive, fairly aligned. Now flip B to negative three and negative one, and the dot product becomes negative five. Same magnitude, opposite meaning.

Now matrices. The productive way to read a matrix is not as a grid of numbers but as a function, a linear map. It takes a vector in and gives a vector out, and the columns of the matrix tell you exactly where the basis vectors land. Multiply a two by two matrix against every point of a unit square and the square becomes a parallelogram. Rotations, stretches, shears, any combination, as long as lines stay lines and the origin stays put. The determinant is the area scaling factor of that map. It goes negative when the map flips orientation, and it is zero when the map squashes the plane into a line, destroying information. That is exactly why a determinant of zero means "not invertible." You cannot un-squash. A neural network layer, y equals W x plus b, is precisely this: a linear reshaping of space followed by a shift.

Matrix multiplication composes two maps. A times B applied to x means: apply B first, then A. Element by element, each output entry is a dot product between a row of A and a column of B. Now count the work. If A is m by k and B is k by n, there are m times n output entries, and each one needs k multiply adds. So the cost is two times m times n times k flops. For a square n by n matrix multiply that is two n cubed. Cubic growth. A four thousand ninety six cubed multiply is already about one hundred thirty seven gigaflops. Memorize the phrase "two m n k." It is the numerator of every arithmetic intensity and every model flops utilization calculation you will ever do.

And here is why deep learning is matrix multiply all the way down. A batch of activations times a weight matrix is a linear layer. Queries times keys gives attention scores. Scores times values gives attention output. The reason is partly mathematical, linear maps compose and differentiate cleanly, and partly economic. Matmul does order n cubed compute over order n squared data. That is the rare workload dense enough to keep arithmetic units busy, so the hardware evolved to serve it, and then the architectures evolved to use it.

Second pillar: calculus. A derivative answers one question. If I nudge the input a little, how much does the output move? If f prime of x equals three, then a nudge of zero point zero one in x moves f by about zero point zero three. That sensitivity reading scales to millions of inputs. For a loss that depends on every weight in a network, the gradient collects every partial sensitivity into one vector, and that vector points in the direction of steepest increase. Training is then almost embarrassingly simple: repeatedly step the other way. New weights equal old weights minus the learning rate times the gradient.

The chain rule is how we get that gradient through a composition of functions, and a deep network is nothing but a composition of functions. If the loss is f of g of h of x, then the derivative is f prime, times g prime, times h prime, each evaluated at the right place. Every factor is local. A layer only has to know how its own output responds to its own input. Multiply the local factors along the path from the loss back to any weight and you have that weight's gradient. Computing those factors once, back to front, and reusing shared prefixes, is backpropagation. The next chapter animates it edge by edge.

The chapter's gradient descent playground uses a loss surface of one half x squared plus five y squared. That is an elongated valley, steep across, flat along, and it is the classic hard case. Drop a ball and run it. At a small learning rate it zig zags, because the gradient points mostly across the valley rather than along it. Raise the learning rate past about zero point two and the y direction diverges, because each step overshoots the curved direction. Then switch on momentum and watch the zig zags cancel while progress along the valley accumulates. That damping is the whole reason momentum, and later Adam, exist. Every optimizer in the next chapter is a smarter answer to this exact picture.

Third pillar: probability and information theory. A language model's entire output is a probability distribution. Given the context, a probability for every token in the vocabulary. Three tools govern how we create, sample, and train such distributions.

The first is softmax. Models produce raw scores called logits, which can be any real numbers. Softmax exponentiates each one and divides by the sum of all the exponentials, producing a valid distribution that preserves the order of the scores. The temperature knob divides the logits before the exponential. As temperature goes to zero, all the mass piles onto the top score, which is greedy decoding. At temperature one you use the model's native confidence. Large temperatures flatten toward uniform, more diverse and less accurate. Notice what temperature cannot do: it cannot reorder the bars. The ranking is fixed; only the confidence changes. This one function is the last layer of every language model, the normalizer inside attention, and the sampling knob in every inference API.

The second tool is cross entropy. Information theory measures the surprise of an outcome with probability p as negative log p. Certain events carry no surprise; rare events carry a lot. The entropy of a distribution is its average surprise, how unpredictable it is. And the cross entropy between the true distribution p and a model q is the average surprise you feel when reality is drawn from p but you expected q. The key identity is that cross entropy equals the entropy of the data plus the K L divergence from p to q. The entropy part is irreducible. The K L part is the extra surprise caused purely by your model being wrong. So minimizing cross entropy minimizes K L. It drives the model's distribution toward the data's.

Here is the punchline for language models. During pretraining, the true distribution at each position is one hot. The actual next token had probability one. So the whole sum collapses to a single term: the loss is just negative log of the probability the model gave to the correct token. All of pretraining is: be less surprised by the internet. Exponentiate the per token loss and you get perplexity, which reads as "the model was as confused as if it were choosing uniformly among that many tokens." A loss of two nats is a perplexity of about seven point four. And K L divergence returns in post training. R L H F and D P O add a K L penalty against the base model so the tuned policy improves its reward without drifting far from what it learned.

Fourth pillar: numerical computing. Real hardware does not compute with real numbers. It computes with floating point, which is scientific notation in binary. A float has a sign bit, an exponent that chooses which power of two window you are in, and a mantissa that says where you sit inside that window. F P thirty two spends eight bits on the exponent and twenty three on the mantissa, giving a range up to about three times ten to the thirty eight and about seven decimal digits of precision. F P sixteen has five exponent bits and ten mantissa bits: precise, but its maximum value is only sixty five thousand five hundred and four, so it overflows easily. B F sixteen keeps F P thirty two's eight exponent bits and only seven mantissa bits. Same range, three decimal digits. That trade, range over precision, is why B F sixteen became the training default.

Two consequences follow. First, floats have relative precision. The gap between adjacent representable numbers grows with magnitude, so adding a tiny gradient to a large weight can round to nothing. That is one reason optimizers keep F P thirty two master copies of the weights during B F sixteen training. Second, the exponential function overflows catastrophically. E to the eighty nine already exceeds F P thirty two's maximum, and attention logits routinely get that large.

So softmax, as written, is numerically broken. The fix uses an invariance: shifting every logit by the same constant cancels out in the ratio. Subtract the maximum first, so the largest exponent is e to the zero, which is one, and nothing can overflow. Hold onto this trick tightly. The online softmax in the kernels chapter, the beating heart of FlashAttention, is this same max subtraction performed incrementally, updating the running maximum and the running sum as each block of scores streams through on chip memory, so the full score matrix never has to exist at once.

A word on sources. The geometric reading of linear algebra here follows Grant Sanderson's Essence of Linear Algebra series, and the free interactive textbook Interactive Linear Algebra by Margalit and Rabinoff. The probability intuition tracks Brown University's Seeing Theory. And the stable softmax trick, in its streaming form, comes from the two thousand eighteen paper Online Normalizer Calculation for Softmax by Milakov and Gimelshein, which is the paper behind the kernel exercise in step nine of the study plan.

A few misconceptions worth clearing. People say "the gradient points to the minimum." It does not. It points in the direction of steepest increase, locally, and stepping against it only promises local descent, which is why the learning rate and the curvature of the surface matter so much. People also treat temperature as if it changes what the model knows. It does not; it only reshapes confidence around a fixed ranking. And a common interview trap: why do we divide attention scores by the square root of the head dimension? Because dot products of random d dimensional vectors grow like the square root of d, and softmax of large inputs saturates into a one hot, killing gradients. The scaling keeps the scores order one at any width.
