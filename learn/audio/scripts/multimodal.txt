Here is the single idea that makes every multimodal model make sense. A language model never sees pixels, never hears sound, never touches a point cloud. It only ever consumes a sequence of vectors. So every modality, an image, a waveform, a video, a LiDAR scan, the joint angles of a robot arm, gets in the same way: something turns it into vectors, and those vectors are spliced into the same residual stream that carries text. Once you believe that, the field collapses into two questions. What produces the vectors, and where do they enter the model.

Where they enter has only three answers. The first family is encoder plus projector, the LLaVA pattern from Visual Instruction Tuning by Haotian Liu and colleagues in twenty twenty-three. A pretrained vision encoder produces a grid of feature vectors; a small projector, usually a two layer M L P, maps each one into the language model's embedding dimension; the resulting soft tokens are concatenated with the text embeddings, and the language model runs unchanged. It never knows which positions came from an image. Cheap to train, modular, and the dominant open weight pattern: LLaVA, Qwen V L, Pixtral, InternVL, and for audio, Voxtral.

The second family is cross attention with a resampler: Flamingo, from Alayrac and colleagues at DeepMind in twenty twenty-two, and Llama three point two Vision. Visual features stay in a side stream, and the language model's layers gain inserted cross attention blocks whose queries are text and whose keys and values are visual. A Perceiver resampler usually first compresses thousands of encoder outputs to sixty-four or two hundred fifty-six latent tokens. The base model's text behavior is untouched and the sequence length does not grow with the number of images, but you pay for new parameters in every layer.

The third family is early fusion: Chameleon, the Gemini and GPT four o class models, and Gemma three n. Images and audio are turned into discrete tokens by a learned codec and a single transformer is pretrained on mixed sequences from day one. One vocabulary, one model, any to any generation. The cost is training from scratch on enormous interleaved corpora while the modalities compete for shared capacity. The chapter has an animated diagram with a family selector showing all three flows.

Now the vectors, starting with vision. A vision transformer, from An Image is Worth Sixteen by Sixteen Words by Dosovitskiy and colleagues in twenty twenty, does to an image what a tokenizer does to text, except the tokens are geometric. Cut the image into square patches, fourteen or sixteen pixels on a side, flatten each into a vector, multiply by one learned projection, add a position embedding, and you have a sequence. Here is the arithmetic that governs everything. A two hundred twenty-four pixel image with sixteen pixel patches gives two hundred twenty-four over sixteen, which is fourteen, squared, which is one hundred ninety-six tokens. Tokens scale with area, so doubling resolution quadruples the sequence. Push the chapter's patchify slider to one thousand three hundred forty-four pixels and a single image is seven thousand fifty-six tokens, more than most entire text prompts. That is why a vision language request is a prefill dominated request, and why every modern model carries a token reduction strategy: any resolution tiling for fine detail, two by two spatial merging for a four times cut, learned resamplers that compress to a fixed token count, and Qwen two V L's native dynamic resolution with two dimensional rotary positions.

In code this is tiny. The chapter's PatchEmbed module is a convolution whose kernel equals its stride equals the patch size, which is literally flatten plus linear for every patch. The projector is two linear layers with a GELU between them. The merge is plain concatenation at the position of an image placeholder token. The language model has to learn that these vectors mean pixels, which is what stage one alignment does, and because the forward pass cannot tell image positions from text, the serving engine must track that mapping for attention masks, position ids, and prefix cache keys.

Where does the encoder's knowledge come from. Not ImageNet labels. The vision tower in nearly every V L M is CLIP, from Radford and colleagues at OpenAI in twenty twenty-one, or SigLIP from Google, trained on hundreds of millions of image caption pairs with a contrastive objective. For a batch of N pairs, build the N by N matrix of cosine similarities between every image and every caption, and treat each row and column as an N way classification whose right answer is the diagonal. That is InfoNCE. SigLIP swaps the batch softmax for an independent sigmoid per pair, which scales to bigger batches without global normalization. The result is an encoder whose features are already aligned with language, which is exactly why a two layer M L P suffices to connect it. The chapter's clip animation shows the diagonal of an eight by eight similarity matrix being pulled up as training runs.

Now audio. Sound is sampled sixteen to forty-eight thousand times a second, far too many numbers to feed raw. Every pipeline first slides a twenty-five millisecond window across the waveform in ten millisecond hops, takes the Fourier transform of each window, keeps the magnitudes, and bins frequencies on the perceptual mel scale, about eighty bins. A minute of audio becomes a six thousand by eighty image, and the models treat it as one. The chapter's mel animation shows a rising chirp become a diagonal streak and a steady tone a horizontal line.

From there, two routes, and the choice decides whether the model only listens or also speaks. The continuous route is a Whisper style encoder, Radford and colleagues, twenty twenty-two: two strided convolutions that halve the frame rate, then a transformer stack, about fifty vectors per second, then frame stacking or a resampler down to five to twelve tokens per second, then a projector. Qwen Audio and Voxtral follow this recipe; it is the audio twin of LLaVA and inherits strong speech recognition for free. It is also the shape of the Deepgram pipeline from the hardware chapter, and their trick of compiling encoder and decoder for static shapes works because the mel front end fixes the input shape. Control the shape at the spectrogram boundary.

The discrete route makes audio a vocabulary. Neural codecs, SoundStream and EnCodec and Mimi inside Moshi, compress the waveform to a low rate latent, quantize it against learned codebooks, and decode it back. The clever part is residual vector quantization: the first codebook picks the nearest code, the residual is quantized by a second codebook, its residual by a third, for eight to thirty-two stages. Early codebooks carry phonetic content, later ones carry timbre. With audio as tokens, AudioLM generates speech autoregressively, and Moshi reaches full duplex conversation by modeling the user's stream and its own audio and text streams in parallel at twelve and a half hertz, listening and talking at once with about two hundred milliseconds of latency. How many codebooks per step and in what order is the audio cousin of multi token prediction.

Video breaks naive scaling. Frames times tokens per frame: one minute at one frame per second is eleven thousand seven hundred sixty tokens; at two frames per second with larger frames it is ninety-four thousand, longer than most context windows and about one and a half gigabytes of K V cache on an eight B model. So video design spends that budget with sparse frame sampling, tubelets that patchify across time so two frames share one token, temporal pooling, and streaming memory for hour long video. Positions need care too: Qwen two V L's M RoPE gives each token three position ids, for time, row, and column, so attention can express same row, three frames later directly, and resolution changes do not scramble positions.

Three D is where the next chapter's robots live. A LiDAR scan is an unordered set of points, so the tokenizer must be permutation invariant. PointNet, Qi and colleagues, twenty seventeen, uses shared per point M L Ps and a symmetric max pool; PointNet plus plus adds locality by farthest point sampling of well spread centers and grouping each center's nearest neighbors into a patch that a small PointNet encodes into one token. Those point tokens are to a cloud what patches are to an image, and the LLaVA recipe applies, which is exactly PointLLM and three D L L M. From cameras, three D is inferred with NeRF or with three D Gaussian splatting, now the default for reconstruction. And honestly, standard V L Ms are bad at space, because captions rarely say forty centimeters to the left of; benchmarks like V S I Bench show strong models failing counting and left versus right. The fixes are depth tokens, camera pose, synthetic spatial data, and the embodied route, where a wrong spatial answer produces a visibly failed robot action. Other modalities follow the same template: time series patched like a one dimensional ViT, graphs through message passing, proteins as residue tokens, and robot proprioception as plain normalized vectors that skip any big encoder.

The training recipe is three stages, each cheaper than you would guess. Alignment: freeze encoder and language model and train only the projector on about a million caption pairs, hours on eight GPUs. Instruction tuning: unfreeze the language model, often with LoRA, on about a million multimodal instructions with a resolution curriculum. Preference tuning: D P O on hallucination contrast pairs and R L with verifiable rewards on counting and O C R. In Axolotl, the same YAML drives it, and the knobs that matter are max image size, whether the vision tower is frozen, and keeping image tokens contiguous under sample packing.

Two interview questions to rehearse. Why does a V L M hallucinate objects that are not there. Because the language model answers from what captions usually say; the fix is object presence probes like POPE and contrastive preference data. And what changes in serving when requests become multimodal. Not the engine, the workload shape: the encoder is a cacheable separate stage, visual tokens make prefill long and decode short, and prefix caching must key on image content. Size the K V first: one large image with no merging adds seven thousand fifty-six tokens, about one point one gigabytes of cache per request on Llama three seventy B before any text. Omni models that talk back in real time add a streaming audio encoder and interleaved text and audio tokens, with a budget of about three hundred milliseconds from end of speech to first sound, which makes every trick from the inference chapter load bearing.
