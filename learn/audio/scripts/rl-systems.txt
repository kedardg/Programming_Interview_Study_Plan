Here is the thing nobody tells you before your first reinforcement-learning job on a language model: R L post-training is mostly inference. Before a single gradient is computed, an inference engine has to generate hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens of fresh samples, verifiers have to score them, and then the new weights have to be pushed back into that engine so the next round is on-policy. This chapter is about that machine, the trainer-rollout loop at frontier scale, and it is the part of post-training a model-customization lead actually spends their days on.

Start with why R L at all. Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate demonstrations, but it cannot teach the model to search over its own outputs, to try a derivation, notice it fails a check, and prefer the attempt that passes. R L closes that gap by sampling from the model, scoring the samples, and pushing probability toward what scored well. For a decade the scoring came from humans through a reward model optimized with P P O. The twenty twenty-five shift, in DeepSeek-R1, in Tulu 3's recipe for R L with verifiable rewards, and in Kimi K1.5, was to score with verifiers instead: an exact-answer checker for math, unit tests for code, a compiler, a schema validator. Verifiable rewards are cheap, far harder to hack than a reward model, and scale to millions of problems. The visible side effect is the reasoning-length effect: when the reward only cares about the final answer, the policy learns to spend more tokens thinking, because longer chains of thought pass the checker more often.

The objectives are a family with one skeleton: a policy gradient weighted by an advantage, held near the old policy by a ratio clip or a K L penalty. They differ in where the baseline comes from. P P O uses a learned value model per token, which means a critic as large as the policy, doubling memory and compute. G R P O, from DeepSeekMath and the R1 report, uses the group mean and standard deviation of G sampled answers to the same prompt as the baseline, with no critic, which is what made verifiable-reward R L cheap enough to run at scale. Dapo added the engineering fixes that make G R P O train long reasoning stably: an asymmetric clip with a higher upper bound, dynamic sampling that drops groups that are all correct or all wrong, token-level loss aggregation, and soft shaping for overlong responses. Doctor G R P O removed the length and standard-deviation normalization that bias toward long wrong answers. G S P O, from the Qwen team, computes the importance ratio at the sequence level instead of per token, which is what keeps mixture-of-experts models stable. And R L O O and REINFORCE plus plus are plain REINFORCE with variance reduction, competitive with P P O at a fraction of the cost.

Let me say the G R P O objective in words, because the page writes it out and animates it. For one prompt, sample G responses. Each response gets a scalar reward from the verifier, one or zero. Its advantage is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation, and that single advantage is shared by every token in the response. For each token, compute the importance ratio: the probability of that token under the current policy divided by its probability under the policy that generated it. Multiply ratio by advantage, but clip the ratio to within epsilon of one, and take the minimum of the clipped and unclipped versions so that no token moves more than epsilon in ratio per update. Average over tokens and responses, and optionally subtract a K L penalty to a frozen reference, though many verifiable-reward recipes drop it. Read it as: every token of a response that beat its siblings gets pushed up, every token of a response that lost gets pushed down, by the same amount, and the clip stops any token from running away. The page's code is twenty lines: compute group advantages, exponentiate the log-prob difference for the ratio, clamp with the dapo asymmetric bounds, take the negative minimum, mask to model tokens, and average either over all tokens in the batch, which is dapo's token-level mode, or per response first, which is original G R P O.

The G R P O group animation on the page shows eight responses scored by a verifier, the group mean as the baseline, the centered advantages, and one response's token strip with ratios colored green for pushed up, red for pushed down, and hatched where the clip has already zeroed the gradient. Push the solve-rate slider to ninety-five percent and the group becomes all correct: every advantage is zero and the prompt teaches nothing. That is exactly the case dapo's dynamic sampling discards before it wastes a rollout.

Now the machine itself. A production R L system is two systems bolted together, with five stages per step. Rollout: an inference engine, V L L M or S G lang, holding the current policy generates G responses per prompt for a batch of prompts, with continuous batching, paged K V, and, crucially, prefix caching so the G siblings share the prompt's K V. Reward: verifiers, sandboxes, environments, or reward models score every response, usually on CPUs, often the second bottleneck. Advantage: the group statistics, plus optionally a reference-model forward pass for the K L term and an old-policy forward pass to recompute log-probs under the trainer's numerics. Train: an F S D P 2 or Megatron trainer runs forward and backward over the sampled tokens and takes an optimizer step. Weight sync: the new weights are resharded from the trainer's data-parallel layout into the engine's tensor-parallel and expert-parallel layout and pushed back, and the loop repeats.

The animation that matters most here is the timeline of where the time goes. Rollout dominates, and it has a long tail: the step cannot finish until the longest response finishes, so most engine GPUs idle waiting for a few stragglers. The page lets you switch between three topologies. Colocated and synchronous, which is verl's HybridFlow design: a single Python controller on Ray drives both roles on the same GPUs, time-slicing between engine and trainer. It is the simplest, one pool, weights move by CUDA I P C or an in-process reshard, no second cluster to schedule, and it is the right default for dense models up to tens of billions of parameters and for teams that want one job to debug. The cost is that while the engine generates, the trainer sits idle, and while the trainer steps, the engine's K V cache is torn down. Disaggregated and synchronous runs rollout and training on separate pools, each provisioned for its own regime, high tensor and expert parallelism for a fast engine, F S D P or Megatron for trainer throughput, at the cost of a real network hop for weights, and the trainer GPUs still idle during rollout. Disaggregated and asynchronous, which is the slime and Miles lineage and the prime-R L style, lets the engine keep generating with weights from one or two steps ago while the trainer consumes the previous step's rollouts through a replay buffer. The samples are now off-policy, generated by the old policy, which the importance ratio in the objective already handles provided staleness is bounded to one or two steps and the ratio is clipped. Kimi K1.5's partial rollouts push further: a response that did not finish within the step's budget is paused and resumed under the next weights. The payoff is near-full utilization of both pools; the price is reasoning about staleness and engineering a correct, resumable buffer. Watch the utilization number on the animation change as you switch topologies and you will understand why async won for long-horizon R L.

Weight sync has mechanics worth knowing cold. The trainer holds parameters sharded across data-parallel ranks under F S D P 2 or ZeRO stage three; the engine wants them sharded by tensor parallelism, and by expert parallelism for mixture-of-experts models, across its own ranks. Sync means all-gather each parameter on the trainer side, re-split into the engine's layout, and broadcast over nickel to every engine replica. For a seventy-billion-parameter model in B F sixteen that is a hundred forty gigabytes moving every step, which at NVLink and InfiniBand speeds is seconds rather than minutes, but it adds up over thousands of steps. Colocated systems skip the network by handing CUDA I P C handles between processes on the same GPU. Either way, the engine must flush its prefix cache after sync, because cached K V was computed by stale weights, and forgetting that is a classic silent bug.

Because rollout is the bottleneck, the knobs that matter are engine-side. Prefix caching across the group so prefill is paid once per prompt, not G times. Length budgets with soft overlong shaping rather than hard truncation, which teaches nothing about the cut-off answers. Over-generation and early stop: sample one and a half times the prompts you need and end the step when enough groups are complete, because the long tail belongs to a few prompts. Partial rollouts. Speculative decoding with the model's own multi-token-prediction head where it has one. F P eight K V cache for memory. And expert parallelism with DeepEP-style dispatch so a thirty-seven-billion-active mixture-of-experts model decodes at a reasonable batch size.

The reward function is the specification, and the policy will satisfy exactly what you wrote, not what you meant. Rule-based verifiers, exact or normalized answer match, unit tests in a sandbox, compilers, schema validators, are cheap and deterministic and the backbone of verifiable-reward R L, but they are brittle: a checker that accepts a boxed forty-two but not a bare forty-two trains the model to always box; a test suite with one weak test trains the model to game that test. Model-based rewards are necessary for open-ended quality, and the policy will find their blind spots, verbosity, sycophancy, confident formatting. Shaping rewards for format and length are useful early and dangerous if they dominate. The reward-hacking animation is the single most important plot to watch in any run: the checker gives credit if the correct number appears anywhere, measured reward and true accuracy rise together for a while, then the policy discovers it can list several candidate numbers and measured reward keeps climbing while true accuracy collapses. Press fix the checker, which switches to extracting only the final answer, and the curves re-couple. The page's two reward functions show the shape: the math reward extracts the last boxed expression or the last number, normalizes, and compares numerically so that one half and zero point five agree; the code reward extracts the Python block, appends the hidden tests, runs it in an isolated process with a timeout, and returns one on success and zero on failure or timeout, because an infinite loop is a failure, not a hang of your trainer. Two operational notes: reward computation is a service with its own CPU pool, timeouts, and isolation, because a policy will eventually write code that deletes files; and log the reward distribution, not just the mean, because a bimodal group carries no gradient and a creeping reward with flat held-out accuracy is hacking.

Agentic R L changes the machine in three ways. Rollouts become trajectories through an environment: the engine generates a turn, the harness executes the tool call in a sandbox, a container with a repository, a browser, an API mock, the observation is appended, and the engine generates the next turn, so environment latency is now inside the rollout critical path. Environments become a separately scalable service, the pattern standardized by OpenEnv, a Gym-style reset, step, and state API over containerized environments from the PyTorch and Hugging Face communities, and by NeMo Gym, where environments and reward resource servers are called by the trainer over H T T P. The APIs are still moving; the shape, independent environment pools, one session per episode, a trainer that never looks inside the container, is settled, and libraries like verifiers, prime-R L, and SkyRL build the same shape. And the loss is masked to model tokens only. A trajectory contains the policy's tokens, its thoughts, tool calls, and answers, and the environment's tokens, tool outputs and test logs. You train only on the former. Getting that mask wrong is the most common agentic R L bug, and it shows up as a model that hallucinates tool outputs. The episode animation makes this concrete: task prompt, the agent edits a file, runs tests, reads a failure, edits again, tests pass, terminal reward of one, with the token strip growing underneath and only the green policy tokens in the loss. Every policy token receives the same terminal advantage, which works surprisingly well for short episodes and is exactly what breaks down for fifty-turn ones, hence the research on per-step rewards and turn-level advantages. The page's environment class mirrors the OpenEnv shape with reset returning the task and tool schemas and step applying a patch, running tests, or submitting for the hidden-test reward, and its loss-mask builder walks the trajectory segments and marks only policy-sourced tokens with a one.

R L data is prompts, not answers, and which prompts decides whether a step teaches anything. A prompt the policy always solves yields all ones and zero advantage; one it never solves yields all zeros and zero advantage. So pre-filter by solve rate with the S F T checkpoint, keeping prompts in roughly the ten-to-ninety-percent band and re-estimating as the policy improves; use dapo's dynamic sampling at rollout time; schedule difficulty upward; mix domains with per-domain reward normalization; and mix in S F T data or an S F T loss term so chat quality and safety do not decay, selecting checkpoints on a broad suite rather than the target domain.

Scaling to frontier models adds mixture-of-experts problems: as weights update, the router's top-k choices flip, the same token is processed by different experts under the new and old policies, and per-token ratios swing for reasons unrelated to behavior. The fixes are sequence-level ratios, G S P O, or pinning rollout-time routing during the training forward pass, routing replay. Long rollouts are K V arithmetic on the engine, a thirty-two-thousand-token response at a hundred sixty kilobytes per token is five gigabytes per sequence, and activation memory on the trainer, handled with sequence parallelism, checkpointing, and token-count micro-batching.

The page's cost-model calculator is the worked example I want you to remember. With the defaults, a DeepSeek-V3-class mixture-of-experts policy with thirty-seven billion active parameters, five hundred twelve prompts per step, sixteen samples per prompt, and eight thousand mean response tokens, one step generates about sixty-seven million tokens. Train flops are six times active parameters times tokens plus one extra forward for old-policy log-probs; rollout time is tokens divided by the engine's measured decode throughput. Even with twice the hardware on the rollout side, rollout takes several times longer than the gradient step before the long tail makes it worse. That ratio is why rollout pools are larger than trainer pools, why asynchrony matters, and why a two-times engine speedup from speculative decoding, F P eight K V, or better expert parallelism is worth more than any trainer optimization. It is also why rollout is the first thing to move to cheaper hardware: a Trainium rollout pool feeding a GPU trainer is a legitimate topology if the engine stack delivers parity, and the parity question matters more here than in serving, because numerics differences between engine and trainer show up as a mysterious importance-ratio distribution. Measure the K L between engine and trainer log-probs on identical samples before trusting a mixed-hardware loop.

Then observability. R L jobs run for days across hundreds of GPUs and dozens of sandboxes, and something fails every few hours, so you need checkpoints of policy, optimizer, and buffer state, deterministic resume, restartable engine replicas, and disposable sandbox pools. Watch six curves, each diagnosing something specific. Train reward versus held-out accuracy should rise together; diverging means hacking or a verifier bug. Response length should grow then plateau; runaway growth means length exploitation, collapse means the format reward is dominating. Entropy should decrease slowly; collapse to near zero means exploration is dead. K L to reference should grow small and steady; spikes follow bad weight syncs or routing shifts. The clip fraction should be a few percent of tokens; large means the policy is moving too fast or rollouts are stale beyond the bound. And the fraction of zero-advantage groups should be low; high means refresh the solve-rate filter.

Evaluating an R L'd model has two pitfalls. Verifiable-reward R L reliably raises pass at one, but several studies found the base model's pass at k for large k can match or exceed the R L'd model's, suggesting much of the gain is sharpening onto solutions the base could already sample rather than discovering new ones. Whether R L expands capability or concentrates it is an open debate; for a product you usually want sharpening, but report both numbers. And beware contamination through the verifier: if your R L prompts overlap the eval set, the eval measures memorization of the reward, so keep held-out verifiers and prompt distributions and check for verbosity, over-refusal, and instruction-following drift on a broad suite.

The framework landscape, honestly. Verl, from ByteDance, is the HybridFlow single-controller design on Ray with V L L M or S G lang rollouts and F S D P or Megatron trainers, supporting P P O, G R P O, dapo, and agentic loops; it is the most-used open stack, dense and mixture-of-experts, with the largest recipe community, and its trade-offs are many moving parts and an async path younger than its sync path. Miles, from the slime lineage, is S G lang-native with a Megatron trainer, disaggregated and asynchronous by design, built for enterprise-scale mixture-of-experts R L where rollout throughput dominates. NeMo R L with NeMo Gym has trainers call environments and rewards exposed as servers, best for heterogeneous agentic environments inside the NVIDIA ecosystem. OpenEnv is a specification and hub for environments, not a trainer. T R L's G R P O trainer with an optional V L L M rollout server is the fastest path to a first run on one node. OpenRLHF is Ray plus V L L M plus DeepSpeed with a smaller codebase. SkyRL, prime-R L, and verifiers are async-first, environment-centric research designs whose APIs change monthly. The page includes an illustrative verl-style configuration, with the field names flagged as version dependent: a G R P O advantage estimator with the K L coefficient at zero, five hundred twelve prompts per step, an eight-thousand-token response budget, an S G lang rollout with sixteen samples per prompt at tensor-parallel four, an F S D P actor with clip bounds of point two and point two eight and token-mean loss aggregation, a custom reward manager pointing at the verifier functions, and sixteen nodes of eight GPUs with checkpoints every twenty steps.

The questions a lead actually gets. Colocated or disaggregated? Colocated for dense models up to tens of billions and one job to debug; disaggregated and asynchronous when rollout time and mixture-of-experts scale make utilization the whole game. Why did our reward go up while the eval went down? Because the checker was gameable; show the divergence plot and fix the verifier. Why did the mixture-of-experts run explode? Routing flips under per-token ratios; move to sequence-level ratios or replay the routing. Why does the model hallucinate tool outputs? The loss mask included environment tokens. And the misconception to kill: that R L is the expensive part because of the gradient step. It is expensive because of the generation.
