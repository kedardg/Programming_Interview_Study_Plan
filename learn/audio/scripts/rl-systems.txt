Here is the thing nobody tells you before your first reinforcement-learning job on a language model: R L post-training is mostly inference. Before a single gradient is computed, an inference engine generates hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens of fresh samples, verifiers score them, and the new weights have to flow back into that engine so the next round is on-policy. This chapter is about that machine, the trainer-rollout loop at frontier scale, and it is where a model-customization lead actually spends their days.

Why R L at all? Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate demonstrations, but it cannot teach the model to search over its own outputs, to try a derivation, notice it fails a check, and prefer the attempt that passes. R L samples from the model, scores the samples, and pushes probability toward what scored well. For a decade the scoring came from humans through a reward model optimized with P P O. The twenty twenty-five shift, in DeepSeek-R1, in Tulu 3's recipe for R L with verifiable rewards, and in Kimi K1.5, was to score with verifiers: an exact-answer checker, unit tests, a compiler, a schema validator. Verifiable rewards are cheap, far harder to hack than a reward model, and scale to millions of problems. The visible side effect is the reasoning-length effect: when the reward only cares about the final answer, the policy learns to think longer, because longer chains pass the checker more often.

The objectives are one family: a policy gradient weighted by an advantage, held near the old policy by a ratio clip or a K L penalty, differing in where the baseline comes from. P P O uses a learned value model, a critic as large as the policy, doubling memory. G R P O, from DeepSeekMath and the R1 report, uses the group mean and standard deviation of G sampled answers to the same prompt, with no critic, which made verifiable-reward R L cheap enough to run at scale. Dapo added the fixes that make it train long reasoning stably: an asymmetric clip with a higher upper bound, dynamic sampling that drops groups that are all correct or all wrong, token-level loss aggregation, and soft shaping for overlong responses. Doctor G R P O removed the length and standard-deviation normalization that bias toward long wrong answers. G S P O, from the Qwen team, computes the importance ratio at the sequence level, which keeps mixture-of-experts models stable.

Let me say the G R P O objective in words. For one prompt, sample G responses and score each with the verifier, one or zero. A response's advantage is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation, and that single advantage is shared by every one of its tokens. For each token, the importance ratio is its probability under the current policy divided by its probability under the policy that generated it. Multiply ratio by advantage, clip the ratio to within epsilon of one, and take the minimum of clipped and unclipped so no token moves more than epsilon per update. Average, and optionally subtract a K L penalty to a frozen reference, though many verifiable-reward recipes drop it. Every token of a response that beat its siblings is pushed up, every token of a loser is pushed down, by the same amount. The page's code is twenty lines that do exactly this, with dapo's asymmetric clip bounds and a switch between token-level and per-response averaging. The group animation shows eight responses scored, the mean as baseline, and one response's token strip with green tokens pushed up, red pushed down, and hatched ones already clipped to zero gradient. Push the solve-rate slider to ninety-five percent and the group becomes all correct: every advantage is zero and the prompt teaches nothing. That is exactly what dapo's dynamic sampling discards.

Now the machine. A production R L system has five stages per step. Rollout: V L L M or S G lang, holding the current policy, generates G responses per prompt with continuous batching, paged K V, and, crucially, prefix caching so the siblings share the prompt's K V. Reward: verifiers, sandboxes, environments, or reward models score every response, usually on CPUs, often the second bottleneck. Advantage: group statistics, plus optionally a reference forward pass for the K L term and an old-policy pass to recompute log-probs under the trainer's numerics. Train: an F S D P 2 or Megatron trainer runs forward and backward over the sampled tokens and steps. Weight sync: the new weights are resharded from the trainer's data-parallel layout into the engine's tensor-parallel and expert-parallel layout and pushed back.

The animation to study is the timeline of where the time goes. Rollout dominates, with a long tail: the step cannot finish until the longest response finishes, so most engine GPUs idle waiting for stragglers. Switch between three topologies. Colocated and synchronous, verl's HybridFlow design: a single Python controller on Ray drives both roles on the same GPUs, time-slicing between engine and trainer, weights moving by CUDA I P C with no second cluster to schedule. It is the right default for dense models up to tens of billions and for teams that want one job to debug; the cost is that the trainer idles during rollout and the engine's K V cache is torn down during training. Disaggregated and synchronous runs rollout and training on separate pools, each provisioned for its own regime, at the cost of a network hop for weights, and the trainer still idles during rollout. Disaggregated and asynchronous, the slime and Miles lineage and the prime-R L style, lets the engine keep generating with weights one or two steps old while the trainer consumes the previous step's rollouts through a replay buffer. The samples are now off-policy, which the importance ratio already handles provided staleness is bounded and the ratio is clipped. Kimi K1.5's partial rollouts go further: a response that did not finish within the step is paused and resumed under the next weights. The payoff is near-full utilization of both pools; the price is reasoning about staleness and building a correct, resumable buffer.

Weight sync has mechanics worth knowing cold. The trainer holds parameters sharded across data-parallel ranks; the engine wants them sharded by tensor and expert parallelism across its own ranks. So: all-gather each parameter on the trainer, re-split into the engine's layout, and broadcast over nickel to every replica. For a seventy-billion-parameter model in B F sixteen that is a hundred forty gigabytes every step, seconds rather than minutes over NVLink and InfiniBand, but it adds up over thousands of steps. And the engine must flush its prefix cache after sync, because cached K V was computed by stale weights; forgetting that is a classic silent bug. Because rollout is the bottleneck, the knobs are engine-side: prefix caching across the group, length budgets with soft overlong shaping, over-generation with early stop because the tail belongs to a few prompts, partial rollouts, speculative decoding, and F P eight K V cache.

The reward function is the specification, and the policy will satisfy exactly what you wrote, not what you meant. Rule-based verifiers are cheap and deterministic but brittle: a checker that accepts a boxed forty-two but not a bare forty-two trains the model to always box; a suite with one weak test trains the model to game that test. Model-based rewards are necessary for open-ended quality, and the policy will find their blind spots. The reward-hacking animation is the single most important plot in any run: the checker credits the correct number appearing anywhere, measured reward and true accuracy rise together, then the policy discovers it can list several candidate numbers, and measured reward keeps climbing while true accuracy collapses. Press fix the checker, which extracts only the final answer, and the curves re-couple. The page's two reward functions show the shape: the math reward extracts only the final answer and compares numerically; the code reward runs hidden tests in an isolated process with a timeout, and a timeout is a zero, not a hang of your trainer. Reward computation is a service with its own CPU pool and isolation, and you log the reward distribution, not just the mean.

Agentic R L changes the machine in three ways. Rollouts become trajectories through an environment: the engine generates a turn, the harness executes the tool call in a sandbox, the observation is appended, and the engine generates the next turn, so environment latency is inside the rollout critical path. Environments become a separately scalable service, the pattern standardized by OpenEnv, a Gym-style reset, step, and state API over containerized environments from the PyTorch and Hugging Face communities, and by NeMo Gym, where environments and reward servers are called by the trainer over H T T P; the APIs are still moving, the shape is settled, and verifiers, prime-R L, and SkyRL build the same shape. And the loss is masked to model tokens only: a trajectory contains the policy's thoughts, tool calls, and answers, and the environment's tool outputs and test logs, and you train only on the former. Getting that mask wrong is the most common agentic R L bug, and it shows up as a model that hallucinates tool outputs. The episode animation makes it concrete: edit a file, run tests, read a failure, edit again, tests pass, terminal reward of one, with the token strip growing underneath and only the green policy tokens in the loss. Every policy token receives the same terminal advantage, which works for short episodes and breaks down for fifty-turn ones.

R L data is prompts, not answers: a prompt always solved yields zero advantage, and so does one never solved, so pre-filter by solve rate into roughly the ten-to-ninety-percent band, use dynamic sampling, schedule difficulty upward, and mix in S F T data so chat quality does not decay.

At frontier scale, mixture-of-experts adds a failure mode: as weights update, the router's top-k choices flip, the same token is processed by different experts under the new and old policies, and per-token ratios swing for reasons unrelated to behavior. The fixes are sequence-level ratios, G S P O, or replaying rollout-time routing during training. The page's cost-model calculator is the worked example to remember: a DeepSeek-V3-class policy with thirty-seven billion active parameters, five hundred twelve prompts per step, sixteen samples per prompt, and eight thousand mean response tokens generates about sixty-seven million tokens per step. Train flops are six times active parameters times tokens plus one extra forward for old-policy log-probs; rollout time is tokens divided by measured decode throughput. Even with twice the hardware on the rollout side, rollout takes several times longer than the gradient step before the tail makes it worse. That ratio is why rollout pools are larger than trainer pools, why asynchrony matters, and why a two-times engine speedup is worth more than any trainer optimization. It is also why rollout is the first thing to move to cheaper hardware: a Trainium rollout pool feeding a GPU trainer is legitimate if the engine stack delivers parity, and here parity means measuring the K L between engine and trainer log-probs on identical samples before trusting the loop, because numerics drift shows up as a mysterious ratio distribution.

Then observability. Watch train reward against held-out accuracy, which must rise together; response length, which should plateau rather than run away; entropy, which should fall slowly rather than collapse; K L to reference, where spikes follow bad syncs or routing shifts; and the clip fraction, where a large value means the policy moves too fast or rollouts are stale.

Evaluating an R L'd model has two pitfalls. Verifiable-reward R L reliably raises pass at one, but several studies found the base model's pass at k for large k can match the R L'd model's, suggesting much of the gain is sharpening onto solutions the base could already sample. For a product you usually want sharpening, but report both numbers. And keep R L prompts disjoint from the eval set, or the eval measures memorization of the reward.

The framework landscape, honestly. Verl, from ByteDance, is HybridFlow on Ray with V L L M or S G lang rollouts and F S D P or Megatron trainers; the most-used open stack, dense and mixture-of-experts, the largest recipe community, with many moving parts and an async path younger than its sync path. Miles, from the slime lineage, is S G lang-native with a Megatron trainer, disaggregated and asynchronous by design, built for enterprise-scale mixture-of-experts R L. NeMo R L with NeMo Gym has trainers call environments and rewards exposed as servers, best inside the NVIDIA ecosystem. OpenEnv is a specification and hub for environments, not a trainer. T R L's G R P O trainer is the fastest path to a first run on one node. OpenRLHF, SkyRL, prime-R L, and verifiers round out the landscape, the last three as async-first research designs whose APIs change monthly. The page's illustrative verl-style configuration shows where each of these choices lives: the advantage estimator, the rollout engine and sample count, the clip bounds, and the reward function.

The questions a lead gets. Colocated or disaggregated? Colocated for dense models up to tens of billions and one job to debug; disaggregated and asynchronous when rollout time and mixture-of-experts scale make utilization the whole game. Why did reward go up while the eval went down? The checker was gameable; show the divergence plot and fix the verifier. Why did the mixture-of-experts run explode? Routing flips under per-token ratios; go sequence-level. Why does the model hallucinate tool outputs? The loss mask included environment tokens. And the misconception to kill: that R L is expensive because of the gradient step. It is expensive because of the generation.

One more piece before we wrap up, because the acronym causes real confusion on real teams: R F T. It names two different things, and the recipe for agents uses both. The first is rejection sampling fine tuning. Sample several candidate trajectories per task, keep only the ones a grader passes, deduplicate, and run ordinary supervised fine tuning on the survivors. There is no policy gradient in it. It is S F T whose data a grader produced. The second is reinforcement fine tuning as a product and a recipe, the thing OpenAI sells through its R F T interface and the thing open stacks like verl, T R L, and NeMo R L let you run yourself. That one is real reinforcement learning on a small set of tasks, dozens to a few thousand, with a programmable grader standing in for a learned reward model, and G R P O or P P O underneath. They are stages, not rivals. Rejection sampling fine tuning is the warm start that gives reinforcement fine tuning a non zero success rate to climb from.

The recipe for a multi turn agent runs in seven stages. Design tasks whose end state is checkable: a sweet bench style issue with hidden tests, a tau bench style customer service task whose success is a database state rather than a transcript, a data analysis task with a numeric checker. Give each task tool schemas, a sandbox, and a turn and token budget, because budgets are part of the task. Then build the graders. Prefer outcome graders, tests pass or the final state matches. Add rubric or judge graders only where outcomes are not checkable, and calibrate them first. Process rewards, per turn scores or a process reward model, give you dense signal and are the easiest thing in the whole pipeline to hack, because looking productive is cheap. So the working reward is outcome first, plus small shaping terms for valid tool calls and efficiency, plus anti hacking rules: hidden tests, write protected test directories, and an automatic zero for any trajectory that edits the tests.

Then collect trajectories with real tool execution, several per task, and store everything the update needs later: tokens, per token log probabilities from the rollout engine, the policy versus environment mask, tool outputs, the grader's verdict. Filter by the grader, deduplicate, and do the rejection sampling fine tuning warm start with environment tokens masked out of the loss. That warm start matters more for agents than for math, because with sparse terminal rewards a weak policy produces groups where every trajectory scores zero, and the group relative advantage of an all zero group is exactly zero. No contrast, no gradient. The warm start buys the successes you need to contrast against.

Now the reinforcement fine tuning stage, and its one real design decision: credit assignment. Trajectory level credit broadcasts a single terminal advantage to every policy token in the episode. It is unbiased and simple and it is the default, and it gets noisy as episodes get long, because the wasted turn and the five good turns receive identical credit. Turn level credit gives each turn its own advantage from per turn process rewards, which is informative and exactly as trustworthy as the process reward. Step level group baselines are the clever middle: compare what the other sampled trajectories did from the same intermediate state, so actions taken from that state form their own little group, and you recover a per step signal from outcome rewards alone without any process reward model. That is the idea behind the GiGPO family of methods; the names and estimators are still moving, the idea is stable. Open the credit assignment visualizer on the page. It shows a six turn episode where turn three is a destructive command the agent then has to undo, and lets you switch between the three schemes and watch how much of the signal lands on that bad turn, and whether it is even negative. Under terminal broadcast, a lucky episode rewards the bad turn. Under process rewards it is punished at once. Under step level baselines it is punished using outcomes only, and the signal sharpens as you raise the number of sampled trajectories.

Finish the recipe with evaluation and iteration. Hold out tasks in the same environment, report pass at one and pass at k, and always report success against cost, tokens, tool calls, and turns, because an agent that doubles its turns for two more points may be a worse product. Run the broad regression suite on every checkpoint, and audit the grader itself by reading passing trajectories by hand. Then grow the task set from production failures, every escalation is a candidate task with a known good end state, and run the loop again.
