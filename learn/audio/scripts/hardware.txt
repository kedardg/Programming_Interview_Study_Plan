Every accelerator you will ever meet is the same machine wearing a different coat. Underneath, there is a big matrix multiply engine, a chunk of fast on-chip memory that software has to manage by hand, and a stream of tiles moving between the two. If you learned to write a tiled matmul on a GPU, you already know most of what Trainium, the T P U, and AMD's chips want from you. This chapter is about learning the pattern once so that each new chip becomes a vocabulary lesson instead of a new field. It is the companion to Step sixteen of the study plan.

Start with why alternatives exist at all, because the reasons are entirely economic. First, cost per token. Inference at scale is a utility bill, and an accelerator that delivers the same tokens for thirty percent less compounds into millions of dollars a year. Third, vertical integration. Amazon, Google, and Meta run the same handful of models at enormous scale, so a chip co-designed with the workload and billed at cost can beat a general-purpose part sold at list price.

One honest caveat before any numbers, straight from the study plan. Read each architecture with its instruction set or tuning guide, because vendor peak numbers are not performance measurements. Every peak is a ceiling, not a promise, and the Deepgram case study later in this chapter shows what reaching even part of that ceiling actually takes.

Now the main event: AWS Trainium and the NeuronCore. A Trainium chip contains several NeuronCores, and a NeuronCore is organized very differently from an NVIDIA streaming multiprocessor. Where an S M is a sea of identical warps scheduled onto shared pipelines, a NeuronCore is a small set of heterogeneous engines connected by explicit on-chip memories: a tensor engine, a one hundred twenty eight by one hundred twenty eight systolic array that does nearly all the floating point work; a vector engine for elementwise and reduction math; a scalar engine for per-element functions like exponent; and the G P SIMD engine, eight small D S P cores for the odd control-heavy operations such as custom top-k.

The memory hierarchy is the real mental shift coming from CUDA. There is no hardware cache doing you favors. Data lives in H B M, the device memory. From there it must be moved explicitly into S buf, a twenty four megabyte software-managed staging buffer organized as one hundred twenty eight partitions. The tensor engine reads its operands from S buf and writes matmul results into P sum, a small accumulator memory. Your kernel, or the compiler acting on your behalf, explicitly D M A's tiles from H B M into S buf, feeds the engines from S buf, accumulates partial results in P sum, and copies the finished tile back out. In CUDA terms, S buf is shared memory made much larger and fully software managed, P sum is the accumulator you allocate by name, and every data movement is an explicit D M A rather than a load through a cache.

Picture the tiled matmul animation on the page. To compute one output tile, the A tile and the B tile are D M A'd from H B M into S buf partitions. The tensor engine streams them through its systolic array and accumulates partial products into P sum across the K loop, and the important detail is that P sum fills in place, which is exactly what an accumulator memory is for. The pipelining insight is the one you already earned in the kernels chapter: while the tensor engine chews on tile k, the D M A engines are already fetching tile k plus one. That is double buffering with different names.

The programming model for all of this is N K I, the Neuron Kernel Interface. Think of it as the Triton of this hardware: a Python-embedded tile language. You write functions over tiles, arrays whose first dimension, the partition dimension, is at most one hundred twenty eight to match S buf, and the compiler maps your tile operations onto engines and schedules the D M A's. The matmul kernel on the page reads almost line for line like a Triton matmul: load tiles into S buf, accumulate in a P sum buffer inside an affine range loop the compiler may pipeline, store the result. The concepts transfer directly; the constraint that actually changes your tiling is the one hundred twenty eight partition limit and the explicitly sized S buf and P sum. And here is the part that matters for your study plan: N K I ships a simulate kernel function that runs kernels on an ordinary C P U with real numerics. That is why the Step eighteen exercise, porting your online softmax or your rotary embedding kernel to N K I and comparing it against the reference, needs no AWS account at all.

The N K I Library is the production kernel catalog, and its structure quietly repeats the inference chapter. Attention ships in two flavors: C T E, context encoding, which is prefill with its big parallel matmuls, and T K G, token generation, which is decode, bandwidth bound and batch skinny. The two phases want different tilings, so the prefill and decode asymmetry is baked right into the kernel names. Around attention you find M L P and mixture of experts kernels, fused projections, rotary embeddings, quantization kernels, and NeuroTile, a tile iterator library that abstracts the H B M to S buf to P sum choreography.

Now the case study that turns all of this into an engineering checklist. The Deepgram on Trainium case study, published on the AWS machine learning blog in twenty twenty six, describes moving the Nova three speech to text model's batch inference onto Trainium two. The headline result was eleven to seventy percent more audio processed per dollar than their G P U baseline, depending on the workload. What made that possible was a set of decisions you can reuse for any accelerator. First, static shapes. The encoder and the decoder were compiled separately, each for fixed input shapes, because ahead-of-time compilers for systolic hardware want static graphs; dynamic shapes either trigger recompilation or fall back to the host. Second, zero C P U fallbacks. Any operation the compiler cannot map runs on the host, and a single fallback inside a hot loop can erase the hardware's entire advantage, so reaching no fallbacks was a milestone, not a given. Third, replicas before parallelism. They ran eight model replicas per instance, because for a model that fits on one core, data-parallel copies beat model parallelism: no communication, perfect scaling, exactly the parallelism chapter's first rule. Fourth, custom kernels only where they pay. N K I kernels were written for the few layers called repeatedly that drive end to end latency, not for the whole model. Profile first, customize the top of the profile, and let the compiler handle the rest. The resulting port checklist has five questions. Can every op compile? Can shapes be made static? Does the model fit per core? What is hot? And did you measure cost per unit of work against the G P U baseline rather than peak flops?

Turn now to Google's T P U, the systolic original. The bet, made in twenty fifteen and unchanged in spirit through the seventh generation, is that if matmul is almost all the work, the chip should be mostly one giant matmul unit. The matrix unit is a systolic array: a grid of multiply accumulate cells where weights are loaded once and stay put, activations march in from the left one diagonal per clock, and partial sums flow downward with each cell adding its weight times activation as data passes through. There is no instruction fetch and no register file between operations. Data movement is the computation, which is why systolic arrays are so efficient for dense matmul, and why Trainium's tensor engine is also one. Jouppi and colleagues made that argument in the original T P U datacenter analysis, and the T P U version four paper carries it to pod scale. In the four by four animation on the page, count the clocks: for an n by n array the first result takes about two n cycles to fill the pipeline, and after that one complete output column exits the bottom every single clock with zero memory traffic inside the array. Around the matrix unit sit a vector unit, V mem as the software-managed buffer playing the S buf role, and Pallas as the tile language, while pods connect thousands of chips over I C I torus links, which is why T P U sharding layouts differ from NVLink clusters.

AMD is the closest relative to NVIDIA in this family. The Instinct M I three fifty uses compute units with matrix cores, a local data share where you would expect shared memory, and H B M stacks totaling two hundred eighty eight gigabytes, which is the headline reason it wins deals for serving huge models on a single node. HIP is deliberately CUDA shaped, so kernels port with a handful of changes and then need retuning, and Composable Kernel, A I T E R, HipKittens, and Triton cover the kernel library side.

Put the four vendors in one table and the convergent evolution is unmistakable. Everyone landed on a big matmul engine, a software-managed on-chip tile buffer, a Python tile D S L to program it, and a proprietary scale-up fabric. Learn that pattern once, tiles in fast memory feeding a matmul unit, overlapped with explicit data movement and measured against a roofline, and each new chip is a vocabulary lesson.

A few misconceptions worth clearing up, because they come up in interviews. People assume you need the hardware to learn the programming model; you do not, since simulate kernel runs N K I on your laptop and Pallas runs in interpreter mode on a C P U or compiles on your own G P U.
