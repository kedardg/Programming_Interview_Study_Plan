Every accelerator you will ever meet is the same machine wearing a different coat. Underneath, there is a big matrix multiply engine, a chunk of fast on-chip memory that software has to manage by hand, and a stream of tiles moving between the two. If you learned to write a tiled matmul on a GPU, you already know most of what Trainium, the T P U, and AMD's chips want from you. This chapter is about learning the pattern once so that each new chip becomes a vocabulary lesson instead of a new field. It is the companion to Step sixteen of the study plan.

Start with why alternatives exist at all, because the reasons are entirely economic. First, cost per token. Inference at scale is a utility bill, and an accelerator that delivers the same tokens for thirty percent less compounds into millions of dollars a year. Second, supply. Top-end NVIDIA parts have been allocation constrained for years, so a second source is simply risk management. Third, vertical integration. Amazon, Google, and Meta run the same handful of models at enormous scale, so a chip co-designed with the workload and billed at cost can beat a general-purpose part sold at list price. For you as an engineer, the consequence is portability pressure. The serving stack of the future targets more than one backend, and people who can open an unfamiliar architecture document and find the roofline quickly are rare.

One honest caveat before any numbers, straight from the study plan. Read each architecture with its instruction set or tuning guide, because vendor peak numbers are not performance measurements. Every peak is a ceiling, not a promise, and the Deepgram case study later in this chapter shows what reaching even part of that ceiling actually takes.

Now the main event: AWS Trainium and the NeuronCore. A Trainium chip contains several NeuronCores, and a NeuronCore is organized very differently from an NVIDIA streaming multiprocessor. Where an S M is a sea of identical warps scheduled onto shared pipelines, a NeuronCore is a small set of heterogeneous engines, each built for one job, connected by explicit on-chip memories. There is a tensor engine, which is a one hundred twenty eight by one hundred twenty eight systolic array that does the matrix multiplies and therefore almost all of the floating point work. There is a vector engine for elementwise and reduction math over tiles, which handles the pieces of softmax, the normalizations, and the activations. There is a scalar engine for per-element functions like exponent and square root, streamed over data. And there is the G P SIMD engine, eight general-purpose D S P cores for the odd, control-heavy operations that fit nowhere else, such as custom top-k selection or dynamic shapes.

The memory hierarchy is the real mental shift coming from CUDA. There is no hardware cache doing you favors. Data lives in H B M, the device memory. From there it must be moved explicitly into S buf, a twenty four megabyte software-managed staging buffer organized as one hundred twenty eight partitions. The tensor engine reads its operands from S buf and writes matmul results into P sum, a small accumulator memory. Your kernel, or the compiler acting on your behalf, explicitly D M A's tiles from H B M into S buf, feeds the engines from S buf, accumulates partial results in P sum, and copies the finished tile back out. If you want a translation table, S buf plays the role of shared memory, except it is much larger and the partition dimension of at most one hundred twenty eight shapes every kernel you write. P sum plays the role of the tensor core accumulator registers, except it is a named, addressable memory you allocate buffers in. The engines are like the pipelines inside an S M, except they are independently programmable, so overlapping them is something you or the compiler arranges explicitly rather than something a warp scheduler does for you. And every data movement is an explicit D M A, not a load or store through a cache.

Picture the tiled matmul animation on the page. To compute one output tile, the A tile and the B tile are D M A'd from H B M into S buf partitions. The tensor engine streams them through its systolic array and accumulates partial products into P sum across the K loop, and the important detail is that P sum fills in place, which is exactly what an accumulator memory is for. Then the vector and scalar engines apply the epilogue, a bias or an activation, as the result copies back to S buf, and a final D M A returns it to H B M. The pipelining insight is the one you already earned in the kernels chapter: while the tensor engine chews on tile k, the D M A engines are already fetching tile k plus one. That is double buffering with different names.

The programming model for all of this is N K I, the Neuron Kernel Interface. Think of it as the Triton of this hardware: a Python-embedded tile language. You write functions over tiles, arrays whose first dimension, the partition dimension, is at most one hundred twenty eight to match S buf, and the compiler maps your tile operations onto engines and schedules the D M A's. The matmul kernel on the page reads almost line for line like a Triton matmul. A load call moves a tile from H B M into S buf. An affine range loop tells the compiler it may pipeline iterations. A zeros call allocates the accumulator in P sum. A matmul call sends tiles through the tensor engine and accumulates. A store call sends the result home. The concepts transfer directly; the constraint that actually changes your tiling is the one hundred twenty eight partition limit and the explicitly sized S buf and P sum. And here is the part that matters for your study plan: N K I ships a simulate kernel function that runs kernels on an ordinary C P U with real numerics. That is why the Step eighteen exercise, porting your online softmax or your rotary embedding kernel to N K I and comparing it against the reference, needs no AWS account at all.

The N K I Library is the production kernel catalog, and its structure quietly repeats the inference chapter. Attention ships in two flavors: C T E, context encoding, which is prefill with its big parallel matmuls, and T K G, token generation, which is decode, bandwidth bound and batch skinny. The two phases want different tilings, so the prefill and decode asymmetry is baked right into the kernel names. Around attention you find M L P and mixture of experts kernels, Q K V and output projections with fused normalization, rotary embeddings, F P eight and M X quantization kernels, and NeuroTile, a tile iterator library that abstracts the whole H B M to S buf to P sum choreography for kernel authors. Logical NeuronCore sharding, L N C, lets one kernel span multiple physical cores; think of it as the tile-level analog of tensor parallelism.

Now the case study that turns all of this into an engineering checklist. The Deepgram on Trainium case study, published on the AWS machine learning blog in twenty twenty six, describes moving the Nova three speech to text model's batch inference onto Trainium two. The headline result was eleven to seventy percent more audio processed per dollar than their G P U baseline, depending on the workload. What made that possible was a set of decisions you can reuse for any accelerator. First, static shapes. The encoder and the decoder were compiled separately, each for fixed input shapes, because ahead-of-time compilers for systolic hardware want static graphs; dynamic shapes either trigger recompilation or fall back to the host. Notice that splitting encoder from decoder is the C T E versus T K G split again. Second, zero C P U fallbacks. Any operation the compiler cannot map runs on the host, and a single fallback inside a hot loop can erase the hardware's entire advantage, so reaching no fallbacks was a milestone, not a given. Third, replicas before parallelism. They ran eight model replicas per instance, because for a model that fits on one core, data-parallel copies beat model parallelism: no communication, perfect scaling, exactly the parallelism chapter's first rule. Fourth, custom kernels only where they pay. N K I kernels were written for the few layers called repeatedly that drive end to end latency, not for the whole model. Profile first, customize the top of the profile, and let the compiler handle the rest. The resulting port checklist has five questions. Can every op compile? Can shapes be made static? Does the model fit per core? What is hot? And did you measure cost per unit of work against the G P U baseline rather than peak flops?

Turn now to Google's T P U, the systolic original. The bet, made in twenty fifteen and unchanged in spirit through the seventh generation, is that if matmul is almost all the work, the chip should be mostly one giant matmul unit. The matrix unit is a systolic array: a grid of multiply accumulate cells where weights are loaded once and stay put, activations march in from the left one diagonal per clock, and partial sums flow downward with each cell adding its weight times activation as data passes through. There is no instruction fetch and no register file between operations. Data movement is the computation, which is why systolic arrays are so area and power efficient for dense matmul, and why Trainium's tensor engine is also one. In the four by four animation on the page, count the clocks: for an n by n array the first result takes about two n cycles to fill the pipeline, and after that one complete output column exits the bottom every single clock with zero memory traffic inside the array. The original T P U datacenter analysis by Jouppi and colleagues made the systolic argument, and the T P U version four paper describes the chip, the optical interconnect, and the pod-scale system. Around the matrix unit sit a vector unit for everything that is not matmul, V mem, a software-managed on-chip buffer that plays exactly the S buf role, and Pallas as the kernel language, which shares its tile-based design with Triton and N K I. At pod scale, chips connect over I C I links into two and three dimensional torus topologies, with a version four pod wiring four thousand ninety six chips, and the torus shape is why T P U collective algorithms and sharding layouts differ from NVLink tree G P U clusters.

AMD is the closest relative to NVIDIA in this family. The Instinct M I three fifty uses compute units with matrix cores, a local data share where you would expect shared memory, and H B M stacks totaling two hundred eighty eight gigabytes, which is the headline reason it wins deals for serving huge models on a single node. HIP is deliberately CUDA shaped, with wavefronts of sixty four lanes instead of thirty two, so kernels port with a handful of textual changes and then need retuning. Composable Kernel is AMD's CUTLASS, A I T E R packages ready inference operators the way FlashInfer does, HipKittens brings the tile D S L idea over, and Triton targets AMD too, which is often the lowest effort path to a fast custom kernel there.

Put the four vendors in one table and the convergent evolution is unmistakable. Everyone landed on a big matmul engine, a software-managed on-chip tile buffer, a Python tile D S L to program it, and a proprietary scale-up fabric. Learn that pattern once, tiles in fast memory feeding a matmul unit, overlapped with explicit data movement and measured against a roofline, and each new chip is a vocabulary lesson.

A few misconceptions worth clearing up, because they come up in interviews. People assume you need the hardware to learn the programming model; you do not, since simulate kernel runs N K I on your laptop and Pallas runs in interpreter mode on a C P U or compiles on your own G P U. People assume peak tera-ops tell you something about your model; they tell you the ceiling, and your real performance depends on static shapes, fallbacks, and the tiling of a few hot layers. And people assume porting means rewriting a model; Deepgram's port was mostly compilation discipline plus a few kernels where the profile demanded them.
