Every other chapter in this notebook explains why a system works. This one is the tour of the tools you actually operate when you customize a model and take it to production. One warning first: these A P Is move fast. What I describe are shapes and abstractions, and the docs are always the source of truth for exact flags.

Start with the map, because every framework lives at exactly one station of one pipeline. Data flows into an S F T trainer. The supervised checkpoint flows into an R L trainer, which cannot work alone: it needs rollout engines to generate samples and environments or reward functions to score them. Checkpoints flow through evals that gate releases, the winner flows into serving, and monitoring feeds production traffic back into data. Four things travel along the edges: weights, rollouts, rewards, and metrics. The hard problems, resharding weights between trainer and engine, scaling environments, keeping evals honest, are edge problems, not box problems.

The training station first. PyTorch F S D P two is the native implementation of the ZeRO three idea from the parallelism chapter: every parameter, gradient, and optimizer state is sharded across the data parallel group, and each module's parameters are all-gathered just before its forward or backward pass, used, then freed. The change from the original F S D P is representation. F S D P two shards per parameter as a D Tensor, a tensor that carries its own placement over a device mesh, instead of flattening a module into one buffer. That buys composability, because the same D Tensor machinery implements tensor parallelism, so F S D P two plus tensor parallel is simply a two dimensional mesh. The knobs are where you apply fully shard, usually per transformer block, the mixed precision policy with B F sixteen compute and F P thirty two master weights, resharding after forward, and activation checkpointing. Reach for it for fine-tuning and post-training of dense models up to roughly a hundred billion parameters on a few nodes, anything lora or Q lora, and any time you want plain PyTorch modules with no model rewrite. It is the trainer underneath Axolotl, T R L, verl, and NeMo R L.

Megatron-Core is NVIDIA's pretraining-scale toolkit: parallel embeddings and linears, fused attention through Transformer Engine, mixture of experts layers, and pipeline schedules, with Megatron L M as the harness around it. Where F S D P two gives you one parallelism in native PyTorch, Megatron gives you all of them as first-class flags with hand-optimized kernels: tensor parallel, sequence parallel, pipeline parallel with interleaved schedules, context parallel, expert parallel, and a distributed optimizer. Two more concepts matter: the model spec, which declares which kernels compose a layer, and the distributed checkpoint format, which stores tensors with their parallel layout so a checkpoint saved at tensor parallel eight loads at tensor parallel four. Megatron wins for pretraining or continued pretraining of large dense and mixture of experts models across many nodes and anything needing pipeline or expert parallelism; the cost is that your model must live in Megatron's module zoo.

Here is the sharding difference said out loud, because interviewers love it. Picture one linear layer on four accelerators. Under F S D P two the weight is sharded by rows; before the layer computes, all four devices all-gather the full matrix, compute, and free it. It moves parameters once per step and tolerates slow interconnects. Under Megatron tensor parallel each device keeps a column slice permanently resident, computes a partial output locally, and the devices all-reduce the activation. It moves activations every layer and needs N V Link. Same layer, opposite trade, and real systems combine them on a two dimensional mesh.

Three layers sit above those trainers. DeepSpeed implements the same ZeRO stages plus C P U offload through a J S O N config; choose it when you need offload or a recipe uses it. Axolotl is the Y A M L layer: one config names the base model, datasets and prompt format, the adapter, sequence packing, and the F S D P or DeepSpeed settings, and one train command does the rest, the fastest path from fifty thousand examples to a trained adapter. Hugging Face T R L is the Python counterpart, with S F T, D P O, and G R P O trainers; it is right for experiments up to a node or two.

The R L station is the one workload where three systems cooperate inside a single step: a trainer holding parameters, a rollout engine generating samples, and environments scoring them. Verl, from ByteDance's Seed team and built on the HybridFlow paper, is the most widely used, with three ideas worth internalizing. First, the single-controller programming model: one Python driver on Ray writes the algorithm as ordinary sequential code, generate, compute reference log probabilities, compute rewards, update, while each call fans out to a worker group of accelerators. Second, worker groups per role: the actor with its rollout engine and frozen reference model usually co-located and time-slicing the same devices, a critic for P P O only, an optional reward model, each with a backend of F S D P two or Megatron for training and V L L M or S G lang for generation. Third, the three D hybrid engine, which reshards the actor's weights in place from the trainer's layout into the engine's tensor parallel layout for generation, without touching disk. That reshard and sync is the weight sync bar in the chapter's timeline animation.

Miles descends from slime, the framework the G L M team built around S G lang for rollouts and Megatron-Core for training. Where verl began colocated and synchronous, the slime and Miles lineage starts disaggregated: rollout engines and the trainer are separate resource pools connected by a data buffer, so rollout workers keep generating while the trainer consumes batches with bounded staleness. Rollout and training scale and fail independently, and S G lang serves DeepSeek-class mixture of experts rollouts natively. Choose Miles when rollout throughput dominates and you already run Megatron; choose verl for the broadest algorithm coverage and the most reproductions.

NeMo R L is NVIDIA's post-training library, Ray-orchestrated with D Tensor and Megatron backends and V L L M generation. Its companion NeMo Gym turns environments into servers: a resource server hosts tools, a sandbox, or a reward model behind H T T P, and a recipe binds environments, rewards, data, and trainer, so the same environment serves evaluation and training. OpenEnv, from Meta and the PyTorch ecosystem, standardizes what an environment is: a Gym-style reset, step, and state interface served over H T T P from an isolated container, with typed schemas, tool exposure including M C P, and a hub for sharing. Publish a coding sandbox once, and verl, NeMo R L, T R L, or Miles can all train against it.

The serving station. V L L M is the default engine for serving and for rollouts. Its V one architecture separates a front-end process for tokenization, the OpenAI-compatible A P I, and streaming from an engine core running the scheduler and model executor in a tight loop, and every inference-chapter concept has a flag: continuous batching with chunked prefill, paged attention with prefix caching, the parallelism degrees, lora adapters batched over one shared base, speculative decoding, F P eight weights and K V cache, and grammar-constrained outputs. S G lang shares most of that list and differs in emphasis. Its runtime is built on Radix Attention, where the K V cache is a radix tree over token prefixes, so any request sharing a prefix, a system prompt, multi-turn history, or the eight samples of a G R P O group, reuses it automatically. It is the reference implementation for DeepSeek-class serving, and its router does prefix-aware load balancing. TensorRT L L M is NVIDIA's engine for peak performance with the lowest-level F P eight and N V F P four paths. Choose it when you own the NVIDIA fleet, the model is stable, and the last ten to twenty percent of throughput is worth an engine-build pipeline.

The Neuron S D K is the stack for Trainium and Inferentia, and the mental shift is that Neuron compiles graphs ahead of time. PyTorch code is traced, compiled by neuron x c c for fixed shapes, and executed by the runtime; dynamic shapes become buckets, and anything the compiler cannot lower falls back to C P U and must be eliminated, the Deepgram lesson. The pieces are the compiler, torch neuron x for tracing, and NeuronX Distributed, whose inference package ships ready model implementations with continuous batching and bucketing and is what the V L L M Neuron plugin calls into, plus the Neuron profiler and N K I for custom kernels. Concretely you budget and cache a compile step, bucket shapes, and profile engine utilization rather than streaming multiprocessor occupancy.

Three glue layers tie it together. Ray provides actors, tasks, an object store, and placement groups, exactly the vocabulary an R L framework needs: a worker group is a placement group, and the driver calling generate on the rollout group is a remote method on an actor. For plain S F T you do not need Ray; torchrun under Slurm or Kubernetes is enough. Checkpoints are where frameworks disagree: safetensors in the Hugging Face layout is the universal exchange format, distributed checkpoint is what F S D P two and verl's F S D P backend write and reshards on load, and Megatron's dist checkpoint stores parallel metadata. Train in whatever your trainer wants, but every eval and deployment consumes safetensors, so the merge and convert step is part of the pipeline from day one, and keep the tokenizer and chat template with the weights, because most fine-tune-is-worse-in-production bugs are template mismatches. LeRobot plays all these roles for robots.

Finally the managed paths, because an F D E's job includes knowing when to say the customer should not self-manage. Amazon Bedrock is the fully managed model service: fine-tuning and continued pre-training jobs on supported models, distillation jobs where a teacher generates synthetic responses for a smaller student, and Custom Model Import, which serves your own fine-tuned open weights through the Bedrock A P I on demand or with provisioned throughput. The trade is minimal operations against limited control over serving internals. Amazon SageMaker A I is the managed infrastructure layer: training jobs on managed instances including Trainium, HyperPod resilient clusters with faulty-node replacement and checkpoint-aware resume for multi-week runs, and endpoints hosting your own containers; you own the frameworks and configs, not the cluster plumbing. Self-managed on E C two or Kubernetes remains right for frontier scale, Trainium-first programs, custom R L harnesses, and bespoke serving.

Now the whole stack on one problem: post-train a seventy billion parameter coding model with R L on tests, then serve it with many adapters. Curate repository tasks with executable tests, build the supervised set from successful trajectories, and train a rank sixty four lora on a Llama seventy B base with Axolotl on F S D P two across sixteen H one hundreds. Merge to safetensors and establish the eval baseline. For R L, package the task environment as an OpenEnv container where reset restores a commit, step applies an edit or runs tests, and the reward is whether tests pass. Run verl with G R P O or dapo, actor on F S D P two, rollouts on V L L M at tensor parallel four with prefix caching so every group shares the repository context, eight samples per prompt, multi-turn tool calling, environment tokens masked out of the loss. Expect generation to dominate, and if it becomes the wall and you already run Megatron, evaluate Miles. For serving, export the policy as a lora over the shared base and serve with V L L M with sixteen adapters resident, so per-team adapters batch over one copy of the seventy billion weights. Then benchmark the same harness on Trainium through V L L M on Neuron and let the cost per token table decide the fleet.

A few interview questions. Why Ray when torchrun exists? Because R L needs heterogeneous worker groups under a global controller, and placement groups plus an object store are exactly that; for S F T, torchrun is enough. F S D P or Megatron? F S D P two for fine-tuning dense models on a few nodes with no rewrite; Megatron when you need pipeline or expert parallelism at pretraining scale. Bedrock or self-managed? Bedrock when the model is supported, there is no ops capacity, and serving control does not matter; self-managed for custom kernels, custom harnesses, or Trainium-first economics, with HyperPod in between.
