Every other chapter in this notebook explains why a system works. This one is the tour of the tools you actually operate when you customize a model and take it to production, from the trainer that holds the weights to the managed service that hides the cluster. If you can name what each framework is for, its one core abstraction, and the moment you reach for it, you can design a customization program on a whiteboard in ten minutes. And one warning before we start. These A P Is move fast. What I describe are shapes and abstractions, and the docs are always the source of truth for the exact flags.

Start with the map, because every framework in this chapter lives at exactly one station of one pipeline. Data flows into an S F T trainer. The supervised checkpoint flows into an R L trainer, which cannot work alone, because it needs rollout engines to generate samples and environments or reward functions to score them. Checkpoints then flow through evals that gate releases. The winner flows into serving. And monitoring feeds production traffic back into the data station, closing the loop. Four things travel along the edges of this picture: weights, rollouts, rewards, and metrics. The hard engineering problems, resharding weights between a trainer and an inference engine, scaling environments, keeping evals honest, are all edge problems, not box problems. Hold that picture, because the rest of the chapter is just filling in the boxes.

The training station first. PyTorch F S D P two is the native implementation of the ZeRO three idea from the parallelism chapter. Every parameter, gradient, and optimizer state is sharded across the data parallel group, and each module's parameters are all-gathered just before its forward or backward pass, used, and then freed. The change from the original F S D P is the representation. F S D P two shards per parameter as a D Tensor, a tensor that carries its own placement over a device mesh, instead of flattening a whole module into one buffer. That one change buys composability, because the same D Tensor machinery implements tensor parallelism, so F S D P two plus tensor parallel is simply a two dimensional mesh. It also gives simpler checkpoints, natural partial freezing for lora, and torch compile compatibility. The knobs you configure are where you apply the fully shard call, usually on each transformer block and once on the root, the mixed precision policy with B F sixteen compute and F P thirty two master parameters and gradient reductions, whether to reshard after forward to save memory at the cost of a second all-gather, activation checkpointing, and the device mesh with its data parallel and tensor parallel dimensions. Reach for it for fine-tuning and post-training of dense models up to roughly a hundred billion parameters on a few nodes, anything lora or Q lora, and any time you want plain PyTorch modules with no model rewrite. It is the default trainer underneath Axolotl, T R L, verl's F S D P backend, NeMo R L's D Tensor backend, and TorchTitan.

Megatron-Core is the pretraining-scale toolkit from NVIDIA, a library of parallel transformer building blocks, parallel embeddings and linears, fused attention through Transformer Engine, mixture of experts layers, and pipeline schedules, with Megatron L M as the training harness around it. Where F S D P two gives you one parallelism in native PyTorch, Megatron gives you all of them as first-class flags, each with hand-optimized kernels. Tensor parallel splits every linear by columns and rows inside the N V Link island. Sequence parallel shards the norm and dropout activations along the sequence between tensor parallel ranks, reclaiming the activation memory tensor parallel leaves replicated. Pipeline parallel places layer stages across nodes with one F one B or interleaved schedules. Context parallel handles long sequences, expert parallel spreads experts across accelerators with all-to-all dispatch, and the distributed optimizer gives you the ZeRO one memory win. Two concepts matter in practice beyond the flags. The model spec declares which submodule implementations compose a layer, so swapping an attention or norm kernel is a spec change rather than a model rewrite. And the distributed checkpoint format stores tensors with their parallel layout, so a checkpoint saved at tensor parallel eight and pipeline parallel two can be loaded at tensor parallel four and pipeline parallel four. Megatron wins for pretraining or continued pretraining of large dense and mixture of experts models across many nodes, for anything that needs pipeline parallelism, and for mixture of experts training where grouped matrix multiplies and expert parallelism decide throughput. The cost is that your model must be expressed in Megatron's module zoo and the learning curve is real.

Here is the sharding difference said out loud, because interviewers love it. Picture one linear layer on four accelerators. Under F S D P two, the weight is sharded by rows, and before this layer can compute, all four devices all-gather the full matrix, compute, and then free it. It moves parameters once per step and tolerates slow interconnects between steps. Under Megatron tensor parallel, each device keeps a column slice permanently resident, computes a partial output locally, and the devices all-reduce the activation. It moves activations every single layer and therefore needs N V Link. Same layer, opposite trade, and real systems combine them on a two dimensional mesh.

Above those two sit three layers you will meet constantly. DeepSpeed predates F S D P and implements the same ZeRO stages plus C P U and N V M e offload and its own pipeline engine, configured through a J S O N file. Choose it when you need offload for a model that otherwise will not fit, or when an existing recipe uses it, and know that new PyTorch-native work lands in F S D P two. Axolotl is the Y A M L layer, where one config names the base model, the datasets and prompt format, the adapter, sequence packing, and the F S D P or DeepSpeed settings, and a single train command does the rest. It is the fastest path from fifty thousand examples to a trained adapter without writing trainer code. Hugging Face T R L is the Python counterpart, with an S F T trainer for supervised fine-tuning with packing and chat templates, a D P O trainer for preference pairs, a G R P O trainer for R L with reward functions that can generate through a co-located or remote V L L M, and reward model trainers, all inheriting F S D P and DeepSpeed support through accelerate. It is the right tool for experiments up to a node or two. At frontier scale you graduate to the R L frameworks.

Now the R L station, the one workload where three different systems must cooperate inside a single training step: a trainer that holds parameters and does backward passes, a rollout engine that generates samples fast, and environments and reward functions that score them. Verl, from ByteDance's Seed team and built on the HybridFlow paper, is the most widely used open R L framework for language models, and it has three ideas worth internalizing. First, the single-controller programming model. One Python driver on Ray expresses the algorithm as ordinary sequential code, generate a batch from the rollout group, compute reference log probabilities, compute rewards, update the actor, while each call fans out to a worker group of accelerators. The dataflow stays readable and the distributed execution is hidden. Second, worker groups per role, the actor with its rollout engine and frozen reference model usually co-located and time-slicing the same devices, a critic for P P O only, and an optional reward model. Each group has a backend, F S D P two or Megatron for training, V L L M or S G lang for generation. Third, the three D hybrid engine, which reshards the actor's weights in place from the trainer's layout into the engine's tensor parallel layout for generation, without a round trip through disk. That reshard and sync is the weight sync bar in the chapter's timeline animation. Algorithms are a config switch, G A E, G R P O, R L O O, REINFORCE plus plus, and the dapo refinements are flags.

Miles descends from slime, the R L framework the G L M team built around S G lang for rollouts and Megatron-Core for training. Where verl began colocated and synchronous and added asynchrony later, the slime and Miles lineage starts disaggregated. Rollout engines and the trainer are separate resource pools connected by a data buffer. Rollout workers keep generating, including long multi-turn agentic trajectories, while the trainer consumes batches with bounded policy staleness and importance weight correction. Its appeal is operational. Rollout and training scale and fail independently, S G lang's prefix caching and M L A paths serve DeepSeek-class mixture of experts rollouts natively, and the Megatron trainer gives frontier-scale parallelism. Choose Miles when rollout throughput dominates and you already run Megatron. Choose verl when you want the broadest algorithm coverage and the most reproductions.

NeMo R L is NVIDIA's post-training library, Ray-orchestrated, with D Tensor and Megatron backends and V L L M generation. Its distinctive companion is NeMo Gym, which turns the environment side into servers. A resource server hosts tools, a sandbox, or a reward model behind an H T T P interface, an agent definition describes how the policy interacts with it, and a recipe binds environments, rewards, data, and the trainer together. Scaling the environment becomes an ordinary service-scaling problem, and the same environment serves both evaluation and training. OpenEnv, initiated by Meta with the PyTorch ecosystem, standardizes what an agentic environment is: a Gym-style interface with reset, step, and state, served over H T T P from an isolated container, with typed action and observation schemas, tool exposure including M C P, and a hub for sharing environments. The point is decoupling. An environment author publishes a coding sandbox or an internal workflow once, and any trainer, verl, NeMo R L, T R L, or Miles, can train against it.

The serving station. V L L M is the default open-source engine and the default rollout engine inside the R L frameworks. Its V one architecture separates a front-end process that handles tokenization, the OpenAI-compatible A P I, and streaming, from an engine core that runs the scheduler and model executor in a tight loop. Every concept from the inference chapter has a flag attached. The scheduler does continuous batching with chunked prefill, bounded by maximum sequences and maximum batched tokens. The K V cache manager does paged attention blocks with prefix caching on by default. Parallelism flags cover tensor, pipeline, data, and expert parallel. Lora flags let requests name an adapter and the scheduler batches different adapters over one shared base, which is multi-adapter serving. Speculative decoding supports n-gram, draft model, and EAGLE or M T P methods, quantization covers F P eight weights and F P eight K V cache, and structured outputs use a grammar backend. S G lang shares most of that feature list and differs in emphasis. Its runtime is built around Radix Attention, where the K V cache is a radix tree over token prefixes, so any request sharing a prefix with a live or recent request, a system prompt, a few-shot header, multi-turn history, or the eight samples of a G R P O group, reuses it automatically. Its frontend language lets you write multi-call programs that branch, constrain, and reuse, scheduled as one prefix-sharing tree. It has been the reference implementation for DeepSeek-class serving, with data parallel attention and prefill decode disaggregation, and its router does prefix-aware load balancing. Choose S G lang when prefix reuse or DeepSeek-style models dominate. TensorRT L L M is NVIDIA's engine for peak performance on NVIDIA hardware, historically a compiled shape-specialized engine build, now also a PyTorch-backend flow with the same fused kernels and in-flight batching. It gives the lowest-level access to F P eight and N V F P four paths. Choose it when you own the NVIDIA fleet, the model is stable, and the last ten to twenty percent of throughput is worth an engine-build pipeline.

The Neuron S D K is the software stack for Trainium and Inferentia, and the mental shift from the G P U stack is that Neuron compiles graphs ahead of time. PyTorch code is traced into a graph, compiled by neuron x c c into an executable for fixed shapes, and run by the runtime. Dynamic shapes become buckets, a small set of compiled sequence lengths and batch sizes, and anything the compiler cannot lower falls back to C P U and must be found and eliminated, which is the Deepgram lesson of zero fallbacks. The pieces are the compiler itself, torch neuron x for tracing and the device abstraction, and NeuronX Distributed, the parallelism library. Its core provides parallel embeddings and column and row parallel linears, Megatron's ideas for Neuron cores. Its inference package ships ready model implementations with continuous batching, bucketing, speculative decoding, and quantization, and it is what V L L M's Neuron plugin calls into, so you get the V L L M A P I and scheduler with Neuron underneath. Its training package is the Megatron-style harness. Then the Neuron profiler, which shows per-engine timelines and D M A stalls, and N K I with the N K I Library when a hot layer needs a custom kernel. The concrete differences from the G P U workflow are a compile step you must budget and cache, bucketing instead of arbitrary shapes, parallelism degrees chosen with the core topology in mind, and a profiling loop that looks at engine utilization rather than streaming multiprocessor occupancy.

Three glue layers tie the stations together. Ray provides actors, stateful processes on specific resources, tasks, an object store for passing large tensors, and placement groups that reserve bundles of accelerators. That is exactly the vocabulary an R L framework needs. A worker group of eight actors pinned to one device each on one node is a placement group, sending a batch to the reward group is an object store transfer, and the driver calling generate on the rollout group is a remote method on an actor handle. For plain S F T you do not need Ray at all. Torchrun with one process per device is enough, scheduled under Slurm or Kubernetes. Checkpoints are where frameworks disagree, so know the three formats. Safetensors in the Hugging Face layout is the universal exchange format with no parallel layout. Distributed checkpoint, D C P, is what F S D P two and TorchTitan and verl's F S D P backend write, shard-wise and reshardable on load. Megatron's dist checkpoint stores tensors with parallel metadata. The practical rule is that you train in whatever sharded format your trainer wants, but every eval and every deployment consumes Hugging Face safetensors, so the merge and convert step is part of your pipeline from day one, and you keep the tokenizer and chat template with the weights, because most fine-tune-is-worse-in-production bugs are template mismatches. For embodied work, LeRobot plays all these roles at once, a dataset format, policy implementations, and train and eval C L Is, mirroring the same lesson that standard data formats and policy interfaces let training, evaluation, and deployment be swappable systems.

Finally the managed paths, because an F D E's job includes knowing when to say the customer should not self-manage. Amazon Bedrock is the fully managed model service. For customization it offers fine-tuning and continued pre-training jobs on supported foundation models, model distillation jobs where a teacher generates synthetic responses and a smaller student is fine-tuned on them, and Custom Model Import, which lets you bring your own fine-tuned open weights in safetensors and serve them through the Bedrock A P I on demand or with provisioned throughput, plus evaluations, guardrails, and managed R A G. The trade is minimal operations against limited control over serving internals. Amazon SageMaker A I is the managed infrastructure layer, with training jobs on managed instances including Trainium and G P Us, HyperPod persistent resilient clusters with automatic faulty-node replacement and checkpoint-aware resume for multi-week runs, and inference endpoints that host your own V L L M, S G lang, or Neuron containers. You still own the frameworks and their configs, but not the cluster plumbing. Self-managed on E C two or Kubernetes remains the right call for frontier scale, Trainium-first programs, custom R L harnesses, and bespoke serving.

Let me put the whole stack together on one problem, the chapter's worked example: post-train a seventy billion parameter coding model with R L on tests, then serve it with many adapters. For data and S F T, curate repository tasks with executable tests, the customer's own C I history being the gold source, build the supervised set from successful trajectories, and train a rank sixty four lora on a Llama three point one seventy B base with Axolotl on F S D P two across sixteen H one hundreds, a day rather than a week. Merge to safetensors and establish the eval baseline, pass at one on a held-out task suite, p ninety five latency, and cost per solved task. For R L, package the repository task environment as an OpenEnv container where reset restores a commit, step applies an edit or runs tests, and the reward is whether the tests pass. Run verl with G R P O or dapo, the actor on F S D P two, rollouts on V L L M at tensor parallel four with prefix caching so every group shares the repository context, eight samples per prompt, multi-turn tool calling, and environment tokens masked out of the loss. Expect generation to dominate and size the rollout share of accelerators accordingly, and if rollout throughput becomes the wall and you already run Megatron, evaluate Miles's disaggregated path. For serving, export the policy as a lora over the shared base and serve with V L L M with lora enabled and sixteen adapters resident, so per-team adapters batch over one copy of the seventy billion weights, with F P eight weights and K V cache for cost and EAGLE speculation for latency. Then benchmark the same harness on Trainium through V L L M on Neuron and let the cost per token table decide the fleet, gate releases with the eval suite, and stream production failures back into the task set.

A few misconceptions and the interview questions that come with them. Why Ray, when torchrun exists? Because R L needs heterogeneous worker groups with a global controller, and placement groups plus an object store are exactly that; for S F T, torchrun is enough and simpler. F S D P or Megatron? F S D P two for fine-tuning and post-training of dense models on a few nodes with no model rewrite; Megatron when you need pipeline or expert parallelism and hand-tuned kernels at pretraining scale, accepting the module zoo. Bedrock or self-managed? Bedrock when the model is supported, the team has no ops capacity, and serving control does not matter; self-managed when you need custom kernels, custom R L harnesses, or Trainium-first economics, with SageMaker HyperPod in between for resilient managed clusters. And the quiet killer: the merge and convert step and the chat template are part of the system, not an afterthought.
