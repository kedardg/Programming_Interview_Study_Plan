If you have ever opened a list of one hundred and fifty problems and felt the floor drop, this episode is for you. Grinding problems one at a time teaches you one hundred and fifty answers. Learning by pattern teaches you a dozen moves and lets you recognize which one a new problem is asking for before you write a line. That recognition is the whole game in a timed interview, and it is what Aditya Verma's channel is built around.

Two honest notes first. His videos are in Hindi and Hinglish, with captions. And the chapter you are listening alongside reconstructs his taxonomy from the structure of his public playlists, the video titles and their well known content, because the videos could not be fetched when the page was written. If you run the fetch script on your own machine, the videos' own captions get attached under every video slot, and the status card at the top shows how many transcripts are attached. We will come back to that at the end.

Here is what he does differently. Every playlist follows the same arc. First, identification: what in the problem statement tells you this is a sliding window problem, a knapsack problem, a monotonic stack problem? He says the cue out loud, as a rule you can test against the statement. Second, a parent problem solved from first principles: the brute force recursion with its choice diagram, then memoization added as a two line change, then the bottom up table derived by reading the memoized code. Third, the variations, a dozen problems that are each the parent with one line changed, so by the end you recognize a family rather than memorizing solutions.

The study plan uses him one specific way, and you should too. Hit the forty five minute cap on a problem. Watch the identification video and the parent video for the pattern, not the video for your exact problem. Close the video. Re-solve with A I assistance off. That is the same muscle memory rule Alisa Liu applies to M L coding, and it applies to LeetCode exactly the same way. The templates on the page are his approaches in idiomatic Python; the Coding Patterns chapter has full solutions for all seventy five.

Let us walk the playlists in the page's order.

Sliding window. His cue: the problem says subarray or substring, and gives either a window size k, a fixed window, or a condition to satisfy, a variable window, as in longest subarray such that something holds. The fixed template is three steps per index: add the new right element, check whether the window is full, and if it is, record the answer and drop the leftmost element. The variable template is expand right, shrink from the left while the condition is broken, and record the answer whenever it holds. The playlist runs from maximum sum subarray of size k and first negative in every window, through count of anagram occurrences and maximum of all subarrays of size k, into the variable window: largest subarray of sum k, longest substring with k unique characters, longest substring without repeating characters, and minimum window substring. On Blind seventy five that unlocks number fifty, longest substring without repeating characters, number fifty two, minimum window substring, and number fifty one, longest repeating character replacement, the same variable window with a different validity test. On neat code one fifty it also covers permutation in string, his anagram video, and sliding window maximum, his maximum of all subarrays video.

Binary search. His cue: the input is sorted, rotated sorted, bitonic, or, the big one, monotonic in the answer. If a yes or no question flips exactly once as a value grows, you can binary search the value; he calls it binary search on answer. The parent template is first occurrence: when you find the target you record it and keep searching on the answer side instead of stopping. Rotation count looks for the element smaller than both neighbours and discards the sorted half. Allocate minimum number of pages is the binary search on answer parent: the answer lies between the largest book and the total, and a feasibility function decides whether a capacity works. Blind number seven, find minimum in rotated sorted array, and number eight, search in rotated sorted array, are literally his videos. On neat code, Koko eating bananas is allocate minimum pages with bananas instead of books, search a two D matrix is his sorted matrix video, and time based key value store is his floor video.

Stack. His cue: the brute force is a nested loop where the inner loop scans left or right from position i for the nearest element satisfying a comparison. That dependence on the nearest element is the monotonic stack signature, and four base problems generate everything: nearest greater to the left, nearest greater to the right, nearest smaller to the left, nearest smaller to the right. The template keeps a stack of candidates in decreasing order; for each element pop everything that cannot be the answer, read the top, push the element. Maximum area histogram is nearest smaller on both sides giving each bar its width, and from there come stock span, maximum rectangle in a binary matrix, rain water trapping, min stack, and the celebrity problem. On Blind seventy five that is number fifty five, valid parentheses. On neat code it is daily temperatures, which is nearest greater to the right, largest rectangle in histogram, trapping rain water, min stack, and car fleet.

Recursion and backtracking. His cue is a method rather than a keyword: decide the input and output of the recursive call, shrink the input by one decision, and draw the recursion tree. He calls it I B H, induction, base, hypothesis. Backtracking is the same tree with a choice taken and undone at each node, so print all and count all problems live here. Subsets is choose or skip each character; balanced parentheses is that tree with the choices constrained by the counts remaining; height of a binary tree is the purest hypothesis, assume the children's heights are known and add one. The playlist runs through sorting a stack recursively, reversing a stack, kth symbol in grammar, tower of Hanoi, subsets and unique subsets, permutations with spaces and case changes, balanced parentheses, and Josephus. On Blind seventy five, number sixty, maximum depth of a binary tree, is his height video, the recursion on trees move covers numbers sixty one through seventy, and word search, numbers forty nine and seventy three, is backtracking on a grid. On neat code, subsets, subsets two, and generate parentheses are his videos.

Heap. His cue: the statement contains a k and one of the words smallest, largest, closest, frequent, or top. Sorting costs n log n; a heap of size k costs n log k. The rule is counterintuitive and worth saying twice: for k largest keep a min heap of size k, so the top is the kth largest; for k smallest keep a max heap of size k; for closest, heap on the distance. The playlist is kth smallest and largest, sort a k sorted array, k closest numbers, top k frequent, frequency sort, k closest points to origin, and connect ropes. On Blind seventy five, number seventy four, top k frequent elements, is his video; seventy five, find median from a data stream, is two heaps; forty three, merge k sorted lists, is his k sorted array move with a heap of list heads. On neat code it adds kth largest element in a stream, k closest points, task scheduler, and last stone weight.

Dynamic programming, the long one. His cue: there is a choice at every element, take it or not, cut here or not, match or skip, and the question asks for an optimum or a count. Write the recursion with its choice diagram, memoize on the parameters that change, then tabulate. He organizes the fifty video playlist into families. Zero one knapsack: include or exclude each item against a capacity; subset sum, equal sum partition, count of subsets, minimum subset sum difference, and target sum are the same table with booleans or counts instead of a max. Unbounded knapsack reuses the current row after taking an item, giving rod cutting, coin change for the number of ways, coin change for the minimum coins, and maximum ribbon cut. Longest common subsequence: on a match take one plus the diagonal, otherwise the max of up and left; then longest common substring, shortest common supersequence, minimum insertions and deletions, longest palindromic subsequence, which is L C S of the string with its reverse, and minimum deletions to make a palindrome. Matrix chain multiplication is partition D P: for a range try every split point and combine the halves; palindrome partitioning, boolean parenthesization, scrambled strings, and egg dropping all have that shape. And D P on trees: diameter and the maximum path sum variants. On Blind seventy five, number seventeen, coin change, number nineteen, longest common subsequence, and number sixty three, binary tree maximum path sum, are his videos directly. Indirectly, house robber is the include or exclude choice of knapsack, combination sum four is ordered coin change, word break is partition D P, and climbing stairs, longest increasing subsequence, and unique paths are the Fibonacci, L I S, and grid families he names in his introduction.

Graphs, briefly. His cue: entities with relationships, cities and roads, courses and prerequisites, grid cells and their neighbours. Then ask which template: traversal with breadth first search or depth first search, ordering with topological sort, cycles, connectivity, or shortest path. Grids are graphs whose edges are the four directions. The templates are a queue based breadth first search and Kahn's topological sort, where leftover nodes mean a cycle. On Blind seventy five that is clone graph, course schedule, number of islands, and alien dictionary directly.

Now the reverse index, the part of the page you will use most. It lists all seventy five Blind problems with the plan's numbering and all one hundred and fifty neat code problems in their eighteen categories, and gives each his pattern as a colored pill, the video that teaches the move, and a coverage label. Direct means a video solves this problem or an isomorphic one. Indirect means the playlist teaches the move and you apply it. Other means a technique his playlists do not cover, hash maps, two pointers, linked lists, intervals, bits, tries, where the Coding Patterns chapter is your source. The numbers: his playlists cover fourteen of the seventy five directly and thirty one more indirectly, and forty one of the one hundred and fifty directly with sixty two more indirectly. There is a text filter, one button per pattern, and a direct only toggle. Filter by the pattern you are weakest in and read down the list; you will see the same video name repeating, and that repetition is the point.

The page also orders the playlists to the plan's thirteen weeks. Weeks one and two, alongside arrays, binary, and linked lists: sliding window and the binary search introduction, the shortest playlists with the clearest cues. Weeks three and four: the rest of binary search through binary search on answer. Weeks five and six, with strings, trees, and intervals: stack and recursion, because nearest greater unlocks histogram and rain water, and the recursion playlist is the base of every tree and backtracking problem that follows. Weeks seven through nine: heap, then graph. Weeks ten through thirteen, the D P block and interview mode: dynamic programming in his order, zero one knapsack, unbounded, L C S, M C M, then trees. That matches the plan's own rule: stuck on LeetCode, keep the forty five minute cap, then watch the matching tutorial before you retry.

About the transcripts. Every video on the page has a slot, filled from a folder that a script populates. Run python three scripts slash fetch underscore transcripts dot p y on your own machine with y t d l p installed. It resolves his playlists, downloads each video's own captions in Hindi and English, strips the timestamps, and writes one text file per video plus an index the page reads. Serve the repo over H T T P and the slots show the captions; from a plain file they stay empty with a note. They are auto captions, so expect Hinglish and the occasional garbled technical word, but they let you search a video for the exact moment he states a cue.

What to do when this ends. Open the reverse index and filter by your weakest pattern. Drill the A V Patterns deck until the identification cues are reflexive. Pick one problem from that list, watch the video the index names, close it, and re-solve the problem in the Coding Patterns chapter with A I off. Do that once per pattern and the one hundred and fifty stop being one hundred and fifty.
