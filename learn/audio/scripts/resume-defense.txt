Welcome to the chapter on defending your résumé. Every other chapter in this notebook teaches a concept. This one starts from the other end: from the bullets on a résumé, and the questions an interviewer asks when they decide to test one. The bullets we use belong to a forward-deployed senior data scientist who post-trains open-weight language models for a lending and financial-planning domain. We never name the employer or the product. We call each one "the bullet claiming", because that is also how an interviewer will refer to it.

The method is simple. Every claim has three layers. Layer one is the claim itself: what changed, for whom, by how much, and measured how. Layer two is the mechanism: why the technique you chose produced the change, and what you compared it against. Layer three is the receipts: parameter counts, bytes per parameter, tokens, GPU hours, pair counts, labeller agreement. The interviewer decides how deep to go. Your job is to have all three ready for every bullet.

Start with the bullet claiming a five percent gain on TAT-QA from post-training gpt-oss-20B on eight A100s. TAT-QA is a question answering benchmark over hybrid financial-report context: each question comes with a table and the paragraphs around it, about sixteen and a half thousand questions, with span, multi-span, count and arithmetic answers, scored by exact match and a numeracy-aware F1 that checks both the value and the scale. The first trap is that "five percent" has no unit. Five percentage points and five percent relative are different claims. Fix it by saying the metric, the split, and the start and end numbers.

gpt-oss-20B is a mixture-of-experts model: about twenty-one billion total parameters, about three point six billion active per token, thirty-two experts with four routed per token, released in M X F P four so the checkpoint is about thirteen gigabytes. A100s have no four-bit tensor cores, so training upcasts the experts to B F sixteen, which is about forty-two gigabytes of weights. That single number drives every memory decision in the bullet.

The bullet lists five techniques. Supervised fine-tuning on distilled reasoning traces means a stronger teacher writes derivations, you keep the ones whose final answer matches the gold label, and you train the student on them. Be ready to say how many traces, the teacher's acceptance rate, whether you masked the prompt, and how the gain splits across answer types. If most of the gain is on arithmetic answers, the traces story holds. LoRA rank sweeps: trainable parameters per adapted matrix are rank times the sum of input and output dimensions. The sweep usually plateaus; be ready to name the rank you chose and what you held fixed. On a mixture of experts, say which modules the adapter targeted, and whether you left the routers frozen.

"PPO slash DPO" reads as hedging. They need different data. PPO needs a reward signal and four models in memory. DPO needs pairs and two models. And for a benchmark with gold answers, the modern default is GRPO with a verifiable reward, so expect "why not that?" A defensible story is that DPO on pairs mined from the rejection-sampling step gave most of the gain, and PPO matched it at higher cost, so you shipped DPO.

Now the seam interviewers look for: DeepSpeed sharding and LoRA in the same bullet. ZeRO shards optimizer state, gradients and parameters so that a model that does not fit on one GPU can be trained on many. LoRA exists so that you do not need the optimizer state for the full model. Do the arithmetic. Full fine-tuning in B F sixteen with Adam keeps sixteen bytes per parameter, so twenty-one billion parameters need about three hundred and thirty-six gigabytes, which fits eight eighty-gigabyte A100s under ZeRO stage three. LoRA needs the forty-two gigabyte frozen base per GPU, which fits an eighty-gigabyte card with no sharding at all, but not a forty-gigabyte card. So either DeepSpeed was simply the data-parallel training loop, or the cards were forty gigabytes and ZeRO three was required, or you ran a full fine-tune and the LoRA sweep was an ablation. Pick the true one and say it.

Serving with vLLM tensor parallelism invites the same kind of question, because the model fits on one A100. The honest reasons for tensor parallelism anyway are latency, since decode is bandwidth-bound and two GPUs read the weights in half the time; KV-cache headroom for long financial-report contexts; or forty-gigabyte cards. Otherwise, two data-parallel replicas give more throughput per dollar.

The second bullet claims a post-training data loop: vLLM baselines across five models, an LLM judge with a materiality rubric, and synthetic, human-verified DPO pairs. The questions are about rigour. For the baselines, be able to sketch the table of what you held fixed: prompt template, decoding settings, reasoning effort, maximum tokens, scoring script, precision, seeds. For the judge, materiality is the accounting idea that a misstatement matters if it could change a decision; the rubric grades sign and scale errors as critical and rounding as immaterial. The judge must be calibrated against humans on a labelled subset, with position bias, length bias and self-preference controlled. For the pairs, "synthetic, human-verified" invites "verified how, by whom, how many": give counts generated, kept and reviewed, and the agreement. Then know the DPO loss and what beta does, and that a loop means more than one round.

The third bullet claims four times deal conversion from a PageRank lead list over news graphs and CRM data. PageRank is a whiteboard algorithm, so know it properly. A random surfer follows an out-edge with probability d, the damping factor, usually zero point eight five, and teleports with probability one minus d. The stationary distribution is the PageRank vector: p equals one minus d times v, plus d times M transpose p. Global PageRank teleports uniformly. Personalized PageRank teleports to a seed set, your existing customers from the CRM, so the score becomes proximity to the seeds through the graph, which is exactly what a lead list wants. Power iteration converges geometrically at rate d, each step costs one pass over the edges, and dangling nodes get their mass redistributed. Open the chapter's animation and step through it with personalization on and off: the winning node changes, and that is the whole point.

The business side of that bullet is the number. Four times conversion needs a baseline, a window, a denominator and attribution. Conversion of the old list over the same quarters, counting leads contacted, ideally from an interleaved test. A before-and-after over a market upswing is not four times. A smaller, well-attributed number is worth more in the room than a large unattributed one.

The fourth bullet claims schema normalization with retrieve-then-select, SFT then DPO on hard negatives and synthetic distractors. Retrieve means a bi-encoder pulls the top-k canonical fields; select means the model picks one, or none. SFT trains the selection; DPO's rejected side is a candidate the retriever ranked highly but which is wrong, or a distractor that differs by unit or period. Retriever recall at k bounds the whole system, so carry that number. And notice that SFT-then-DPO now appears in two bullets. Present it as one reusable recipe and explain how the negatives differed between the tasks.

The fifth bullet claims RLHF end to end: a LoRA reward model, clipped PPO, generalized advantage estimation, and a KL penalty to a frozen reference. The reward model is Bradley-Terry: minimise negative log sigmoid of the reward gap between chosen and rejected. The clipped objective takes the minimum of the ratio times the advantage and the clipped ratio times the advantage, with epsilon around zero point two. The KL penalty is per token, with beta around zero point zero five, and sequence KL growing past a few nats is the first sign of reward hacking. GAE computes deltas as reward plus gamma times next value minus value, and sums them with weights gamma lambda to the power l. Lambda one is Monte Carlo; lambda zero is one-step TD. The chapter's animation runs that recursion on a twelve-token response. The consistency question is unavoidable: PPO appears in the first bullet too. Either this bullet is the same system, in which case merge them, or it is a different task, in which case name it and its metric.

The sixth bullet claims more than sixty percent faster per-record processing from a multi-agent medical record review system with RAG, MCP servers, and drift and hallucination guardrails. The domain jump from finance to clinical records will be noticed; have one sentence of context. Then the parts: an orchestrator, specialist workers and a reconciler; section-level chunking and hybrid retrieval with a recall number; the Model Context Protocol, which exposes tools, resources and prompts over JSON-RPC so any agent runtime can use them; hallucination guardrails that require every claim to cite a span and check entailment; and drift monitors for inputs, hosted models and prompts with a nightly golden set. Sixty percent faster than what, over how many records, and did quality hold on an audited sample. Carry both numbers.

Finally, the consistency checklist. PPO twice. SFT-then-DPO twice. Three numbers without units or baselines. Sharding beside LoRA. Tensor parallelism for a model that fits one GPU. A slash between two algorithms. Synthetic beside human-verified. A judge with no calibration number. Six substantial projects in about a year. Each of these has a one-line resolution, and resolving it usually makes the bullet better. The chapter ends with rewrite templates and a stress-tester you can paste your own bullets into.
