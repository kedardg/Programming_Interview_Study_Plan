This chapter takes you from a single neuron to the training dynamics of billion parameter models. Forward and backward passes, the training loop, optimizers, and what changes at scale. The chain rule and gradient descent from the math chapter do all the heavy lifting here, and if you listen closely you will notice that almost nothing in this chapter is new math. It is the same two ideas, applied with discipline, at enormous scale.

Start with the simplest learned function: linear regression. Y equals w x plus b, fit by minimizing squared error. A neuron is linear regression with many inputs and one twist: a nonlinear activation function applied to the weighted sum. The twist is everything. Stack linear layers without nonlinearities and the stack collapses, because a composition of linear maps is itself a linear map. That is the "matrices compose" fact from the previous chapter. With nonlinearities between the layers, stacked layers can bend space, and the universal approximation theorem says even one sufficiently wide hidden layer can approximate any continuous function.

So why go deep? Depth is not needed for possibility. It is needed for efficiency. Deep networks reuse intermediate features the way programs reuse subroutines, representing in thousands of parameters functions that a shallow network would need exponentially many for. A multi layer perceptron, an M L P, alternates linear maps and activations. Modern transformers still carry this exact block. The feed forward network inside every transformer layer is a two layer M L P, and it holds roughly two thirds of the model's parameters.

Which activation? The field converged on a small family. ReLU, the maximum of zero and x, is cheap and it avoids the vanishing gradients that killed sigmoid and tanh networks, because its gradient is exactly one wherever the unit is active. But a ReLU unit can die. If it gets stuck at zero, it gets zero gradient forever. GELU and SiLU, also called Swish, smooth the corner so gradients flow even slightly below zero, and they are the defaults in modern language models, often in the gated SwiGLU arrangement, which multiplies two projections together so the network learns its own per feature gate. Look at the activation plotter on the page with the derivative overlay on. Sigmoid's derivative never exceeds zero point two five and vanishes in both tails. Stack ten sigmoid layers and the gradient can shrink by zero point two five to the tenth power, about one in a million, on its way back. That is the vanishing gradient problem, visible in one dashed curve.

Now the forward and backward pass. Training needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every weight, and backpropagation computes all of them in one backward sweep, at roughly twice the cost of the forward pass. The algorithm is just the chain rule with bookkeeping. Run forward and remember the intermediate activations. Then walk backward from the loss, computing each node's gradient from the gradients of the nodes that consumed it. Each layer needs only local knowledge: given the gradient of the loss with respect to my output, produce the gradient with respect to my inputs and my weights. That locality is why autograd frameworks can differentiate arbitrary graphs.

The page animates this on a tiny network: two inputs, three hidden ReLU units, one output. The input is one point zero and zero point five, the target is one point zero, and the loss is one half of the squared error. Let me talk through the shape of what you will see. Forward, each hidden neuron computes its pre activation z as a weighted sum, then its activation h as ReLU of z. The output y is a weighted sum of the hidden activations. The loss is one half times y minus target, squared. Backward, the gradient at the output is simply y minus the target. Each output weight's gradient is that upstream gradient times the hidden activation it multiplies. Then the gradient flows back into each hidden unit, gets multiplied by the ReLU derivative, which is one if the unit was active and zero if it was not, and then each input weight's gradient is that times the input it multiplies. The animation deliberately includes one dead ReLU, a hidden unit whose pre activation is negative, so its activation is zero. Watch its incoming weights receive exactly zero gradient. A dead unit learns nothing, ever, unless something upstream changes.

Two facts from this picture reach all the way into systems design later in the track. First, those stored activations are why training uses so much more memory than inference, and why activation checkpointing trades recomputation for memory. Second, the backward pass does two matrix multiplies for every forward matrix multiply, one for the gradient with respect to activations and one for the gradient with respect to weights. So training flops are about three times forward flops. That ratio is the source of the six N D rule we will get to.

With gradients in hand, training is a loop. Sample a minibatch, run forward, compute the loss, run backward, take an optimizer step, repeat. Minibatches rather than the full dataset make each step cheap, and their gradient noise is not purely a defect. It regularizes, helping the model find flatter minima that generalize better. Batch size is also the main throughput lever on GPUs, which is why the GPU chapter cares about it so much.

The loop's central tension is generalization. Training loss falling is guaranteed; you are optimizing it directly. What you care about is validation loss on held out data. When the two diverge, training loss keeps falling while validation turns upward, the model is memorizing rather than learning. That is overfitting. The classical toolkit: weight decay shrinks weights toward zero each step and prefers simpler functions. Dropout randomly zeros a fraction of units each forward pass so no unit can depend on a specific partner, and at inference dropout is off. Early stopping keeps the checkpoint where validation loss bottomed out. And more data, which is the regularizer that actually built modern language models. It is worth knowing that L L M pretraining mostly skips dropout. With trillions of tokens each seen once, memorization is not the binding constraint. The overfitting animation on the page shows the train and validation curves separating, with a cross marking the early stopping point, and a live network where each epoch a new random dropout mask greys out hidden units.

Normalization is why training deep networks is possible at all. As layers train, the distribution of their outputs shifts under the layers above them, destabilizing optimization. Normalization layers re center and re scale activations so each layer sees inputs at a predictable scale. BatchNorm normalizes each feature across the minibatch. It is great for convolutional networks, but it couples examples in a batch and behaves differently at training and evaluation time. LayerNorm normalizes across features within each example, batch independent, so it became the transformer default. R M S Norm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and just divides by the root mean square, with a learned gain. One fewer reduction, one fewer parameter. It works just as well and it is cheaper, so Llama class models use it. It is also simple enough that writing its fused Triton kernel is a week six exercise in the study plan.

Now optimizers. Plain stochastic gradient descent follows the raw minibatch gradient, and its failure mode is the elongated valley from the math chapter. Huge gradients across the valley, tiny ones along it, so the safe learning rate is set by the steep direction and progress along the flat direction crawls. Each improvement fixes part of this. Momentum keeps a velocity: v becomes beta times v plus the gradient, and the weights move along v. Oscillations cancel in the velocity while progress along the valley accumulates. Adam adds a second idea: divide each weight's step by the square root of a running average of its squared gradients, so every weight gets its own effective step size, and steep and flat directions get rescaled automatically. AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weights rather than mixing it into the gradient, so decay strength is no longer entangled with the adaptive rescaling. AdamW is the L L M default.

Adam's price is memory. It keeps two F P thirty two state tensors, the first and second moments, per parameter. With F P thirty two master weights that comes to about sixteen bytes of optimizer state per parameter. A seventy billion parameter model therefore needs over a terabyte of optimizer state, which is the single biggest reason training needs the sharding techniques, ZeRO and F S D P, that the training chapter covers. Watch the optimizer race on the page. S G D zig zags and crawls. Momentum damps the zig zag and accelerates down the valley. Adam rescales each coordinate by its own history and moves almost straight to the minimum. The two dimensional cartoon is unfair in some ways, but the geometry it shows is the honest reason these optimizers exist.

No single learning rate is right for a whole run. L L M training almost universally uses warmup followed by cosine decay. Ramp linearly from near zero over the first few hundred or few thousand steps, because early gradients are huge and poorly scaled and Adam's second moment estimates are not yet calibrated, so warmup prevents an immediate blow up. Then decay smoothly so late training takes fine grained steps that settle into a good minimum. A variant worth knowing is warmup stable decay, which holds the plateau and decays only at the end, so one long run can be checkpoint forked into several shorter ones.

Finally, what changes at scale. Scaling laws are the empirical backbone of modern language models. Across many orders of magnitude, loss falls as a smooth power law in model size N and training tokens D. The Chinchilla paper, Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind, two thousand twenty two, turned this into a budgeting rule: for a fixed compute budget, loss is minimized near twenty tokens per parameter. A seventy billion parameter model wants about one point four trillion tokens, not the three hundred billion that earlier models used. Production models now train far past compute optimal. Llama three eight B saw fifteen trillion tokens, roughly nineteen hundred per parameter, because inference cost rewards over training a small model. You pay training flops once and serving costs forever.

The companion rule prices a run. One token of training costs about six flops per parameter: two for the forward matmuls and four for backward. So training flops are approximately six times N times D. Let us do Llama three eight B out loud. Six, times eight billion parameters, times fifteen trillion tokens, is about seven point two times ten to the twenty three flops. On one thousand H one hundreds at forty percent utilization, each delivering about four hundred teraflops per second of useful work, that is seven point two times ten to the twenty three divided by four times ten to the seventeen, which is about one point eight million seconds, or about twenty one days. That one calculation contains the vocabulary of every training infrastructure conversation: M F U, model flops utilization, the achieved useful flops over hardware peak, where thirty five to forty five percent is respectable at scale, and the gap between peak and achieved, which the GPU and parallelism chapters spend themselves explaining.

Two more realities round out the picture. Instability: big runs hit loss spikes, sudden jumps from rare bad batches or numerical issues. The defenses are gradient clipping, which rescales the global gradient norm to a cap, typically one point zero, so no single step is catastrophic, plus careful initialization and skipping or rewinding bad batches. And mixed precision: compute in B F sixteen, which halves memory and bandwidth and runs at twice F P thirty two throughput on tensor cores, but keep F P thirty two master weights and optimizer state, because a B F sixteen weight's seven bit mantissa cannot absorb a tiny gradient update. The addition rounds to nothing and learning silently stalls. F P sixteen needs an extra loss scaling trick to stop small gradients underflowing its narrow range. B F sixteen's F P thirty two sized exponent is exactly why it displaced F P sixteen for training.

Some interview questions you should be able to answer after this. Why not just use a very wide single hidden layer? Because depth buys efficiency through feature reuse, not just possibility. What does Adam store per parameter and why does that matter for a seventy B model? Two moments in F P thirty two, sixteen bytes per parameter with the master copy, over a terabyte total. Why warmup? Because early gradients are badly scaled and Adam's statistics are uncalibrated. Why B F sixteen over F P sixteen? Range beats precision for training, and no loss scaling is needed. And why do we keep F P thirty two master weights at all? Because small updates round to nothing in a seven bit mantissa.

The same sixty points sit on two canvases. On the left, logistic regression trains, and all it can ever draw is one straight line through the plane, so it cuts off the inner cluster and stays wrong no matter how many epochs you give it, which is a capacity limit, not an under training problem. On the right, a network with one hidden layer of tanh units trains on the same points, and its boundary bends around the cluster because each hidden unit contributes one bend and the output layer combines them. That wrapping is overfitting, and it is the reason the rest of the chapter talks about validation curves and regularization.
