Every number in a neural network lives in some binary format, and that format quietly decides three things: how much memory the model takes, how fast it can be read from memory, and how much silicon it costs to multiply. Quantization is the art of spending fewer bits per number without the model noticing. We will go from how a float is built, to why a four bit Llama seventy B decodes nearly four times faster, to the one sparsity pattern hardware rewards.

Start with the bits. A floating point number spends its bits on three jobs: one sign bit, a few exponent bits, and the remaining mantissa bits. The split is the entire personality of the format. Exponent bits buy range: with E exponent bits you get roughly two to the E powers of two to play with. F P thirty two and B F sixteen both have eight exponent bits, so both reach about three times ten to the thirty eight. F P sixteen has only five, so it tops out at sixty five thousand five hundred and four, and it underflows below about six times ten to the minus eight. Mantissa bits buy precision: M mantissa bits give you two to the M evenly spaced values inside each power of two, which is about M plus one significant bits.

The decoded value of a normal float is minus one to the sign, times two to the exponent minus the bias, times one plus the mantissa divided by two to the M. When the exponent field is all zeros the number is subnormal: the implicit leading one disappears and the format fades gently to zero. When the exponent is all ones you get infinity or not a number. One famous exception: F P eight E four M three is so starved for bits that it gives up infinity entirely.

Here is the question an interviewer loves: why did B F sixteen win training over F P sixteen? Because F P sixteen's five bit exponent underflows gradients and overflows loss scales, which forced everyone into fiddly loss scaling tricks. B F sixteen keeps the full eight bit exponent of F P thirty two, so it has the same range and needs no loss scaling, and it gives up mantissa bits instead. The current training recipe is B F sixteen master weights with F P eight, and now F P four, matrix multiplies wherever they prove stable.

Now the math of quantization itself. Quantization maps real values onto a small integer grid plus a little shared metadata. In the symmetric, or absmax, scheme, the scale is the largest absolute value divided by one hundred twenty seven for int eight. Divide by the scale, round to an integer code, and multiply back to dequantize. Let us do the chapter's worked example out loud. Quantize the four numbers zero point three, minus one point two, two point four, and zero point zero five. The absmax is two point four, so the scale is two point four over one hundred twenty seven, about zero point zero one eight nine. Zero point three divided by the scale is fifteen point eight seven, which rounds to sixteen, and sixteen times the scale is zero point three zero two four. The error is plus zero point zero zero two four. Two point four becomes exactly one hundred twenty seven and comes back exact; zero point zero five becomes code three and comes back as zero point zero five six seven. The largest value is perfect; everything else lands on the nearest grid point.

Now the catch, and it is the single most important picture in the chapter: one outlier sets the scale for everyone. If a single weight is fifty while the rest live between minus one and one, the grid step becomes fifty over one hundred twenty seven, about zero point three nine, and every normal weight collapses onto two or three codes. The fix is granularity. Per tensor uses one scale for the whole matrix. Per channel gives every output row its own. Per group gives each run of thirty two to one hundred twenty eight elements its own scale, the default for four bit L L M weights.

Next, weight only post training quantization, where the first four X is nearly free. Remember from the inference chapter that decode is bandwidth bound: every generated token requires streaming every weight byte out of H B M. Llama seventy B in F P sixteen is about one hundred forty gigabytes. In four bit it is about thirty five gigabytes, plus roughly three percent for the group scales. Same flops, one quarter of the bytes, up to four times faster decode at small batch, and the model fits on half as many G P Us. This is W four A sixteen: weights stored in int four, activations kept in F P sixteen, and the weights dequantized on the fly inside the matrix multiply kernel, as in Marlin or ExLlama, so the tensor cores still do F P sixteen math.

Rounding each weight to the nearest grid point is not optimal, because what matters is the layer's output, not each weight individually. The ecosystem settled on two algorithms. G P T Q, by Frantar and colleagues in twenty twenty two, quantizes a weight matrix column by column, and after fixing each column it updates the remaining columns to compensate, using second order information, the Hessian of the layer's reconstruction error built from calibration activations. A W Q, activation aware weight quantization by Lin and colleagues in twenty twenty three, observes that a small fraction of weight channels matter far more than others, namely the ones multiplied by large activations. Rather than mixed precision, it rescales: divide the salient activation channels by a factor s and multiply the matching weight channels by s, which is mathematically a no op, so the important weights occupy more of the grid before rounding. No backprop, no Hessian, and the usual default for four bit serving.

Weight only quantization stops helping once the batch grows and the matrix multiplies become compute bound. Then you want cheaper math, which means quantizing activations too, so the tensor cores can run at int eight, F P eight, or F P four rates. Each halving of operand width roughly doubles peak flops. F P eight on Hopper and Ada uses E four M three for forward tensors and E five M two where range matters. Each tensor carries a scale chosen from its recent amax history, called delayed scaling. DeepSeek V three even trained in F P eight. SmoothQuant migrates the difficulty from outlier heavy activations into the weights, which can afford it, so both quantize cleanly to eight bits.

At four bits per tensor scaling is hopeless, so on Blackwell the scale moves into the format itself. M X F P four gives every thirty two elements a shared power of two scale. N V F P four gives every sixteen elements a shared F P eight E four M three scale, plus one F P thirty two scale per tensor. Finer blocks and a real valued scale are why N V F P four holds accuracy where plain M X F P four struggles. It is the per group idea baked into silicon.

Then the K V cache. At long context the cache, not the weights, dominates memory, and it is read every decode step, so quantizing it buys capacity and speed at once. The wrinkle, from the K I V I paper by Liu and colleagues, is that keys and values fail differently. Key tensors have a few channels with persistently large magnitude across all tokens, so keys want per channel scales. Value tensors have no outlier channels but are consumed token by token through the attention weights, so values quantize best per token. With that split, two bit K V works, and F P eight K V cache is near lossless and a standard serving flag today.

Training can also learn to live on the grid. Quantization aware training inserts fake quantization into the forward pass, rounding weights and activations to the target grid and back, so the network experiences quantization noise while it still has gradients to adapt with. Rounding has zero gradient almost everywhere, so the backward pass uses the straight through estimator: pretend rounding is the identity. Crude, but it recovers most of the gap at four bits and below. Q lora, by Dettmers and colleagues in twenty twenty three, takes the other road: freeze the base model in N F four, a sixteen point grid placed at the quantiles of a standard normal distribution, which is information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights, and train small F P sixteen lora adapters on top. Gradients flow through the frozen four bit weights into the adapters only, so a seventy B fine tune fits where only a seven B used to.

Finally sparsity. Quantization shrinks every number; sparsity deletes some entirely. The catch is that hardware hates irregularity. A matrix with random zeros still has to be fetched and multiplied unless the pattern is structured enough for silicon to exploit. That is why two to four structured sparsity, supported since Ampere, is the only sparsity with first class hardware support: in every group of four weights, exactly two must be zero. The G P U stores the two survivors plus two bit indices, halving memory, and the sparse tensor cores skip the zeros for up to two times math throughput. Unstructured pruning removes more weights but nothing can be skipped, so it rarely pays on G P Us.

Three misconceptions to retire. First, lower bits always means faster. No. Weight only four bit helps the bandwidth bound decode regime; once you are compute bound you need activation quantization so the math itself gets cheaper. Second, int eight is one thing. It is not; per tensor, per channel, and per group int eight are different models with different accuracy. A good interview answer connects format choice to the roofline: find your bound, then pick the technique that attacks it.
