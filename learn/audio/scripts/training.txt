How does a pile of text become a chat model? Not in one step. A modern language model passes through four kinds of learning, and the stages are wildly asymmetric: pretraining eats more than ninety nine percent of the compute and sets almost all of the model's knowledge, while everything after it is steering, cheap by comparison but decisive for behavior. This chapter walks the pipeline, then the memory anatomy of a training step, then lora and Q lora, then D P O and G R P O, and finally Axolotl as the practical map that ties it all together.

The pipeline first. Pretraining consumes trillions of tokens of web text, code, and books, with next token cross entropy as the objective, at ten to the twenty four to ten to the twenty six flops, weeks on thousands of G P Us. Supervised fine tuning uses curated instruction and response pairs with the loss on response tokens only. Preference tuning uses chosen and rejected pairs. And reinforcement learning on tasks uses verifiable rewards, tests that pass, math that checks.

Pretraining mechanics. The objective could not be simpler: for every position in every sequence, predict the next token, with cross entropy between the model's distribution and the actual token. The compute follows the six N D rule: training flops are about six times N parameters times D training tokens. Two N D for the forward pass and four N D for the backward. Here is the worked example. Llama three seventy B on fifteen trillion tokens: six times seventy billion times fifteen trillion is about six point three times ten to the twenty four flops. An H one hundred peaks near nine hundred eighty nine teraflops in B F sixteen; at forty percent model flops utilization that is about four times ten to the fourteen useful flops per second per G P U. Divide, and you get about one point six times ten to the ten G P U seconds, roughly four point four million G P U hours, which is about sixteen thousand H one hundreds for eleven or twelve days, ignoring restarts.

Then the Chinchilla question. The Chinchilla paper by Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind in twenty twenty two showed that for a fixed compute budget, loss is minimized near twenty tokens per parameter, so a seventy B model wants about one point four trillion tokens. But compute optimal is not cost optimal once you count inference. A smaller model trained far past Chinchilla, like Llama three eight B at fifteen trillion tokens, nearly nineteen hundred tokens per parameter, is cheaper to serve forever at slightly higher training cost. Modern frontier models are heavily over trained for exactly this reason. It is a model economics decision, not a scaling law violation. Data mixture matters as much as architecture, and long runs hit loss spikes, where the playbook is rewind to the last checkpoint, skip the offending data, and continue.

Now the single most practically useful fact in the chapter: the memory anatomy of a training step. Inference needs weights plus K V cache. Training needs much more. With Adam and standard mixed precision, every parameter costs sixteen bytes before a single activation is stored: two bytes for the B F sixteen weights the forward pass reads, two bytes for the B F sixteen gradients, four bytes for the F P thirty two master weights the optimizer updates, four bytes for Adam's momentum, and four bytes for Adam's variance. Sixteen bytes per parameter, so a seven B model needs one hundred twelve gigabytes before activations. That is why a seven B fine tune does not fit on a sixteen gigabyte R T X fifty eighty, or even an eighty gigabyte H one hundred, without the techniques below. Activations come on top and scale with batch times sequence length, which is what gradient checkpointing attacks: store only each layer's inputs and recompute the rest during the backward pass, trading about thirty percent more compute for an order of magnitude less activation memory.

Which brings us to lora, low rank adaptation, by Hu and colleagues at Microsoft in twenty twenty one. The empirical observation is that the weight update a fine tune produces has low intrinsic rank. So freeze W and learn a low rank bypass: the output becomes W times x plus alpha over r times B times A times x, where A is r by d in, B is d out by r, and r is small. The trainable parameters per adapted matrix are r times d in plus d out. Worked example: Llama three eight B at hidden size four thousand ninety six, adapting the query, key, value, and output projections in all thirty two layers with rank sixteen. Each projection adds sixteen times eight thousand one hundred ninety two, about one hundred thirty one thousand parameters; times four matrices times thirty two layers is about sixteen point eight million trainable parameters, zero point two percent of the model. Optimizer memory drops about five hundred times, and the frozen base can be shared across many adapters. After training, B times A merges into W, so serving pays zero overhead.

Q lora, by Dettmers and colleagues in twenty twenty three, pushes the frozen base below B F sixteen. Weights are stored in N F four, a four bit code whose sixteen levels sit at the quantiles of a normal distribution, matching how trained weights are actually distributed, with double quantization of the per block scales, while the adapters and the compute stay in B F sixteen. The net result is a seven or eight B fine tune in six to ten gigabytes, which is exactly the study plan's R T X fifty eighty exercise. When is lora enough? For style, format, instruction following, and domain adaptation, lora at modest rank typically matches full fine tuning. It falls short when the task needs genuinely new knowledge at scale, continued pretraining or new languages, because the update is no longer low rank.

Post training. The original recipe is R L H F with P P O, from the InstructGPT paper by Ouyang and colleagues at OpenAI in twenty twenty two: train a reward model on human preference pairs, then optimize the policy against it with P P O and a K L penalty to a frozen reference so the policy does not wander into reward hacked gibberish. It built ChatGPT, but it is heavy: four models in memory and sampling inside the training loop. D P O, direct preference optimization by Rafailov and colleagues in twenty twenty three, collapses the reward model and the R L loop into one supervised looking loss on preference pairs. In words: increase the policy's log probability margin of the chosen answer over the rejected answer, measured relative to the reference model, with a temperature beta controlling how far you may drift. It is the R L H F objective solved in closed form under the Bradley Terry preference model.

G R P O, group relative policy optimization from the DeepSeek Math and DeepSeek R one reports, is the workhorse of the reasoning era, built for verifiable rewards. Sample a group of G answers per prompt, score each, and use the group's own mean and standard deviation as the baseline, with no learned value model at all. The advantage of each answer is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group's standard deviation. Push up the tokens of above average answers, push down the below average ones, clip P P O style, and keep a K L to the reference. As the policy improves the group mean rises, so the baseline adapts for free, which is the whole point of skipping the value model.

Axolotl is the plan's chosen post training framework because its configuration surface is a checklist of this chapter. One Y A M L file names the base model, the dataset type, the adapter, lora or Q lora or full, the rank and alpha, four bit loading, gradient checkpointing, flash attention, the Liger plugin, and the F S D P block, every line a section of this chapter. Sample packing deserves a word: instruction datasets are mostly short sequences, and padding every one to four thousand ninety six tokens burns most of each batch on pad tokens. Packing concatenates several examples into each row and masks attention so examples cannot see across boundaries. It is typically a two to four times speedup you did not have to earn. The hands on from step fourteen: fetch the Axolotl examples, take a Llama three eight B Q lora config, train on your fifty eighty, merge the adapter, and serve the merged checkpoint with v L L M while measuring time to first token and time per output token.

 On the systems side, F S D P and DeepSpeed ZeRO shard parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across data parallel ranks. Liger Kernel rewrites R M S norm, rotary embeddings, SwiGLU, and cross entropy as fused Triton kernels. Its flagship, cut cross entropy, never materializes the logits tensor, which at batch eight, sequence four thousand ninety six, and a one hundred twenty eight thousand vocabulary is about eight gigabytes for a single activation.

Finally, knowing whether it worked. Held out loss is cheap and comparable, but it measures the pretraining objective, not usefulness. Benchmarks saturate and leak. L L M as judge best matches whether the model is better to talk to, at the price of judge bias. And contamination is the standing threat: the more a benchmark matters, the more its signal has decayed.

Two misconceptions. Fine tuning does not teach facts reliably; it teaches behavior, and knowledge belongs to pretraining or retrieval. And D P O is not a weaker R L H F; under its assumptions it is the same objective, cheaper. The interview question that covers the chapter is: you have one G P U with twenty four gigabytes and a seven B model, make it follow your company's format. The answer is Q lora with packing and checkpointing, a D P O pass if you have preferences, held out loss for ablations, and a judge for the final call.
