Every other chapter in this notebook is about what to know. This one is about the process that tests it. The industry job search for M L and research roles is a full time job with its own timeline, skill set, preparation method and emotional cost, and the most common way to underprepare is to treat it as a side activity to research. Two people who went through it kept the receipts and wrote down exactly how it went, and this chapter is built from their accounts.

The first is Alisa Liu's Notes on the Industry Job Search, published in June twenty twenty six after a six year N L P P H D at the University of Washington, applying for Research Scientist and member of technical staff roles. The second is Nathan Lambert's Job Hunt as a P H D in A I, M L and R L, from July twenty twenty two, after his Berkeley P H D in reinforcement learning and robotics. Alisa describes the search from the outside as a sorting hat: senior students disappear for months and reappear with their fates decided, and nobody tells you what happened in between. Her central observation is that the process was far more demanding than she imagined, that it has its own rules you learn while playing, and that what she learned now feels like common knowledge, the kind that is only common once someone writes it down.

Both accounts are unusually quantitative, which is what makes them credible data rather than anecdote. Alisa interviewed at eleven companies across fifty seven interviews, with forty six recruiter calls and sixteen post offer chats not counted in that number. Nathan reports plus or minus fifty three one off interviews, forty six networking calls, six virtual onsites, and four offers from thirty companies he contacted, and he published the raw data. The posts were written four years apart for adjacent roles by people with different styles. Where they agree you are looking at a structural feature of the process; where they differ you are looking at the variance to expect. As Nathan puts it, no process will be repeatable, but the themes will continue to unfold.

Start with the shape of the timeline, because both describe the same one. A networking phase turns into an interview phase, recruiter screens fan out into technical rounds, a few convert into onsites, offers cluster near the end, and then comes a separate and surprisingly long post offer phase of team chats and negotiation. Nathan gives dates: he announced he was starting on the twenty sixth of October twenty twenty one, found most companies waiting until twenty twenty two to interview, used the gap for forty six networking calls, which he calls a fantastic way to learn what is out there without the pressure of interviews, and wrote his post with offers in hand in July twenty twenty two. Alisa publishes no calendar dates in the text, but describes being stressed, miserable and not functioning in other parts of her life for several months. The chapter draws both as a two lane timeline animation, Nathan's lane on his real month axis with approximate phase boundaries, Alisa's as a relative sequence with her counts attached. Two things jump out. The fan out: recruiter conversations roughly equal interviews for both authors, so expect as many logistics calls as technical rounds. And the tail: sixteen post offer chats is a whole phase most people never plan for.

On sequencing companies, Alisa states the common wisdom, a few practice companies first and the rest timed so offers land together, then adds three corrections: stamina is finite, so practice interviews can burn you out before the companies you care about; headcount and which teams are actively hiring can matter more than your preparation; and deadlines bend more than they look, except for exploding offers, so find out early how long candidates are given to sign. Nathan adds that rejections always come first and that headcount locks mid cycle are not about you. Getting the first interview is its own pre phase: do good work, collaborate widely, be social at conferences, because sometimes you need someone inside vouching for you, and reach out to people you know or do not know. Nathan is blunt that prestige shaped his search, frames it as a privilege, and suggests that candidates expecting lower response rates spend more effort per contact rather than sending more emails.

Now the interview types, and Alisa's headline finding deserves to be heard twice: technical skills and knowledge are evaluated much more than research experience, though research experience probably gets you the interview in the first place. The C V opens the door; the rounds test fundamentals. M L coding is by far the most common round: implement an architecture, a decoding strategy or a classic M L algorithm in PyTorch, occasionally in numpy only. It measures muscle memory, whether you can write and debug attention, softmax, cross entropy or k means under time pressure without an assistant, and the M L Coding chapter exists for it. General coding is basically LeetCode, which Coding Patterns covers; Nathan calls a few months of LeetCode Premium his best preparation. Technical discussion comes in two modes: an extended design conversation about experiments, which measures how you think, and rapid fire breadth, where Alisa's real examples are ways of encoding positional information, what five D parallelism is, and the difference between P P O and G R P O; the flashcard decks and the Parallelism, Attention and R L Systems chapters are the drill. Research discussion follows the thread of a past project. The job talk is shorter than an academic talk and focused on one direction. Behavioral rounds are textbook questions, and Alisa failed her first by improvising. Some companies run a math round, from puzzles to pen and paper derivations; her math notes exist because of one such interview, and the Interview Math chapter is built from them. Engineering flavored roles add system design and many coding rounds, and Nathan's R L specific rounds asked him to write the Q learning update and to complete and debug a large code skeleton, which he thinks best mirrors real engineering.

Nathan's four role buckets explain why the mix varies: the paper writing research scientist loop that resembles a faculty search, the product focused research loop, the M L engineer loop of fundamentals and many coding rounds, and the robotics engineer loop of systems questions. The chapter turns this into the round mix by role interactive: pick a role and see the expected weighting of round types, clearly labeled as an illustrative synthesis of the two posts, each round linked to the chapter that prepares it. Use it to decide where to spend the next week, and ask the recruiter what to prepare.

Alisa's preparation section is a method rather than a list. There is truly no better use of your time than studying for interviews, she writes. She started by watching all of Stanford's C S three thirty six lectures, which organized scattered concepts into one coherent picture of the field, then deep dived concepts one at a time; this notebook's chapter order is the same move, survey then depth. Her strongest line is about homework one of that course: implementing and debugging a transformer comes up so often that turning it into muscle memory pays off massively. Understanding attention is not the bar; writing it correctly in eight minutes while talking is. Practice with A I assistance completely off, because you underestimate your reliance otherwise. Then the principle that organizes everything: each interview is its own mini cram. Scope it from the description, the company's topics, recruiter hints and reputation, then load the relevant knowledge fresh, like a class you never attended with three days before the midterm. Sleep beats cramming: her first technical interview came after two hours of sleep spent cramming inference details, none of which came up, and she lost ten minutes to an off by one error because her gears were barely turning. Record notes after every interview, because questions repeat. Nathan adds: ask the recruiter what to prepare, say no to companies that will not communicate a schedule, and write a research agenda with timelines, because his most common questions were what you want to work on when you show up and what you would do with unlimited compute. Alisa also found the studying had enormous side benefits, making her more confident in research conversations and more effective at her own project; preparation done this way compounds into research. The chapter's forty eight hour mini cram planner operationalizes her method: choose the round type and company flavor, and it sequences the right chapters, decks and drills into six blocks, always ending with an empty block before the interview, for sleep, on purpose.

The non technical rounds are the ones people underprepare because they feel like conversation. Alisa's story bank: before any interview, enumerate memorable stories from your P H D or work, map each onto the common question families, conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, disagreement with a senior person, and rehearse until you retrieve the right anecdote instantly, so you never hear that you did not answer the question. For research discussions, articulate why you chose your problems, the opinions you formed, and the directions you find promising, then tailor the pitch per role because tired interviewers need the right keywords. For the job talk, Nathan says it is not about technical content but about conveying a vision and a story, and the more feedback he got, the fewer plots he kept. And prepare questions that reveal how the team actually works.

Then the offers, and Alisa was shocked that the work is not nearly done when they arrive. Initial offers leave room by design; recruiters told her they did not expect her to take the first one, so not negotiating is doing yourself a disservice. A few weeks of energy here can literally equal years of work at the initial number. You are outmatched on market knowledge, so lean on friends for data points and recruiter know how. Script every call: write down what you will and will not share, with lines you can recite, and anticipate their arguments. The page gives an illustrative scripted counter in exactly that shape, with a reason the recruiter can carry upstairs, a disclosure boundary decided beforehand, a calibrated number and a deadline you control. Nathan's four offers show the other half of the decision: all were good technical matches, so it came down to people and life. He declines to share numbers and credits a negotiation coach.

A word of perspective. Nathan is candid that his search would have been very different without a top school address, that A I is a community driven by prestige, and that even a similar candidate six months later sees a different process, so read both posts as themes, not scripts. Alisa's closing words are about managing the emotions of being on the market, the comparison with peers and a decision space where small choices have an outsized impact; she writes so that readers in the same place know they are not alone, and notes she did her best work when she was having fun chasing the questions her mind would not lay to rest. Nathan's view from the other side is the counterweight: having a job is really amazing, and most companies respect your life and time. The search is a season, not a state.
