Here is the uncomfortable truth about research engineer and member of technical staff interviews. The most common technical round is not a LeetCode problem and it is not a whiteboard chat about scaling laws. It is this: open a blank file, implement a piece of a transformer, and then debug it with me watching. Alisa Liu, after fifty-seven interviews across eleven companies, reported that M L coding was by far the most common interview type, that C S three thirty six assignment one was, in her word, crucial, and that she practiced with A I assistance completely off because you underestimate how much you lean on it until you take it away. Nathan Lambert described the same thing from the interviewer's chair. The interviewer wants to watch you build something you have built before, so the signal is fluency and debugging, not whether you happen to remember a formula. This is a muscle memory skill. This chapter is the drill.

What is actually graded, roughly in order. First, correctness with shapes. You should be able to say the shape of every tensor before you create it. B by T by d model coming in. B by heads by T by d k after the head split. B by heads by T by T for the scores. Interviewers listen for that narration. Second, debugging fluency. Something will break, and often they break it on purpose. Can you bisect with a tiny input, print shapes, and name the bug in under two minutes? Third, explaining as you go. Why pre-norm, why the one over root d k, why upcast R M S norm to float thirty-two, why Adam W decouples weight decay. One sentence each, while you keep typing.

Here is the forty-five minute blank-file protocol. Set a timer. Open an empty file with only torch imported. Pick one target from the assignment. Write it, run the test, and when a test fails, resist reading the reference. Print shapes, build a two-token example by hand, and find it. Repeat the same target on three separate days before moving on, because muscle memory means the third attempt takes a third of the time of the first. Finish every session by explaining one component aloud in sixty seconds, as if to an interviewer. C S three thirty six wires your code to its tests through the adapters file, so every piece below has a test that tells you whether you are right.

Now the build, in the assignment's order. Piece one, the linear layer. No bias, since modern language models drop them. Store the weight as d out by d in, so the forward is x times the transpose of W. Initialize with a truncated normal of standard deviation root two over d in plus d out, truncated at three sigma. The classic bug is storing the weight the other way around and forgetting the transpose. When d in equals d out the matmul still runs and silently produces garbage, so always write the shape in a comment. Piece two, the embedding. A vocab by d model table, truncated normal with standard deviation one, indexed by token ids.

Piece three, R M S norm. Compute the mean of x squared over the last dimension, add epsilon, take the square root, divide x by it, and multiply by a learned gain. Upcast to float thirty-two before squaring, because in b float sixteen the sum of squares of a four-thousand-wide vector loses precision badly, and cast back at the end. No mean subtraction, no bias. Piece four, SiLU and swiglu. SiLU is x times sigmoid of x. Swiglu gates one up-projection with the SiLU of another and projects down: W two applied to SiLU of W one x, elementwise times W three x. Because there are three matrices instead of two, d f f is about eight thirds of d model, rounded to a multiple of sixty-four, so the parameter count matches a classic four-times M L P. The classic bug is swapping which projection gets the SiLU. The reference puts it on w one; put it on w three and every number is wrong while every shape is right.

Piece five, rope. Rotary position embedding rotates each consecutive pair of dimensions of a query or key by an angle equal to the position times theta to the power of minus two i over d k. Precompute the cosine and sine tables once for the maximum sequence length, index them by token positions, and apply to Q and K only, per head, after the head split. Two classic bugs: pairing the first half of the dimensions with the second half, the rotate-half convention, when the reference pairs adjacent dimensions. Both are valid ropes, but only one matches the snapshot. And applying rope to V, which has no positional meaning.

Piece six, softmax. Subtract the row maximum, exponentiate, divide by the sum. The max subtraction must be automatic: e to the thousand is infinity in every float type, and softmax is shift invariant so the answer is unchanged. Piece seven, scaled dot product attention. Scores are Q times K transposed over root d k. The mask is boolean, true where attention is allowed. Fill the disallowed positions with negative infinity before the softmax so they get exactly zero weight, then multiply by V. Write everything in terms of the last two dimensions and the batch and head dimensions fall through for free, which is exactly what the four-dimensional test checks.

Piece eight, causal multi-head self-attention, with and without rope. Project to Q, K, and V with one d model by d model matrix each. Split heads by viewing as B by T by heads by d k and then transposing dimensions one and two, never by viewing directly as B by heads by T by d k, which interleaves tokens and heads. Apply rope to Q and K per head. Build the causal mask with tril, attend, transpose back, call contiguous, then view to B by T by d model, and project out. The bugs here are the famous ones: view after transpose without contiguous, which either raises or scrambles heads if you patch it with reshape, and rope applied before the head split so its last dimension is d model instead of d k.

Piece nine, the transformer block, pre-norm: x plus attention of R M S norm of x, then that plus the feed-forward of R M S norm of that. State dict keys matter for the test, so name your submodules to match the reference. Piece ten, the full language model: embedding, the stack of blocks, a final norm, and an untied output projection to the vocabulary. The truncated-input test checks that a shorter prefix gives identical logits for the shared positions, which is exactly what causal masking plus position-indexed rope guarantees, and exactly what breaks if position t can ever see position t plus one. Say whether you tie the embeddings; G P T two did, the reference and Llama do not.

Piece eleven, cross entropy. Shift the logits by their maximum, compute log softmax as logits minus the log of the sum of the exponentials, in one expression, and gather the target's log probability. Never compute the log of a softmax in two steps; the probabilities underflow to zero and the log becomes negative infinity. For a B by T by V tensor, flatten to B T by V first.

Piece twelve, Adam W. Keep a first moment m and a second moment v per parameter. m becomes beta one times m plus one minus beta one times the gradient. v becomes beta two times v plus one minus beta two times the gradient squared. Fold the bias correction into the step size: learning rate times root of one minus beta two to the t, over one minus beta one to the t. Update the parameter by minus that step times m over the square root of v plus epsilon. Then, separately, multiply the parameter by one minus learning rate times weight decay. That last line is the W in Adam W: decay applied to the weights directly, with the plain learning rate, not the bias-corrected one. Classic bugs: using the corrected step for the decay term, adding epsilon before the square root, and in a real run, decaying R M S norm gains and biases, which you avoid with parameter groups.

Piece thirteen, the cosine schedule with warmup: linear from zero to the maximum over the warmup iterations, then max plus half of one plus cosine of pi times progress down to the minimum, then hold. The test uses seven warmup iterations, a twenty-one iteration cycle, and a minimum of ten percent, and the off-by-one that bites is treating the boundary iteration as warmup instead of the start of the cosine. Piece fourteen, gradient clipping by global norm: square root of the sum over all parameters of the squared gradient norms, and if it exceeds the maximum, scale every gradient by the maximum over that total plus epsilon, in place. Clip the global norm, not each parameter's norm.

Piece fifteen, get batch. Sample B random start offsets into the token memmap with an exclusive upper bound of length minus context length, take the inputs from each offset, and take the targets shifted by exactly one. The whole language modeling objective lives in that plus one. Piece sixteen, checkpoints: save the model state dict, the optimizer state dict, and the iteration; load with map location on C P U.

Piece seventeen, byte pair encoding training. Start from the two hundred fifty-six byte values. Split the text on special tokens so merges never cross them, then pre-tokenize with the G P T two regular expression so merges never cross words or punctuation. Count each pre-token as a tuple of byte tokens, then loop: count adjacent pairs, take the most frequent, breaking ties toward the lexicographically greater pair, record the merge, rewrite the words, and grow the vocabulary until it reaches the target size. The speed test gives you one and a half seconds, so after the naive version works, cache pair counts and update them incrementally. Piece eighteen, the tokenizer. Encode by pre-tokenizing, applying merges in training order, with special tokens matched longest first and bypassing merging. Encode iterable must stream within a one megabyte memory budget. Decode must concatenate all the bytes first and decode once with errors set to replace, because a multi-byte U T F eight character can be split across tokens, and decoding token by token produces replacement characters. The round-trip unicode test exists for exactly that bug. Finish with generation: temperature divides the logits, top p keeps the smallest prefix of the sorted probabilities with mass at least p, renormalize, sample, append, repeat.

Some interviews skip PyTorch and ask for the backward pass in plain numpy, and C S two thirty one n gives the recipe. Break the function into a graph of simple gates, run forward and cache every intermediate, then walk backward multiplying each incoming gradient by the gate's local gradient. For a two-layer M L P with ReLU and softmax cross entropy, the backward pass is six lines: the gradient at the logits is the softmax minus the one-hot labels, divided by the batch size; the gradient of the second weight is the hidden activations transposed times that; the gradient flowing to the hidden layer is that times the second weight transposed; zero it wherever the pre-activation was negative, which is the ReLU gate; and the gradient of the first weight is the input transposed times the result. Then gradient check: nudge one weight by ten to the minus five in each direction, difference the losses, and compare to your analytic gradient. The chapter's computational graph animation steps the classic x plus y times z example forward and then backward, and that is the picture to hold.

Classic M L from scratch comes up in both M L coding and traditional M L screens. K means: assign each point to the nearest centroid, recompute centroids as means, repeat; order n k d per iteration. Logistic regression by gradient descent: the gradient is the input transposed times sigmoid of the logits minus the labels, over n. K nearest neighbors: compute distances, take the k smallest, vote. A decision stump: scan thresholds for the best split by Gini or information gain. P C A via S V D: center the data, take the singular value decomposition, project onto the top components. Each should be a few minutes of typing with the complexity stated.

Decoding strategies, when asked. Greedy is the argmax every step; deterministic and repetitive. Temperature divides the logits before the softmax; as it goes to zero you get greedy, above one it flattens. Top k keeps the k largest and renormalizes. Top p, the practical default, keeps the smallest set whose cumulative mass reaches p. Beam search keeps the B best partial sequences by total log probability and length-normalizes; it is for translation and summarization, rarely for chat. A repetition penalty divides or subtracts the logits of tokens already generated to curb loops in small models.

Now the twelve bugs interviewers love, with the sixty-second diagnostic for each. A mask with the wrong dtype or broadcast: print its dtype and shape; masked fill needs a boolean, and a T by T mask must broadcast against B by heads by T by T. A missing one over root d k: attention rows saturate to one-hot, so check the entropy of a row on random input; it should be near log T, not near zero. Softmax over the wrong dimension: the rows must sum to one along the last dimension. Rope applied before the head split: every rope test fails by a little, not a lot, so assert rope's last dimension is d k. The rope pairing convention: check position zero is the identity, then do position one on a four-dimensional vector by hand. R M S norm in b float sixteen: occasional NaNs in long runs, so look for the upcast. Weight decay on norms and biases: gains drift toward zero, so inspect the parameter groups. An off-by-one in the causal mask: tril including the diagonal is correct; diagonal equals minus one is the bug, and if the token cannot see itself the loss sticks near log of the vocabulary size. View on a non-contiguous tensor: after any transpose call contiguous before view, and know that reshape hides the problem rather than fixing it. Log of softmax: infinite loss on confident predictions; use one log-sum-exp expression. Labels not shifted: the loss drops to zero instantly because the model is copying its input. And the tokenizer decoding per token: replacement characters on non-A S C I I text; join the bytes first.

Running the real thing. A small model on TinyStories, four layers, d model five hundred twelve, sixteen heads, context two hundred fifty-six, a ten-thousand-token vocabulary, Adam W at one e minus three with a thousand warmup steps and a cosine down to one e minus four, starts with a validation loss near the log of ten thousand, about nine point two, and falls to about one point four within a few thousand steps, at which point its samples read like grammatical children's stories. If the loss plateaus near nine, your labels or your mask are wrong. If it drops to near zero, your labels are not shifted.
