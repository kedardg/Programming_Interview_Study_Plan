A CPU is built to make one thing finish as fast as possible. A GPU is built to make a million things finish at once, and it is willing to make each one slower to get there. Almost everything confusing about GPU programming, warps, occupancy, coalescing, the roofline, follows from that one design choice. This chapter is the hardware foundation for steps five through eight of the study plan.

Start with latency machines versus throughput machines. A CPU spends most of its silicon on making one instruction stream finish soon: big caches, branch predictors, out of order execution, prefetchers. A GPU strips nearly all of that out, spends the silicon on thousands of simple arithmetic lanes, and hides every stall, a memory access, a dependency, by instantly switching to another thread that is ready. The quantitative version is Little's law: concurrency equals latency times throughput. An H one hundred's H B M delivers about three point three five terabytes per second at roughly five hundred nanoseconds of latency, so to keep the memory system saturated the chip must have about one point seven megabytes of memory requests in flight at every instant. No single thread can do that. Tens of thousands of threads can. Everything in this chapter exists to manufacture and sustain that concurrency.

Now the hardware. A GPU is a grid of streaming multiprocessors, S Ms: one hundred thirty two on an H one hundred, eighty four on your R T X fifty eighty. Each S M contains cooda cores, scalar F P thirty two units, one hundred twenty eight per S M. Tensor cores, small matrix multiply units, four per S M, that compute an entire small matrix product per instruction and deliver eight to sixteen times the flops of the cooda cores for F P sixteen and lower precisions. Registers, two hundred fifty six kilobytes per S M, private per thread, one cycle, the fastest and scarcest memory on the chip. Shared memory, up to two hundred twenty eight kilobytes per S M on an H one hundred, about twenty five cycles, on-chip S R A M that you manage explicitly from a kernel, the staging area for tiling and the S R A M in the FlashAttention paper. L two cache, fifty megabytes, about two hundred cycles. And device memory: eighty gigabytes of H B M three at three point three five terabytes per second on the H one hundred, or sixteen gigabytes of G D D R seven at about zero point nine six terabytes per second on the fifty eighty, at four to six hundred cycles. When a paper says memory bound, it means bound by that last level.

Each level downward gains one hundred to one thousand times the capacity and loses about ten times the bandwidth. The twenty times latency gap between shared memory and H B M is the entire reason tiled kernels exist. Fast kernels move data up the pyramid once and reuse it many times before letting it fall back down.

Next, the execution model, SIMT, single instruction multiple threads. You launch a kernel as a grid of thread blocks. Each block is assigned to one S M and never migrates. Inside the S M, the hardware splits the block into warps of thirty two threads, and the warp, not the thread, is the real unit of execution. One instruction is fetched and issued for all thirty two lanes at once. You write scalar code per thread; the machine runs it thirty two lanes at a time. The sharp edge is warp divergence. If threads in the same warp take different branches, the warp executes both paths serially, masking off the lanes that did not take each path. A fifty fifty branch inside a warp halves throughput. A thirty two way switch can cost thirty two times. Divergence between different warps is free; only divergence inside a warp serializes.

Occupancy and latency hiding. Each S M holds many resident warps, up to sixty four. When a warp stalls on a five hundred cycle load, the scheduler issues from another ready warp on the very next clock. Stalls are hidden, not avoided. Occupancy is the fraction of resident warp slots you actually filled, capped by whichever per-S M resource your block exhausts first: registers per thread, shared memory per block, or threads per block. High occupancy is not a goal in itself. It is spare parallelism the scheduler can use. Memory bound kernels need a lot of it; register-heavy matmuls deliberately run at around twenty five percent occupancy because each warp carries so much independent work in registers.

Memory coalescing. D R A M moves data in transactions of thirty two to one hundred twenty eight bytes, not single words. When the thirty two threads of a warp load four-byte values from consecutive addresses, the hardware coalesces them into as few as one transaction. When they load with a stride, every lane needs its own transaction: the same instructions, up to thirty two times the memory traffic. Coalescing is the first thing to check in any memory bound kernel. In the matmul ladder in the next chapter, it alone is the six and a half times jump between kernel one and kernel two, from a single index swap. The on-chip sibling is shared memory bank conflicts: shared memory has thirty two banks, and if lanes of a warp hit different addresses in the same bank, the accesses serialize. The classic fix is to pad a tile to thirty two by thirty three so column accesses land in different banks.

Now the one chart that predicts the speed limit of every kernel: the roofline, from the paper by Williams, Waterman, and Patterson in 2009. Define arithmetic intensity as flops performed divided by bytes moved to and from device memory. Attainable performance is the minimum of peak flops and arithmetic intensity times memory bandwidth. On log-log axes that is a slanted roof, the bandwidth-limited region, meeting a flat roof, the compute-limited region. The corner, the ridge point, is peak flops divided by bandwidth: about two hundred ninety five flops per byte for an H one hundred with F P sixteen tensor cores, about two hundred thirty for the fifty eighty. A kernel left of the ridge cannot be fixed with more math units; it needs to move fewer bytes, through tiling, fusion, or quantization. A kernel right of the ridge is compute bound and needs tensor cores.

Here is the worked example. Multiplying two N by N F P sixteen matrices costs two N cubed flops and moves at minimum six N squared bytes: read A, read B, write C. So arithmetic intensity is N over three. At N equals four thousand ninety six, that is about one thousand three hundred sixty five flops per byte, far right of the ridge. Big matmuls are compute bound and can approach peak tensor core throughput. Now the counter example, a batch-one decode step in an L L M, which is a matrix-vector product: two flops per weight, two bytes per weight, arithmetic intensity about one. That is three hundred times left of the ridge. An H one hundred decoding at batch one runs at about zero point three percent of its peak flops, and no kernel cleverness changes that. Only batching, which adds flops per byte, or quantization, which removes bytes per flop, moves the point. This single plot explains most of the inference chapter.

Finally, profiling: measure, don't guess. Benchmark honestly in PyTorch: warm up, synchronize, use cooda events, never wall-clock time around asynchronous launches. Use Nsight Systems for the timeline, because many slow kernels are actually launch gaps between kernels. Then Nsight Compute per kernel, read in order: the speed of light section gives percent of peak for compute and memory, and the pair tells you which wall you are on. Low and low means latency bound, not enough parallelism. High memory and low compute means bandwidth bound. Then memory workload analysis for achieved bandwidth and transactions per request, which is coalescing. Then occupancy and launch statistics, and warp stall reasons, where long scoreboard means waiting on memory. Run Compute Sanitizer before believing any speedup. On the fifty eighty, let Nsight Compute read performance counters by running with sudo or setting the restrict profiling kernel parameter to zero.

Common misconceptions and interview questions. More threads is not always better; occupancy is a means, not an end. A kernel at one hundred percent occupancy can be slow because it is bandwidth bound. Shared memory is not a cache; it is memory you must explicitly stage data into. And the question you should be able to answer cold: why is L L M decode bandwidth bound while prefill is compute bound? Answer with arithmetic intensity: decode is a G E M V at intensity near one, prefill is a G E M M at intensity in the hundreds.
