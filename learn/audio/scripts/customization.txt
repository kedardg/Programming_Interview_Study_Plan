Every model customization engagement I have seen fail, failed in the first week, and it failed the same way: the team started with a technique instead of a target. Someone said we want to fine-tune Llama, and three months later nobody could say whether it had worked. This chapter is about the opposite discipline: turning a vague business ask into numbers, climbing only as far up the ladder of techniques as those numbers demand, and proving the result with evaluations the customer's own engineers can reproduce.

Let me start where the chapter starts, with framing. A technical lead's first job is to convert the ask into three numbers that an executive and an engineer both accept. Quality, measured on a held-out set the customer trusts, never a public benchmark alone, because public benchmarks tell you about the base model and not about their problem. Latency, as percentiles, split into time to first token and time per output token, measured at the load they actually expect, because a model that hits the median at batch one can miss the ninety-fifth percentile at batch sixty-four. And cost, in dollars per unit of business work: per resolved ticket, per thousand requests, per accepted pull request. Cost per token is an input to that number, not the answer.

The worked example on the page is a coding assistant for an internal codebase. The vague ask, it should understand our code, becomes pass at one of at least fifty-five percent on a four-hundred-task internal suite built from the last twelve months of merged pull requests with their continuous-integration tests, up from thirty-one percent with the current prompted frontier model. It should feel fast becomes a ninety-fifth percentile time to first token of at most eight hundred milliseconds on eight-thousand-token prompts, and at most twenty-five milliseconds per output token, at forty concurrent developers. It can't cost a fortune becomes at most one point two cents per completion request, all in, about nine thousand dollars a month at projected volume. Notice that each row forces a design decision. The pass-at-one target decides whether prompting could ever suffice. The latency target at forty users bounds the model size and precision. The cost target decides whether a distilled small model is required. The eval bar is the spec.

Two more habits separate leads who succeed. Write down the baseline, what the current system or a prompted frontier model scores on the same suite, because surprisingly often it already clears the bar and the engagement turns out to be a serving problem. And write down the regression budget: which general capabilities must not degrade, and by how much, measured on a small general suite. A model that gains twenty points on the task and quietly loses instruction following is a failure the customer discovers in production.

Now the ladder. Every technique is a rung, ordered by how much it changes the model, how much data and compute it needs, and how badly it can go wrong. The rule is to climb only as high as the eval demands, with each rung justified by a measured gap the rung below could not close. From the bottom: prompting and few-shot, which needs dozens of examples and no training, and fails when the gap is knowledge the model never saw. Retrieval-augmented generation, which needs a corpus and adds retrieval latency, and fails when answers need synthesis across many documents or procedural skill rather than lookup. S F T with lora adapters, one to fifty thousand demonstrations, hours on one node, low regression risk. Full S F T, which only makes sense when you have far more data, because it carries real forgetting risk. Preference optimization with D P O or K T O on five to a hundred thousand pairs, which fails when the preferences are inconsistent. R L with verifiable rewards, which needs prompts plus checkers and very few labels, is rollout heavy, and fails when the reward can be gamed. Distillation into a smaller model, which needs a teacher and unlabeled prompts and delivers the big win, five to twenty times cheaper serving. Continued pre-training on billions of domain tokens, weeks of multi-node compute, high regression risk without replay. And at the top, a domain foundation model trained from scratch, which is almost never the right call.

The page has an interactive decision tool for this: you set how many labeled examples you have, whether the gap is format, knowledge, reasoning, or preferences, whether a verifiable reward exists, how much cheaper or faster than the frontier you must be, how fast the domain changes, and how close the base model already is to the bar, and it lights up the recommended rung in green and the rungs to combine with it in amber, with the reasoning spelled out. It is a heuristic, not an oracle. Its value is that it encodes the reasoning you should be able to say aloud in a design review, and that changing one input at a time shows you which assumption actually moves the recommendation.

Distillation deserves its own treatment because most engagements end with a cost target the frontier model cannot meet. There are three ways to distill, and the choice matters more than the student size. Sequence-level distillation generates teacher outputs for your prompts and runs S F T on them; it is simple and works with an API-only teacher, but it suffers from exposure bias, because the student learns to continue teacher text rather than its own. Logit distillation, from Hinton and colleagues in twenty fifteen, matches the student's next-token distribution to the teacher's with a K L loss at every position; the soft targets carry what Hinton called dark knowledge, the information that a second-best token was plausible, and they usually beat hard labels for the same data, but you need white-box teacher logits and a shared tokenizer. On-policy distillation, from MiniLLM by Gu and colleagues in twenty twenty-three and G K D by Agarwal and colleagues in twenty twenty-four, has the student generate and the teacher score the student's own tokens with a reverse K L, updating the student on the states it actually visits. That single change, scoring the student's text instead of the teacher's, removes exposure bias, and recent reports show it matching R L-trained reasoning quality at a fraction of the compute for mid-size students. When you own the teacher, it is the modern default. The chapter's code shows both losses in words: forward K L spreads the student's mass over everything the teacher finds plausible, mode covering; reverse K L makes the student commit to the teacher's high-probability modes, mode seeking, which is better when the student is much smaller. The sequencing that works in practice is: make one strong model great, with S F T, then preferences, then R L if a verifier exists, then distill it into the size the cost target demands, and re-run the suite on the student, because the student's ceiling is the student's capacity.

Fine-tuning teaches format and behavior; it is bad at teaching knowledge. If the gap is that the model does not know a forty-thousand-page regulatory corpus, S F T on question-answer pairs will overfit the pairs and still hallucinate the regulations. The honest options for knowledge gaps are retrieval first, because it is cheap and always current, and continued pre-training second. Continued pre-training's hazard is catastrophic forgetting, and the fix is replay: mix ten to thirty percent general data into the domain stream and keep the learning rate well below the original peak. The forgetting animation on the page makes the trade visible, with the domain score climbing fastest at zero replay while the general score collapses, and the general score holding within a few points at around twenty to thirty percent replay. Tokenizer extension matters too: adding a few thousand domain tokens, initialized from the mean of their old sub-token embeddings, recovers ten to thirty percent fewer tokens per word in specialized domains, and it must happen before continued pre-training so the new embeddings get trained.

Here is the compute arithmetic, using the six N D rule from the training chapter. Continued pre-training of a seventy-billion-parameter model on two hundred billion domain tokens is six times seventy billion times two hundred billion, about eight point four times ten to the twenty-second flops. An H one hundred at roughly forty percent utilization sustains about four times ten to the fourteenth flops per second, so that is about fifty-eight thousand H one hundred hours: two hundred fifty-six GPUs for about nine and a half days, somewhere between a hundred fifty and two hundred fifty thousand dollars. A lora fine-tune of the same model on fifty thousand examples is about one thousandth of that. And a domain foundation model from scratch is the top rung for a reason: BloombergGPT in twenty twenty-three trained a fifty-billion-parameter model on three hundred sixty-three billion financial tokens plus three hundred forty-five billion general tokens, and within a year general frontier models matched or beat it on finance tasks. The honest bar for building a base from scratch is that the data is enormous and unlike web text, genomics, molecules, time series, or that the tokenization or architecture itself must change.

Data strategy is where a lead earns their keep, because the data decisions carry legal and product consequences. Audit what exists and who owns it, and build a coverage matrix of task type by data source by count. Deduplicate exact copies with hashes and near-duplicates with MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing; Lee and colleagues showed in twenty twenty-two that deduplication consistently improves quality per token, and the page animates the whole MinHash pipeline on six documents, from word shingles to signatures to the band that catches the two near-duplicates without ever comparing the others. Filter for quality with heuristics and then a learned classifier in the style of FineWeb-Edu. Decontaminate by removing any training document that shares long n-grams, thirteen-grams by the GPT-3 convention, with any eval item. Tune the mixture as a hyperparameter validated on the suite, not as taste. And design the data flywheel from month one: production prompts, failures, and corrections are the best training signal you will ever get, but capturing them requires consent, retention, and redaction decisions made with the customer up front.

You will never have enough human demonstrations for the long tail, so synthetic data closes the gap, and since twenty twenty-three nearly every strong open model's post-training mix is majority synthetic. Self-Instruct and Evol-Instruct seed a strong model with a few hundred tasks and have it generate and then evolve instructions to raise difficulty and diversity. Rejection sampling with verifiers, from S T a R by Zelikman and colleagues and the rejection-sampling fine-tuning papers, samples sixteen candidates per prompt, keeps only those a verifier accepts, unit tests for code, a checker for math, a schema validator for structured output, and runs S F T on the survivors. It is the single highest-leverage technique wherever a verifier exists, and it is the S F T-side twin of R L with verifiable rewards. Where no verifier exists, a judge model can filter against a rubric, but only after you calibrate it against a few hundred human labels. The funnel animation on the page shows the pass rate climbing round after round with the filter on; untick the filter and you watch diversity collapse, the model-collapse failure that Shumailov and colleagues documented in Nature in twenty twenty-four.

Evaluation is where engagements are won or lost in review, and the harness should be built before the first fine-tune and treated as production code. For anything with a verifier, use pass at k, and use the unbiased estimator from Chen and colleagues in twenty twenty-one: sample n candidates, count c correct, and compute one minus the number of ways to choose k from n minus c, divided by the number of ways to choose k from n. The page walks one example: ten samples, three correct. For k equals one that is one minus seven tenths, thirty percent, which matches c over n as it must. For k equals five it is one minus twenty-one over two hundred fifty-two, about ninety-one point seven percent, because five tries almost surely include a correct one. For open-ended outputs, a rubric of three to five binary criteria beats a one-to-ten score because binary criteria can be audited.

Judge models, from Zheng and colleagues' M T Bench paper in twenty twenty-three, make open-ended evaluation affordable but have failure modes you must control: position bias, so evaluate both orders; length bias, so report length-controlled win rates; self-preference, so use a judge from a different model family; and rubric drift, so pin the prompt and judge version. Always report the judge's agreement with a human-labeled calibration set, and below roughly eighty percent agreement, the judge is measuring something other than what you care about.

Then significance. Eval noise is dominated by the number of items, and the bootstrap gives you confidence intervals without assumptions. The page's sample-size calculator answers the roadmap question: how many items do I need to detect the improvement I am claiming? A two-point gain on a fifty-five-percent suite needs thousands of items with independent samples, and pairing, running both systems on the same prompts, cuts that several-fold, which is why suites are always paired. Conversely a four-hundred-item suite has roughly a plus or minus five-point interval, so a three-point gain measured on it is noise until replicated. Say that out loud in reviews. Then ship behind an A B test on the business metric with guardrail metrics that halt the rollout, formalize a release gate, and monitor drift.

Two production patterns close the chapter. Multi-adapter serving, from S-LoRA by Sheng and colleagues and Punica by Chen and colleagues, keeps one base model resident and batches requests that use different adapters: the shared base matmul runs once for the batch, each row applies its own low-rank delta through a segmented matmul, and adapter weights are paged in and out of GPU memory like K V blocks. V L L M exposes this with enable-lora and a maximum number of adapters. The animation shows requests tagged by adapter color sharing the grey base computation. And the coding-agent and vertical-model patterns combine everything: continued pre-training on the repository for knowledge, S F T on agent trajectories for format and tool use, R L on the customer's own tests for reliability, distillation to the IDE's latency budget, and prefix-cached serving.

Finally cost and capacity. The model is simple: a replica's hourly price divided by its sustained throughput gives dollars per million tokens for prefill and for decode separately; weight by your input-to-output ratio; divide by requests. The page's calculator defaults to an eight-GPU tensor-parallel replica serving a seventy-billion model at about twenty-four dollars an hour, and it shows decode costing fifteen to twenty times more per token than prefill because decode is bandwidth bound, so output length dominates cost even when inputs are fifteen times longer. Halve the hourly price and the throughput to model a Trainium 2 instance from a benchmark, or raise decode throughput four times to model a distilled thirteen-billion student, and you have the total-cost-of-ownership argument for distillation in one number. Below a few million tokens a day the API almost always wins; above it, a distilled model on reserved hardware wins by three to ten times, if the eval suite says the student clears the bar.

A few misconceptions and the questions a lead gets asked. Why not just R L everything? Because R L discovers behavior while distillation transfers it; if a strong teacher exists, R L from scratch is more compute and more variance for the same result. How do you know the eval is trustworthy? Because it is held out and decontaminated, paired, bootstrapped, and its judge has a stated agreement with humans. Isn't more data always better? Not duplicated, not contaminated, and not synthetic data trained on itself without a filter. And does fine-tuning teach the model our documents? No; fine-tuning teaches format, retrieval or continued pre-training teaches knowledge.
