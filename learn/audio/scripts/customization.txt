Every model customization engagement I have seen fail, failed in the first week, and it failed the same way: the team started with a technique instead of a target. Someone said we want to fine-tune Llama, and three months later nobody could say whether it had worked. This chapter is the opposite discipline: turn the business ask into numbers, climb only as far up the ladder of techniques as those numbers demand, and prove the result with evaluations the customer's own engineers can reproduce.

A technical lead's first job is to convert the ask into three numbers that an executive and an engineer both accept. Quality, measured on a held-out set the customer trusts, never a public benchmark alone, because public benchmarks describe the base model, not their problem. Latency, as percentiles, split into time to first token and time per output token, measured at the load they actually expect, because a model that hits the median at batch one can miss the ninety-fifth percentile at batch sixty-four. And cost, in dollars per unit of business work: per resolved ticket, per thousand requests, per accepted pull request. Cost per token is an input to that number, not the answer.

The chapter's worked example is a coding assistant for an internal codebase. It should understand our code becomes pass at one of at least fifty-five percent on a four-hundred-task internal suite built from the last twelve months of merged pull requests and their tests, up from thirty-one percent with the current prompted frontier model. It should feel fast becomes a ninety-fifth percentile time to first token of at most eight hundred milliseconds on eight-thousand-token prompts and at most twenty-five milliseconds per output token, at forty concurrent developers. It can't cost a fortune becomes at most one point two cents per request, about nine thousand dollars a month. Each row forces a design decision: the pass-at-one target decides whether prompting could ever suffice, the latency target bounds model size and precision, and the cost target decides whether a distilled small model is required. The eval bar is the spec. Two habits go with it: write down the baseline, because surprisingly often the current system already clears the bar and the engagement is really a serving problem, and write down the regression budget, which general capabilities must not degrade and by how much.

Now the ladder. Every technique is a rung ordered by how much it changes the model, how much data and compute it needs, and how badly it can go wrong, and the rule is to climb only as high as the eval demands, each rung justified by a measured gap the rung below could not close. From the bottom: prompting and few-shot; retrieval-augmented generation; S F T with lora adapters on one to fifty thousand demonstrations; full S F T; preference optimization with D P O; R L with verifiable rewards, which needs prompts and checkers rather than labels and fails when the reward can be gamed; distillation into a smaller model, the big win at five to twenty times cheaper serving; continued pre-training on billions of domain tokens; and at the top a domain foundation model from scratch, which is almost never the right call. The page's interactive decision tool takes your labeled-data count, the kind of gap, whether a verifier exists, your cost and latency pressure, how fast the domain changes, and how close the base model already is, and lights the recommended rung in green with the reasoning spelled out. It is a heuristic, not an oracle; its value is that changing one input at a time shows which assumption actually moves the recommendation, which is exactly what to probe in a design review.

Distillation gets its own section because most engagements end with a cost target the frontier model cannot meet. There are three ways to do it, and the choice matters more than the student size. Sequence-level distillation runs S F T on teacher outputs; simple, works with an API-only teacher, but suffers exposure bias because the student learns to continue teacher text rather than its own. Logit distillation, from Hinton and colleagues in twenty fifteen, matches the student's next-token distribution to the teacher's with a K L loss at every position; the soft targets carry what Hinton called dark knowledge, the fact that a second-best token was plausible, and they usually beat hard labels, but you need white-box logits and a shared tokenizer. On-policy distillation, from MiniLLM by Gu and colleagues and G K D by Agarwal and colleagues, has the student generate and the teacher score the student's own tokens with a reverse K L. That one change, scoring the student's text instead of the teacher's, removes exposure bias, and recent reports show it matching R L-trained reasoning quality at a fraction of the compute for mid-size students. When you own the teacher, it is the modern default. The page's two loss functions differ in direction: forward K L spreads the student over everything the teacher finds plausible, reverse K L makes it commit to the teacher's modes, better when the student is much smaller. The sequencing that works is: make one strong model great, then distill it to the size the cost target demands, then re-run the suite on the student, because the student's ceiling is the student's capacity.

Fine-tuning teaches format and behavior; it is bad at teaching knowledge. If the gap is that the model does not know a forty-thousand-page regulatory corpus, S F T on question-answer pairs will overfit the pairs and still hallucinate the regulations. The honest options for knowledge are retrieval first, because it is cheap and always current, and continued pre-training second. Its hazard is catastrophic forgetting, and the fix is replay: mix ten to thirty percent general data into the domain stream with a learning rate well below the original peak. The forgetting animation shows the domain score climbing fastest at zero replay while the general score collapses, and the general score holding within a few points at twenty to thirty percent.

The compute arithmetic uses the six N D rule. Continued pre-training of a seventy-billion-parameter model on two hundred billion domain tokens is six times seventy billion times two hundred billion, about eight point four times ten to the twenty-second flops. An H one hundred at forty percent utilization sustains about four times ten to the fourteenth per second, so that is about fifty-eight thousand H one hundred hours: two hundred fifty-six GPUs for nine and a half days, somewhere between a hundred fifty and two hundred fifty thousand dollars. A lora fine-tune of the same model on fifty thousand examples is about a thousandth of that. And the top rung is a cautionary tale: BloombergGPT trained a fifty-billion model on three hundred sixty-three billion financial tokens plus three hundred forty-five billion general ones in twenty twenty-three, and within a year general frontier models matched or beat it on finance. The honest bar for a base from scratch is data that is enormous and unlike web text, genomics, molecules, time series, or an architecture that must change.

Data strategy is where a lead earns their keep, because data decisions carry legal and product consequences. Audit what exists and who owns it. Deduplicate exact copies with hashes and near-duplicates with MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing; Lee and colleagues showed in twenty twenty-two that deduplication improves quality per token, and the page animates the pipeline on six documents, from word shingles to signatures to the band that catches the two near-duplicates without comparing the rest. Filter for quality with a learned classifier, decontaminate by removing any document that shares thirteen-grams with an eval item, and tune the mixture as a hyperparameter. And design the data flywheel from month one, because production prompts and corrections are the best signal you will ever get, but capturing them needs consent, retention, and redaction decisions made with the customer up front.

You will never have enough human demonstrations for the long tail, so synthetic data closes the gap, and since twenty twenty-three nearly every strong open model's post-training mix is majority synthetic. Self-Instruct and Evol-Instruct seed a strong model with a few hundred tasks and have it generate and evolve instructions. Rejection sampling with verifiers, from S T a R by Zelikman and colleagues and the rejection-sampling fine-tuning papers, samples sixteen candidates per prompt, keeps only those a verifier accepts, tests for code, a checker for math, a schema validator for structured output, and runs S F T on the survivors. It is the single highest-leverage technique wherever a verifier exists and the S F T-side twin of R L with verifiable rewards. Where no verifier exists, a judge model can filter against a rubric, but only after calibration against a few hundred human labels. The funnel animation shows the pass rate climbing round after round; untick the filter and diversity collapses, the model-collapse failure Shumailov and colleagues documented in Nature in twenty twenty-four.

Evaluation is where engagements are won or lost in review, so build the harness before the first fine-tune and treat it as production code. Wherever a verifier exists use pass at k with the unbiased estimator from Chen and colleagues in twenty twenty-one: sample n candidates, count c correct, and compute one minus the ways to choose k from n minus c divided by the ways to choose k from n. The page walks one example: ten samples, three correct. For k equals one that is one minus seven tenths, thirty percent, matching c over n as it must. For k equals five it is one minus twenty-one over two hundred fifty-two, about ninety-one point seven percent, because five tries almost surely include a correct one. For open-ended outputs, three to five binary rubric criteria beat a one-to-ten score because they can be audited. Judge models, from Zheng and colleagues' M T Bench paper, make open-ended evaluation affordable if you control position bias by evaluating both orders, length bias with length-controlled win rates, and self-preference by using a judge from another family, and if you report the judge's agreement with human labels, which below eighty percent means it measures something else.

Then significance, because eval noise is dominated by item count. The bootstrap gives confidence intervals without assumptions, and the page's sample-size calculator answers the roadmap question: a two-point gain on a fifty-five-percent suite needs thousands of items with independent samples, and pairing, running both systems on the same prompts, cuts that several-fold, which is why suites are always paired. A four-hundred-item suite has roughly a plus or minus five-point interval, so a three-point gain on it is noise until replicated. Say that out loud in reviews. Then ship behind an A B test on the business metric with guardrails that halt the rollout, and monitor drift.

Two production patterns close the chapter. Multi-adapter serving, from S-LoRA by Sheng and colleagues and Punica by Chen and colleagues, keeps one base model resident and batches requests that use different adapters: the shared base matmul runs once, each row applies its own low-rank delta through a segmented matmul, and adapters page in and out of GPU memory like K V blocks; V L L M exposes this with enable-lora. And the coding-agent and vertical-model patterns combine everything: continued pre-training for knowledge, S F T on trajectories for format and tool use, R L on the customer's own tests for reliability, distillation to the latency budget, and prefix-cached serving.

Finally cost. A replica's hourly price divided by its sustained throughput gives dollars per million tokens for prefill and decode separately; weight by your input-to-output ratio and divide by requests. The page's calculator defaults to an eight-GPU replica serving a seventy-billion model at about twenty-four dollars an hour, and it shows decode costing fifteen to twenty times more per token than prefill because decode is bandwidth bound, so output length dominates cost. Halve the price and throughput to model a Trainium 2 instance, or raise decode throughput four times to model a distilled thirteen-billion student, and you have the total-cost-of-ownership argument for distillation in one number. Below a few million tokens a day the API almost always wins; above it, a distilled model on reserved hardware wins by three to ten times, if the suite says the student clears the bar.

The questions a lead gets asked. Why not just R L everything? Because R L discovers behavior while distillation transfers it; with a strong teacher available, R L is more compute and more variance for the same result. How do you know the eval is trustworthy? It is held out and decontaminated, paired, bootstrapped, and its judge has a stated agreement with humans. Does fine-tuning teach the model our documents? No; fine-tuning teaches format, retrieval or continued pre-training teaches knowledge.
