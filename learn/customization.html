Model Customization Strategy, Data & Evaluation

The chapter for the person who has to decide: a customer wants a model that is better, faster, or cheaper at their task — which of a dozen techniques do you reach for, what data makes it work, and how do you prove it worked? This is the technical-lead counterpart of the training and inference chapters: less about how the machinery runs, more about choosing the machinery and making the choice defensible.

1. Framing the problem: from a business ask to an eval bar

Customization engagements fail at the start, not the end. The failure mode is beginning with a technique ("we want to fine-tune Llama") instead of a target. A technical lead's first job is to turn an ambiguous ask into three numbers that an executive and an engineer both accept, and to agree on how those numbers will be measured before any model is touched. The three numbers are always the same triad:

  • Quality — a task-specific metric on a held-out set the customer trusts: pass rate on their own test cases, exact-match on their forms, win-rate against the current system as judged by their reviewers. Never a public benchmark alone; public benchmarks tell you about the base model, not about their problem.
  • Latency — percentiles, not averages, split into TTFT and TPOT (see Ch. 11), measured at the load they expect, because a model that meets p50 at batch 1 can miss p95 at batch 64.
  • Cost — dollars per unit of business work (per resolved ticket, per 1K requests, per accepted pull request), which folds in model size, precision, hardware, and utilization. Cost per token is an input to this, not the answer.

Worked example: "we want a coding assistant for our codebase"

Two more habits of leads who succeed: write down the baseline (what the current system or a prompted frontier model scores on the same suite — surprisingly often it already clears the bar, and the engagement becomes a serving problem) and write down the regression budget (what general capabilities must not degrade, and by how much, measured on a small general suite). Customization that gains 20 points on the task and silently loses instruction following is a failure the customer discovers in production.

2. The customization ladder

Every technique is a rung on a ladder ordered by how much it changes the model, how much data and compute it needs, and how much it can go wrong. The discipline is to climb only as high as the eval demands: each rung is justified by a measured gap the rung below could not close. The most common expensive mistake is starting at rung five because it is the interesting one.

3. Distillation: the technique that pays for itself

Most engagements end with a cost target the frontier model cannot meet. Distillation is how you keep the frontier model's behavior on your task while serving something a tenth the size: a teacher (a large model, possibly one you first fine-tuned or RL-trained) supervises a student. There are three ways to do it, and the choice matters more than the student size:

  • Sequence-level distillation (also "hard distillation"): generate teacher outputs for your prompts, SFT the student on them. Simple, works with API-only teachers, and is what most teams mean by distillation. Its weakness is exposure bias — the student learns to continue teacher text, not its own.
  • Logit (soft-label) distillation: match the student's next-token distribution to the teacher's with a KL loss at every position (Hinton et al., 2015). Soft targets carry "dark knowledge" — that return was plausible even where yield was chosen — and typically beat hard labels for the same data. Needs white-box teacher logits and a shared tokenizer (or a vocabulary mapping).
  • On-policy distillation: the student generates, the teacher scores the student's own tokens (per-token log-probs or reverse-KL), and the student is updated toward the teacher on the states it actually visits (MiniLLM, Gu et al. 2023; GKD, Agarwal et al. 2024). This fixes exposure bias, needs far fewer teacher tokens than RL needs rollouts, and in 2025 reports it matched RL-trained reasoning quality at a fraction of the compute for mid-size students. It is the modern default when you own the teacher.

Animation: hard labels vs soft targets

For one token position, the teacher's distribution (blue) over a 12-token slice of the vocabulary vs the one-hot hard label (grey). Raising τ flattens the teacher's distribution and exposes the second- and third-best tokens the student should also learn to rate highly; the student (green) is trained to match the blue bars, not just the tall one. The hard label throws away everything but the argmax.

# Knowledge-distillation losses (PyTorch). logits: [B, T, V] for teacher and student. import torch, torch.nn.functional as F def kd_forward_kl(student_logits, teacher_logits, tau=2.0, mask=None): """Hinton-style: KL(teacher || student) on temperature-softened distributions. Mode-covering: student spreads mass over everything the teacher finds plausible.""" p_t = F.softmax(teacher_logits / tau, dim=-1) # soft targets log_p_s = F.log_softmax(student_logits / tau, dim=-1) kl = (p_t * (torch.log(p_t + 1e-9) - log_p_s)).sum(-1) # [B, T] if mask is not None: kl = kl * mask return kl.sum() / (mask.sum() if mask is not None else kl.numel()) * tau**2 def kd_reverse_kl(student_logits, teacher_logits, tau=1.0, mask=None): """MiniLLM-style: KL(student || teacher). Mode-seeking: student commits to the teacher's high-probability modes instead of smearing over its tail — better for generation quality when the student is much smaller.""" log_p_s = F.log_softmax(student_logits / tau, dim=-1) log_p_t = F.log_softmax(teacher_logits / tau, dim=-1) p_s = log_p_s.exp() kl = (p_s * (log_p_s - log_p_t)).sum(-1) if mask is not None: kl = kl * mask return kl.sum() / (mask.sum() if mask is not None else kl.numel()) # On-policy variant: sample sequences FROM THE STUDENT, run the teacher forward on those # sequences to get teacher_logits at the student's own states, then apply kd_reverse_kl. # That single change (whose text we score) is what removes exposure bias.

When does distillation beat RL for a cost target? When the teacher already does the task well. RL discovers behavior; distillation transfers it. The sequencing that works in practice: make one strong model great (SFT → preference → RL if there is a verifier), then distill it into the size the cost target demands, and re-run the eval suite on the student — the student's ceiling is the student's capacity, and the suite will tell you if you went too small.

4. Continued pre-training and domain foundation models

Fine-tuning teaches format and behavior; it is bad at teaching knowledge. If the eval gap is "the model doesn't know our 40,000-page regulatory corpus", SFT on Q&A pairs will overfit the pairs and still hallucinate the regulations. Two honest options exist for knowledge gaps: RAG (rung two, and it should be tried first because it is cheap and always current) or continued pre-training (CPT) — resuming next-token training of the base model on billions of domain tokens.

CPT's known hazard is catastrophic forgetting: train on medical text only and the model gets worse at code, instruction following, and sometimes at medicine-as-asked-by-users. The fix is replay: mix a slice of general pre-training-like data (10–30% is typical) into the domain stream, and keep the learning rate well below the original peak with a short re-warmup. The animation shows the trade: more replay protects general ability but dilutes domain gain per token.

Animation: forgetting vs replay ratio

Stylized but faithful to what CPT papers report: with 0% replay the domain score (green) climbs fastest while the general score (orange) collapses; around 20–30% replay the general score holds within a few points and the domain score still reaches most of its gain. The right fraction is an empirical knob set by your regression budget — which is why the budget had to be written down in Section 1.

Tokenizer extension. Domain text (code, chemistry, legal citations, non-English languages) often tokenizes badly — more tokens per word, so more compute per document and a shorter effective context. Adding a few thousand domain tokens to the vocabulary and initializing their embeddings from the mean of their old sub-token embeddings is cheap and recovers 10–30% tokens/word in specialized domains; it must be done before CPT so the new embeddings get trained.

# The compute arithmetic (same 6ND rule as training.html), CPT of a 70B model on 200B domain tokens FLOPs ≈ 6 × 70e9 × 200e9 = 8.4e22 H100 at ~40% MFU of 989 TFLOP/s (BF16 dense) ≈ 4.0e14 FLOP/s sustained GPU-hours ≈ 8.4e22 / 4.0e14 / 3600 ≈ 58,000 H100-hours → 256 H100s for ~9.5 days, ~$150–250K at typical cloud prices Compare: LoRA SFT of the same model on 50K examples (~50M tokens) ≈ 0.001× of that.

Domain foundation models from scratch — training a new base on trillions of tokens — are the top rung and almost never the right call. BloombergGPT (2023) trained a 50B model on 363B financial tokens plus 345B general tokens, at a cost that today buys CPT of a far stronger open-weight base several times over; subsequent comparisons found general frontier models matching or beating it on finance tasks within a year. The honest bar for a domain FM is: the domain's data is enormous and unlike web text (genomics, molecules, time series), and the tokenization or architecture itself needs to change. Code models are the one place where "domain FM" and "general FM" have converged: every frontier model is now also a code model, so the customer pattern is CPT + SFT + RL on their codebase, not a new base.

5. Data strategy

Model quality is downstream of data quality in every published ablation, and the technical lead owns the data decisions because they are the ones with legal and product consequences. The pipeline that works:

  1. Audit: what exists, who owns it, what license or consent covers it, what it covers and what it misses relative to the eval suite's task distribution. A coverage matrix (task type × data source × count) is a one-afternoon artifact that prevents month-long surprises.
  2. Deduplicate: exact duplicates with hashes, near-duplicates with MinHash + locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). Near-dupes inflate effective epochs on boilerplate, and — critically — duplicate text that also appears in the eval set is contamination. Deduplication consistently improves downstream quality per token (Lee et al., 2022).
  3. Filter for quality: heuristics (length, symbol ratio, perplexity under a small model) then a learned classifier trained on a few thousand hand-labeled good/bad examples; FineWeb-Edu's "educational value" classifier is the template.
  4. Decontaminate: remove any training document sharing long n-grams (13-grams is the GPT-3 convention) with any eval item. Do it both ways — the public base model may already be contaminated on public benchmarks, which is one more reason to build private suites.
  5. Tune the mixture: domain vs replay vs instruction data, and ordering (curriculum); treat mixture weights as hyperparameters validated on the eval suite, not as taste.

Animation: MinHash + LSH near-duplicate detection

Six documents become shingle sets (word 3-grams), each set is compressed to a short MinHash signature whose matching rows estimate Jaccard similarity, and LSH bands bucket signatures so only candidates sharing a band are compared. Two near-duplicates (docs 2 and 5, light edits of the same paragraph) collide in a band and are flagged; the others never get compared — which is how this scales to billions of documents.

The data flywheel. Production traffic is the best training signal you will ever get: real prompts, real failures, real corrections. Capturing it requires a privacy design decided with the customer up front — consent and retention policies, PII redaction before storage, aggregation rather than raw replay where possible — and a labeling loop that routes low-confidence or thumbs-down interactions to reviewers. Teams that build this in month one are still improving in month twelve; teams that skip it plateau at their initial dataset.

6. Synthetic data

You will never have enough human-written demonstrations for the long tail of a domain. Synthetic data closes the gap, and since 2023 nearly every strong open model's post-training mix is majority synthetic. The methods, from simple to the current default:

  • Self-Instruct / Evol-Instruct (Wang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023): seed a strong model with a few hundred tasks and have it generate instructions and answers; "evolve" them (add constraints, deepen reasoning, broaden) to raise difficulty and diversity.
  • Rejection sampling with verifiers (STaR, Zelikman et al. 2022; RFT): sample k candidate solutions per prompt, keep only those a verifier accepts — unit tests for code, a checker for math, a schema validator for structured outputs — and SFT on the survivors. This is the single highest-leverage technique in domains with verifiers, and it is the SFT-side twin of RL with verifiable rewards (Ch. 15).
  • Model-as-judge filtering where there is no verifier: a strong model scores candidates against a rubric. It works only after you calibrate the judge against a few hundred human labels (agreement rate, bias checks) — an uncalibrated judge silently teaches the student the judge's quirks.
  • Seeded diversity: condition generation on personas, documents, or code files so the outputs cover the distribution rather than the model's favorite phrasing (Persona Hub; "ground every synthetic example in a real artifact" is the robust rule).

Animation: rejection sampling funnel — and the collapse you avoid

Top: each round samples 16 candidates (dots) for a prompt; a verifier keeps the passing ones (green) and discards failures (red); survivors join the SFT set and the model's pass rate (bar) climbs. Bottom: the diversity of the model's outputs across generations. Untick the filter to train on unfiltered self-generated data — the pass rate drifts and the diversity collapses, the model-collapse failure documented when models recursively train on their own outputs (Shumailov et al., 2024).

# Rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT) data pipeline — the shape every verifier-domain project shares. import hashlib, json, subprocess, tempfile, os def run_tests(code: str, tests: str, timeout=10) -> bool: with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as d: open(f"{d}/sol.py", "w").write(code); open(f"{d}/test_sol.py", "w").write(tests) r = subprocess.run(["python", "-m", "pytest", "-q", "-x", "test_sol.py"], cwd=d, capture_output=True, timeout=timeout) # sandbox this for real return r.returncode == 0 def rft_round(prompts, generate, k=16): """generate(prompt, n) -> list[str] from the current policy (vLLM with n=k, temperature ~0.8).""" kept, seen = [], set() for p in prompts: for cand in generate(p["prompt"], k): if not run_tests(cand, p["tests"]): continue # verifier gate h = hashlib.sha1(" ".join(cand.split()).encode()).hexdigest() if h in seen: continue # dedup identical solutions seen.add(h); kept.append({"prompt": p["prompt"], "completion": cand}) return kept # -> SFT (Axolotl dataset), then re-generate with the improved model (STaR loop)

7. Evaluation methodology

Evaluation is where engagements are won or lost in review: a result the customer's engineers cannot reproduce or whose confidence interval you cannot state is not a result. Build the harness before the first fine-tune and treat it as production code.

Offline metrics

Exact match / accuracy for closed-form answers; pass@k for anything with a verifier. pass@k is not "fraction of problems solved in k tries" computed naively (that estimator is biased when you sample more than k). Chen et al. (2021) give the unbiased form: sample n ≥ k candidates per problem, count c correct, and estimate

pass@k = E_problems[ 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k) ] worked example, one problem: n = 10 samples, c = 3 correct, k = 1 1 − C(7,1)/C(10,1) = 1 − 7/10 = 0.30 (matches c/n, as it must for k=1) same problem, k = 5: 1 − C(7,5)/C(10,5) = 1 − 21/252 = 0.917 (five tries almost surely include a correct one)

Rubric scoring for open-ended outputs: a written rubric with 3–5 binary criteria beats a 1–10 "quality" score for both humans and judge models, because binary criteria can be audited.

LLM-as-judge, used carefully

Judge models (Zheng et al., 2023, MT-Bench) make open-ended evaluation affordable, with known failure modes you must control for: position bias (prefers the first answer in pairwise comparisons — randomize or evaluate both orders), length bias (prefers longer — report length-controlled win rates), self-preference (a model family favors its own style — use a judge from a different family than the models under test), and rubric drift (fix the prompt and the judge version for the life of the suite). Pairwise judging is more reliable than pointwise scoring for ranking systems; pointwise is needed for absolute gates. Always report the judge's agreement with a human-labeled calibration set (a few hundred items); below ~80% agreement, the judge is measuring something other than what you care about.

Statistical significance

Eval differences are noisy, and the noise is dominated by the number of items. The bootstrap gives confidence intervals without assumptions: resample the item set with replacement a few thousand times, recompute the metric, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. A 2-point improvement on a 55%-accuracy suite needs on the order of thousands of items to detect reliably with independent samples; pairing (running both systems on the same prompts and counting discordant items) cuts that several-fold, which is why eval suites are always paired. Conversely, a 400-item suite has a ±5-point interval — so a "3-point gain" measured on it is noise until replicated. Say this out loud in reviews.

Online evaluation and release gates

Offline suites predict; production decides. Ship behind an A/B test on the business metric (task completion, accepted suggestions, deflection rate) with guardrail metrics (latency percentiles, refusal rate, safety flags) that halt the rollout automatically. Interleaving — showing both systems' outputs to the same user and recording preference — detects small quality differences with far fewer users than A/B. Formalize a release gate: a model ships only if the task suite improves beyond the CI, no regression-suite metric drops beyond its budget, latency and cost targets hold under load test, and the safety suite passes. Then monitor drift: input distribution shift (prompt-embedding drift, new intents), output quality on a sampled, judged slice, and verifier pass rates where available.

Build this first. import json, hashlib, random, statistics, pathlib CACHE = pathlib.Path(".evalcache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True) def cached_generate(model_id, prompt, generate): key = hashlib.sha1(f"{model_id}\0{prompt}".encode()).hexdigest() f = CACHE / f"{key}.json" if f.exists(): return json.loads(f.read_text())["out"] out = generate(prompt); f.write_text(json.dumps({"out": out})); return out def evaluate(model_id, tasks, generate, score): """tasks: [{'prompt':..., 'ref':...}], score(out, ref) -> float in [0,1]. Returns per-item scores.""" return [score(cached_generate(model_id, t["prompt"], generate), t["ref"]) for t in tasks] def bootstrap_ci(scores, iters=5000, seed=0): rng = random.Random(seed); n = len(scores); means = [] for _ in range(iters): means.append(statistics.fmean(scores[rng.randrange(n)] for _ in range(n))) means.sort(); return statistics.fmean(scores), means[int(.025*iters)], means[int(.975*iters)] def paired_delta_ci(a, b, iters=5000, seed=0): """Paired bootstrap on per-item differences — the right test when both systems saw the same items.""" d = [x - y for x, y in zip(a, b)]; return bootstrap_ci(d, iters, seed) # Usage: a = evaluate("candidate", suite, gen_a, pass_tests); b = evaluate("baseline", suite, gen_b, pass_tests) # mean_gain, lo, hi = paired_delta_ci(a, b) # ship only if lo > 0 and the regression suite holds

Task-specific suites you should know the shape of: SWE-bench (resolve a real GitHub issue so hidden tests pass — the coding-agent standard; its Verified subset fixes broken tasks), τ-bench (agents following policies with tools and simulated users — measures consistency across trials with pass^k), and domain exam sets (bar exams, USMLE-style, internal certification questions) for vertical models. Build your customer's suite in the same shape as the public one closest to their task so results are interpretable to outsiders.

8. Not everything is a decoder: GLiNER and Laya — encoder-side fine-tuning vs decoder fine-tuning

The customization ladder above assumes the model is a generative decoder. For a large class of customer asks — pull the entities, fields, and relations out of documents at volume — that assumption quietly costs you a hundredfold in latency and dollars. GLiNER (Zaratiana, Tomeh, Holat and Charnois, 2023/2024) is the clearest example of the alternative: a bidirectional encoder (a DeBERTa-v3 or mDeBERTa backbone, roughly 50M–300M parameters) that reads the entity-type names as a prompt prefix in front of the text, builds a representation for every candidate span up to a maximum width, embeds each label name into the same space, and scores every span×label pair with a sigmoid. There is no generation: one forward pass yields every entity of every requested type, and because the label names are inputs the model reads, a label it has never seen works zero-shot. The family has grown around that trick — multi-task and bi-encoder GLiNER variants (labels encoded once and cached), GLiClass for zero-shot text classification, GLiREL for relation extraction — while extraction-tuned small decoders such as NuExtract occupy the middle ground: still generative, but trained only to emit JSON that matches a template.

Laya: an encoder that decides instead of extracting

GLiNER answers "which spans are entities of these types?". The other encoder-side question a customer asks at volume is "what should we do with this?" — route the ticket, score the urgency, flag the churn risk, pick the next action. Laya (NandhaKishorM/laya, Apache-2.0) is built for exactly that: a non-autoregressive, multilingual "System 1" decision engine. An encoder backbone — ModernBERT-large (421M parameters, 512-token context) for the English checkpoint, mmBERT-base (322M, 1,024 tokens, extendable to 8,192) for the 100+-language checkpoint, and a ModernBERT-large laya-typed-decisions fine-tune — carries a decision head that answers typed questions over any state (a ticket, an email, a JSON document, an agent's observation) in one forward pass: about 33 ms per question on a T4 and 7.2 ms per question batched (README checkpoints table; BENCHMARKS.md reports 32.8 ms p50). Three question types cover most routing work: choice (pick one of the criteria you name), score (an ordinal rating over named rungs), and noul (the probability that the answer is yes). Nothing is generated, so there is nothing to parse and nothing to hallucinate; outputs are probabilities, calibrated with one temperature per question type (expected calibration error 0.081 after fitting, against 0.246 for the published TypeSafe Jev numbers it is benchmarked against). A Router detects the script and language of the state and sends it to the right checkpoint; max_len=8192 lets the multilingual checkpoint read long documents, with the README's own caveat that accuracy is 16–18 of 20 test requests up to about 4,000 tokens of preceding text and more variable beyond. It ships as a Python package with an HTTP and an MCP server, LangChain/LangGraph, LlamaIndex and CrewAI adapters, ONNX Runtime and a TileLang GPU fast path, and SDKs for TypeScript, Go, Rust, Java and .NET — the shape of a component you drop inside an agent loop (Ch. 22) rather than a model you chat with. In the terms of Chapter 5's System 1 vs System 2 section, it is the deliberately System-1 half: fast, calibrated pattern completion for decisions whose answer space you can name in advance.

# Laya quickstart (README): typed decisions over a state, one forward pass from laya import Router router = Router() # downloads a checkpoint on first use; Router(preload=True) loads all three state = "Hi, we were billed twice for March. Please refund the duplicate today or we will cancel our plan." questions = { "department": {"type": "choice", "instructions": "Which department should handle this?", "criteria": {"billing": "invoices, payments, refunds", "technical": "bugs, outages, system errors", "other": "everything else"}}, "urgency": {"type": "score", "instructions": "How urgent is this?", "criteria": ["not urgent", "soon", "blocking"]}, "churn_risk": {"type": "noul", "instructions": "Does the user threaten to cancel or leave?"}, } result = router.predict(state, questions) # add max_len=8192 for long documents result["answers"]["department"]["choice"] # "billing" result["answers"]["churn_risk"]["noul"] # probability the answer is yes result["routing"]["model"] # "english" | "multilingual" (chosen by script/language)

How it is trained, and why that matters. The decision head is not trained with next-token cross-entropy and not with plain classification either. Laya's recipe, RLCD, is reinforcement learning against strictly proper scoring rules: the reward for a predicted distribution over the answers is its proper score (a log or Brier-type score) against the label, so the reward-maximizing policy is the calibrated truth rather than a confident guess — the same property that makes proper scoring rules the right loss for forecasting. The repo describes the optimizer as a GRPO-style policy gradient on the head (README's Fine-Tuning section) and, in the browser-agent write-up, as "gold-distribution soft targets + noisy-logit policy gradient + soft cross-entropy" (docs/finetune_browser_agent.md). Read it with Chapter 15 in mind: a one-step typed decision is a contextual bandit (Ch. 19's RL ladder), so there is no trajectory, no value bootstrapping and no rollout engine — just a group of sampled answers per state, their proper-score rewards, and a policy-gradient step on a head that answers in one pass. After training, one temperature per type (choice, score, noul) is fitted on held-out items and written into the exported config.

# RLCD, in the shape of the loss (sketch; see notebooks/laya_finetune_typed_decisions_mps.py and the Kaggle notebook) # p = head(state, question) -> distribution over answers; y = gold answer (or gold distribution) # proper scores: log score = log p[y] ; Brier score = -(sum_k (p_k - onehot_y_k)^2) # maximized by p == truth # GRPO-style step: sample a group of answers from p (noisy logits), reward each with the proper score, # advantage = reward - group mean, policy-gradient on log p(answer), plus a soft cross-entropy to the # gold distribution as an anchor; then fit one temperature per question type on held-out items. # Fine-tuning entry points (README "Fine-Tuning"): # notebooks/laya_finetune_typed_decisions_2xT4_kaggle.ipynb # 2xT4 DDP: build dataset -> RLCD -> calibrate -> eval -> push # python notebooks/laya_finetune_typed_decisions_mps.py --epochs 4 --micro-batch 1 --grad-accum 32 # Apple Silicon / CPU # gradient checkpointing on the encoder and the head (model.head_checkpointing = True); 4 epochs over ~30k questions # takes roughly 4-5 hours on 2xT4; --calib-max 400 held-out items fit the (choice, score, noul) temperatures.

The honest numbers. The shipped checkpoints are near chance zero-shot on the repo's typed-decisions benchmark (0.36 and 0.35 accuracy against a 0.318 random baseline, README) — and the fine-tuned laya-typed-decisions checkpoint reaches 0.766 on the same 2,000 decisions, above TypeSafe Jev's published 0.727 and above the 0.735 self-agreement ceiling of the teacher that labelled the data. On public English suites the English checkpoint scores 0.953 on AG News and 0.600 on DAIR Emotion; across all 51 MASSIVE languages the multilingual checkpoint reaches 0.40 macro accuracy on 20-way intent (random 0.05) while the English checkpoint wins only on English (0.82 vs 0.71) — and BENCHMARKS.md is unusually candid that the committed multilingual columns did not reproduce on later code and were re-run on three machines (issue #208) before the table was refreshed. The browser-agent case study (docs/finetune_browser_agent.md) is the best illustration of the whole method on one 16 GB GPU with no paid API: element top-1 among ~45 candidates goes from 0.10 (chance) zero-shot to 0.66, operation accuracy from 0.54 to 0.88–0.89, real-task success from 0% to 62%, at 17–23 ms per step for the 322M backbone; moving the candidate elements out of the state and into the option list mattered more than any data change, and confidence-gated escalation to an 8B/27B LLM made results worse. The README's own summary is the right one: treat Laya as a fast base to specialise, not as a zero-shot decision engine.

Where it fits in an engagement. Support triage and routing, email and phishing classification, guardrails, RAG relevance judgments, moderation, model routing (which model should answer this?), and — the agentic case — picking the next action or target in a loop where a 7B decoder would cost a second per step. The data pattern is the distillation move of this chapter applied to decisions instead of text: a frontier LLM (or a human) labels a few thousand states with typed answers, you fine-tune Laya on them with RLCD, and the decisions run at millisecond latency with calibrated confidence you can threshold for escalation. The browser-agent write-up's reverse-generated goals (pick the answer, have a local LLM write the question a user would ask) are a clean way to manufacture such labels without a teacher that has to solve anything.

The practical consequence: an encoder is fine-tuned the way a classifier is — small learning rate on the backbone, larger on the span and label heads, a span-width cap, negative sampling so the model learns to say no entity here, a decision threshold tuned on a validation set, early stopping on span-F1. A decoder is fine-tuned the way this chapter already described: data engineering, SFT, evals, with the extra burden that the output must parse.

Interactive: span × label matching, zero-shot vs fine-tuned

Every candidate span of width ≤ 3 (arcs) is scored against every label (heat strips below the tokens; the best span per label is outlined). Zero-shot scores are soft because the model only knows the label by its name; the fine-tuned toggle sharpens them toward the annotator's boundary conventions (e.g. "Acme Corp" vs "Acme Corp Ltd", "March 3" vs "3rd"). Move the threshold: lowering it trades precision for recall and the span-F1 against the hidden gold annotation moves with it. Rename a label — "company", "firm", "employer" — and watch the organization scores survive, which is the whole point of reading labels as text.

Recipes

# GLiNER: zero-shot extraction, then fine-tuning (gliner library; flags illustrative) from gliner import GLiNER model = GLiNER.from_pretrained("urchade/gliner_multi-v2.1") # mDeBERTa backbone, ~300M params labels = ["person", "organization", "date", "contract value"] # free-text labels, read as a prompt prefix ents = model.predict_entities(text, labels, threshold=0.5) # one forward pass -> [{"text","label","start","end","score"}] # fine-tuning data: whitespace/wordpiece tokens + token-index spans + label strings example = {"tokenized_text": ["Acme", "Corp", "hired", "Ana", "Ruiz", "on", "March", "3"], "ner": [[0, 1, "organization"], [3, 4, "person"], [6, 7, "date"]]} # [start, end] inclusive token indices # training (the library's train loop / Trainer config — sketch of the knobs that matter) cfg = dict( lr_encoder=1e-5, lr_others=5e-5, # backbone slower than span/label heads max_width=8, # longest span considered, in tokens max_neg_type_ratio=1, # sample negative labels so "no entity" is learned num_steps=3000, batch_size=8, warmup_ratio=0.1, eval_every=200, shuffle_types=True, random_drop=True) # label order/dropout augmentation # train(model, cfg, train_data, eval_data) -> pick the threshold that maximizes span-F1 on eval, # early-stop on eval F1; export; serve on CPU or a small GPU.
# Decoder alternative: Qwen3 / LLaMA SFT for extraction, then constrained decoding at inference # Axolotl (see the YAML anatomy in training.html): instruction -> JSON matching a schema {"messages": [ {"role": "system", "content": "Extract entities as JSON matching the schema."}, {"role": "user", "content": "Schema: {person: [str], organization: [str], date: [str]}\nText: Acme Corp hired Ana Ruiz on March 3."}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "{\"person\": [\"Ana Ruiz\"], \"organization\": [\"Acme Corp\"], \"date\": [\"March 3\"]}"}]} # serve with a JSON-schema grammar so every token is valid (vLLM: response_format / guided_json; see inference.html#structured)

Evaluate them on the same footing. For the encoder, span-level precision, recall, and F1 with exact matching (boundaries and label identical) and a relaxed variant (overlap counts) — the gap between the two tells you whether errors are boundary conventions or real misses. For the decoder, field-level accuracy on the parsed JSON plus the parse-failure rate, and, if you asked for offsets, the same span metrics. Then put latency and cost per 1K documents next to the quality numbers: an encoder on one L4 processes thousands of documents a minute for cents; a 7B decoder emitting 200 tokens per document is seconds and dollars. The customer's SLO decides which table matters more (Section 10).

Decision rule, and the hybrid that usually wins

Pick by task shape first. The span encoder (GLiNER) wins when the schema is fixed or changes slowly, volume is high, latency and cost are constraints, outputs must be auditable (every entity is a literal span with a score), or deployment is on-prem/CPU. The decoder wins when extraction needs reasoning over context, documents are long, values must be normalized or linked (dates to ISO, companies to IDs), the schema is open-ended, or the output is prose. The decision encoder (Laya) wins when the question has nameable answers — route, score, yes/no, pick one of these candidates — and you need millisecond latency, calibrated confidence to threshold on, and many languages; it loses the moment you need a span, a free-text value, or an answer space you cannot enumerate. In practice the strongest pattern is the hybrid stack: GLiNER extracts grounded candidates at scale, Laya makes the typed decisions about them (route, prioritize, flag), and the decoder reasons over, normalizes, writes, and links only what those two hand it — or, in the other direction, a frontier LLM labels a few thousand documents, a human spot-checks them, and GLiNER is fine-tuned on that silver data to run the volume. That second pattern is distillation in all but name: the expensive model's judgment compiled into a model that costs a hundredth as much to run.

9. Production patterns

Multi-adapter serving

Once customization is cheap, every team, tenant, or task wants its own model — and you cannot afford a GPU per LoRA. Multi-adapter serving (S-LoRA, Sheng et al. 2023; Punica, Chen et al. 2023) keeps one base model's weights resident and batches requests that use different adapters together: the shared base matmul runs once for the whole batch, and the small per-request LoRA products (x·A·B with per-row adapter selection — a "segmented gather matmul") run as a custom kernel. Adapter weights are paged in and out of GPU memory like KV blocks. The trade: unmerged adapters add a few percent latency per token versus a merged single model, but one replica serves hundreds of adapters. vLLM exposes this as --enable-lora --max-loras N --max-lora-rank R, with adapters selected per request by name.

Animation: heterogeneous adapters in one batch

Requests arrive tagged with adapter IDs (colors). The decode step runs the shared base weights once over the whole batch (grey), then each row applies only its own low-rank delta (colored) — the segmented matmul. Two requests sharing an adapter share its weights; a cold adapter is paged in from host memory (flash) before its first step.

Coding agents and vertical models as engagement patterns

The two most common customer patterns in 2025–26 combine everything above. A coding agent for an internal codebase: CPT on the repository history and docs (knowledge), SFT on agent trajectories (format and tool use), RL with the customer's own test suite as the verifiable reward (reasoning and reliability — Ch. 15), distillation into a size that meets the IDE latency budget, served with prefix caching because every agent turn re-sends the same context (Ch. 18). A vertical foundation model (finance, legal, health): CPT with replay on the domain corpus, tokenizer extension, SFT on expert-written tasks, preference tuning with domain reviewers, multi-adapter serving for sub-specialties, and an exam-style suite plus a regression suite as the release gate. In both, the eval suite is the deliverable the customer keeps using after you leave.

10. Cost and capacity

Capacity decisions are made from benchmark evidence, so the lead must be able to turn measured tokens/sec into dollars. The model: a replica (one GPU or TP group) achieves some sustained throughput in prefill tokens/sec and decode tokens/sec at the latency targets; divide the replica's hourly price by throughput to get $ per million tokens of each kind; weight by your workload's input:output ratio; divide by requests. Everything else — model size, precision, adapters, batching — enters through the measured throughput.

Defaults sketch an 8×H100 TP=8 replica serving a 70B model (≈ $24/h on-demand). Decode is 15–20× more expensive per token than prefill because it is bandwidth-bound (Ch. 11) — so output length dominates cost even when inputs are 15× longer. Now halve the hourly price and the throughput to model a Trainium2 instance from a benchmark (Ch. 17), or raise decode throughput 4× to model a distilled 13B student: that is the TCO argument for distillation in one number.

The TCO comparison every customer asks for — prompt the frontier API vs fine-tune and self-host a smaller model — comes down to volume and output length: API pricing is linear in tokens with zero fixed cost; self-hosting has a fixed replica floor and a steep volume discount. Below a few million tokens a day the API almost always wins; above it, a distilled model on reserved hardware wins by 3–10× — if the eval suite says the student clears the bar, which is why Sections 3 and 7 come before this one.
