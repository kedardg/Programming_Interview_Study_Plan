LLM Architecture Evolution

Every architectural development since GPT-2 that survived contact with scale: what changed, why it won, and what it costs at inference time. This chapter assumes the transformer block and attention variants; here we track how the whole organism evolved.

1. The dense lineage: GPT-2 → Llama 3 as a sequence of small wins

Strip away scale, and the modern dense LLM differs from GPT-2 (2019) by half a dozen targeted substitutions. None is dramatic alone; together they are the difference between a model that trains unstably at 1B and one that trains cleanly at 400B. The honest way to read this table is as a list of fixes, each motivated by a failure at larger scale:

The meta-lesson: architecture changes get adopted when they fix a training-stability problem or an inference-cost problem — almost never because they raise benchmark scores at small scale. GQA and MLA entered pretraining purely because of the serving math you saw in the KV-cache section.

2. Mixture of Experts: paying for capacity, not per token

A dense model spends every parameter on every token. MoE breaks that coupling: replace the MLP with E expert MLPs and a tiny router that sends each token to its top-k experts. Total parameters grow with E (knowledge capacity); per-token FLOPs grow only with k (compute cost). DeepSeek-V3 is the canonical modern datapoint: 671B total parameters, 37B active per token — GPT-4-class capacity at mid-size compute.

The engineering is all in the routing:

  • Top-k softmax routing: router logits → pick k experts → weight their outputs by the (re-normalized) router probabilities. Mixtral uses top-2 of 8; DeepSeek top-8 of 256 fine-grained experts plus one always-on shared expert for common knowledge, so routed experts can specialize.
  • Load balancing: left alone, routers collapse onto a few favorite experts, starving the rest (dead experts) and overloading devices. Classic fix: an auxiliary balance loss pushing uniform usage — which fights quality. DeepSeek-V3's aux-loss-free balancing instead adjusts a per-expert bias only for routing decisions, leaving gradients clean.
  • The systems bill: experts live on different GPUs, so every MoE layer costs two all-to-alls (dispatch and combine) — the expert-parallelism animation in Chapter 10. Serving MoE well is why DeepEP/EPLB exist, and why the NKI Library ships dedicated MoE kernels for both prefill (CTE) and decode (TKG).

3. State-space models: Mamba and the case against the KV cache

Attention's superpower is also its bill: a cache that grows linearly with context and a decode step that must re-read all of it (Ch. 9). State-space models make the opposite trade: compress the entire past into a fixed-size recurrent state. Decode cost and memory are then constant in sequence length.

Mamba's contribution (over earlier S4-style SSMs) is selectivity: the state-transition and input gates are functions of the current token, so the model can choose what to write into its state and what to forget — recovering some of attention's content-based addressing. Mamba-2 (SSD) then showed the selective scan can be computed with matmul-shaped primitives (semiseparable matrices), making it tensor-core friendly and revealing a formal duality with linear attention. Training parallelism comes from the associative scan — the same parallel-scan primitive you built in the kernels chapter.

The honest scorecard: SSMs win on long-context throughput and memory; pure SSMs lag attention on exact recall ("needle in a haystack", copying, in-context lookup) because a fixed state must lossily compress the past. Which is why the production answer became…

Hybrids. Interleave mostly-SSM (or linear-attention) layers with a few full-attention layers: the SSM layers carry cheap sequence mixing, the sparse attention layers provide exact recall. Jamba, Nemotron-H, Falcon-H1, Bamba, IBM Granite 4, and Kimi-Linear (hybrid with Gated DeltaNet-style linear attention) all follow this recipe — and you already met Gated DeltaNet, Mamba-2 SSD, and linear/selective scans as kernels in the NKI Library's experimental catalog: new architectures become real exactly when someone writes fast kernels for them.

Animation: KV cache vs recurrent state

Both models read the same stream. Top: attention appends every token's K,V — memory grows, and each step re-reads it all. Bottom: the SSM folds each token into a fixed-width state (watch it update in place — older contributions fade). Constant memory, constant step cost; the price is that faded information is gone unless a hybrid's attention layer can still see it.

4. Linear attention and sliding windows: cheaper mixers

Two other families attack the same O(n²)/O(n) costs without abandoning attention entirely:

  • Linear attention replaces softmax(QKᵀ)V with feature-mapped φ(Q)(φ(K)ᵀV), which associativity turns into a running (d×d) state — recurrent at decode, parallel at training. Modern gated variants (GLA, Gated DeltaNet, RWKV's line) add forgetting and delta-rule updates that fix the early versions' quality gap. Mamba-2's duality result means this family and SSMs are near-relatives.
  • Sliding-window attention keeps exact softmax attention but only over the last w tokens: O(n·w), cache capped at w. Alone it loses global context; in a hybrid ratio it's the current mainstream — Gemma 3 runs 5 local layers per 1 global, GPT-OSS alternates banded and dense layers, Mistral popularized w=4K windows. The global layers (often RoPE-less or long-RoPE) carry document-scale information; local layers do the bulk of mixing cheaply.

Pattern to remember for interviews: full attention is becoming a scarce resource spent on a few layers, with cheap mixers (windows, SSMs, linear attention) doing everything else. Serving engines mirror this: a hybrid's cache is a mix of per-layer ring buffers (windows), constant states (SSM), and real paged KV (full layers).

5. Multi-token prediction and architecture-for-inference

DeepSeek-V3 trains an auxiliary MTP head: at each position the model also predicts the token after next, through a small extra transformer block. Training signal densifies (slightly better model), but the real payoff is serving: the MTP head is a built-in draft model for speculative decoding — self-speculation with high acceptance, no separate draft model to deploy. Medusa and EAGLE (Ch. 9) are the bolt-on versions of the same idea.

This is the clearest example of a broader shift: architectures are now co-designed with their inference systems. MLA exists to shrink the cache; fine-grained MoE assumes expert parallelism; window/global ratios are tuned against paged-KV implementations; MTP assumes a speculative scheduler. Reading a model card now means reading its serving plan.

6. Multimodal architectures

Three dominant patterns, in increasing order of integration:

  • Encoder + projector (LLaVA pattern): a pretrained vision encoder (ViT/SigLIP) turns the image into patch embeddings; a small projector (MLP or cross-attention resampler) maps them into the LLM's embedding space as "image tokens"; the LLM is then fine-tuned on interleaved data. Cheap, modular, and still the workhorse — Llama-Vision, Qwen-VL, Pixtral, InternVL all descend from it. Resolution handling (tiling, any-res) decides most of the quality.
  • Early-fusion / native multimodal: one transformer consumes text, image (and audio/video) tokens from the start of pretraining — unified representation, better cross-modal reasoning, much more expensive to train. Gemini-class and GPT-4o-class models, and open models like Gemma 3n with audio (the plan's Axolotl list includes Voxtral for audio).
  • Generation-side: diffusion or autoregressive image decoders bolted to the same trunk — outside this plan's scope, but the serving implication isn't: image tokens are thousands of prefill tokens, making multimodal serving prefill-heavy (Ch. 9's chunked prefill matters even more; see vLLM's multimodal-inputs doc from Step 12).

7. The current frontier, honestly labeled

  • Reasoning-era training (o1/R1 lineage): long chain-of-thought via RL (GRPO, Ch. 11). Architecturally mostly unchanged — the innovation is post-training + test-time compute, and the serving impact is huge: thousands-of-token "thinking" generations shift load decode-ward.
  • Text diffusion LMs: generate by iterative denoising of whole sequences instead of left-to-right AR — parallel token generation, very different serving profile. Real research momentum (and an Axolotl training feature), not yet a production default.
  • Sparse attention at pretraining time: DeepSeek's NSA and sparse indexers (an NKI experimental kernel) train the sparsity pattern instead of retrofitting it.
  • What hasn't changed: tokenizer+embedding → repeated mixer/MLP blocks with residuals and norms → LM head. Every winner since 2019 is still that shape. Bet on components changing, not the shape.

9. 2025–2026 developments, model by model

Beyond the big families above, the last two years added a layer of refinements that now appear in most new releases. Each one below is grounded in specific gallery entries; click through to the gallery for the fact sheets and config files.

Step back and the pattern of Section 1 still holds: cheaper mixers (DeltaNet, sliding windows, sparse indexers) take most layers; exact attention becomes rationed; residual streams and norms get re-engineered for stability; and inference hooks (MTP, KV sharing, index caches) ship inside the architecture. If you can explain those four sentences with two model examples each, you understand the 2026 frontier.
