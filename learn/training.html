LLM Training, Fine-tuning & Post-training

How a pile of text becomes a chat model: the pretraining→SFT→preference→RL pipeline, the memory anatomy of a training step, LoRA and QLoRA, DPO and GRPO, and Axolotl as the practical map. This is the training-side counterpart of the Inference chapter, and the systems ideas cross-link to Parallelism.

1. The pipeline: four kinds of learning, one model

Modern LLMs are not trained once — they pass through stages, each with its own data, objective, and scale. The stages are wildly asymmetric: pretraining consumes >99% of the compute and sets almost all of the model's knowledge; everything after it is steering, cheap by comparison but decisive for behavior.

Animation: the training pipeline

Each stage transforms the same weights. Watch the annotations: data volume falls ~six orders of magnitude from pretraining (trillions of tokens of web text) to RL (thousands of prompts), while the objective shifts from "predict the corpus" to "satisfy a preference or reward". The model that ships is the sum of all four.

2. Pretraining mechanics

The objective could not be simpler: for every position in every sequence, predict the next token. The loss is cross-entropy between the model's distribution and the one-hot actual token, averaged over positions (see Math for why cross-entropy = "surprise in nats"). Everything the model knows is a side effect of pushing this one number down on enough diverse text.

The 6ND rule. Training compute is well-approximated by:

FLOPs ≈ 6 × N (parameters) × D (training tokens)
— 2ND for the forward pass (each param does one multiply-add per token),
— 4ND for the backward pass (gradients w.r.t. activations and weights ≈ 2× forward).

Worked example — Llama-3-70B on 15T tokens:

FLOPs = 6 × 70e9 × 15e12 ≈ 6.3e24 H100 BF16 peak ≈ 989 TFLOP/s; at 40% MFU → ~4.0e14 useful FLOP/s per GPU 6.3e24 / 4.0e14 ≈ 1.6e10 GPU-seconds ≈ 4.4M GPU-hours → ~16,000 H100s for about 11–12 days (ignoring restarts and stragglers)

MFU (model FLOPs utilization) is the honest efficiency metric: achieved model FLOPs over hardware peak. 40–50% is good at scale; the gap is attention's non-matmul work, communication, and stalls — the same roofline logic as the GPU chapter.

Chinchilla vs over-training. Chinchilla (2022) showed that for a fixed compute budget, loss is minimized near D ≈ 20N — a 70B model "wants" ~1.4T tokens. But compute-optimal is not cost-optimal once you count inference: a smaller model trained far past Chinchilla (Llama-3-8B saw ~15T tokens, D/N ≈ 1900) is cheaper to serve forever at slightly higher training cost. Modern frontier models are all heavily over-trained for exactly this reason — a model-economics decision, not a scaling-law violation.

Data and stability. The mixture (web/code/math ratios, dedup, quality filters, curriculum — e.g. upweighting high-quality data late in training) is as guarded as the architecture, and ablations show it matters as much. Operationally, runs this long hit loss spikes: a bad batch or numeric instability blows up the loss, and the standard playbook is to rewind to the last checkpoint, skip or reshuffle the offending data window, sometimes lower the LR, and continue — one reason checkpointing cadence (§7) is a first-class design choice.

3. The memory anatomy of a training step

Inference needs weights + KV cache. Training needs much more, and knowing the breakdown cold is the most practically useful fact in this chapter. With Adam and standard mixed precision, every parameter costs 16 bytes before a single activation is stored:

BF16 weights: 2 B/param ─ what the forward pass reads BF16 gradients: 2 B/param ─ produced by the backward pass FP32 master weights: 4 B/param ─ precise copy the optimizer updates Adam m (momentum): 4 B/param Adam v (variance): 4 B/param ────────────────────────────── total: 16 B/param → 7B model = 112 GB before activations

That is why "just fine-tune a 7B" does not fit on your 16 GB RTX 5080 — or even an 80 GB H100 — without one of the techniques below. Activations (every layer's intermediate outputs, kept for the backward pass) come on top and scale with batch × sequence length, which is what gradient checkpointing attacks: store only layer inputs, recompute the rest during backward, trading ~30% more compute for an order of magnitude less activation memory.

4. LoRA and QLoRA: fine-tuning on a budget

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) starts from an empirical observation: the weight update a fine-tune produces has low intrinsic rank. So instead of updating W, freeze it and learn a low-rank bypass:

h = W·x + (α/r) · B·(A·x) W: d_out×d_in frozen A: r×d_in (init random), B: d_out×r (init zero → bypass starts as identity-of-nothing) trainable params: r × (d_in + d_out) per adapted matrix

Worked example: Llama-3-8B's attention projections at hidden size 4096, adapting q,k,v,o in all 32 layers with r=16: each projection adds 16×(4096+4096) ≈ 131k params; ×4 matrices ×32 layers ≈ 16.8M trainable params — 0.2% of the model. Gradients and Adam states for 17M instead of 8B is the whole trick: optimizer memory drops ~500×, and the frozen base can be served/shared across many adapters.

Animation: the low-rank bypass

The activation x flows through the big frozen W (grey) and, in parallel, through the thin trainable A then B (green); the two outputs add. Drag r: the bypass thickens, the trainable-parameter counter grows — but even r=64 is a sliver of W. After training, B·A can be merged into W (W ← W + (α/r)BA), so serving pays zero overhead.

QLoRA pushes the frozen base below BF16: weights are stored in NF4 (a 4-bit code whose 16 levels are placed at the quantiles of a normal distribution — matching how trained weights are actually distributed; see Quantization), with double quantization of the per-block scales, while adapters and compute stay BF16 (weights are dequantized on the fly per layer). Paged optimizer states spill Adam moments to CPU RAM on memory spikes. Net: a 7–8B fine-tune in ~6–10 GB — this is exactly the plan's RTX 5080 exercise.

When is LoRA enough? For style, format, instruction-following, and domain adaptation, LoRA at modest rank typically matches full fine-tuning. It falls short when the task needs genuinely new knowledge or capability at scale (continued pretraining, new languages) — the update is no longer low-rank. Rules of thumb: raise r (and α ≈ 2r) before concluding LoRA can't do it; adapt all linear layers (not just attention) for harder tasks; remember you can't batch different merged models, but you can batch many unmerged adapters over one base (multi-LoRA serving, e.g. S-LoRA/vLLM).

5. Post-training: RLHF, DPO, GRPO

RLHF with PPO, the original recipe (InstructGPT): train a reward model on human preference pairs, then optimize the policy against it with PPO, with a KL penalty to a frozen reference model so the policy doesn't wander into reward-hacked gibberish. It works — it built ChatGPT — but it is heavy: four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, value), sampling in the training loop, and notorious hyperparameter sensitivity. Honest summary: PPO-RLHF is what you use when you have the infrastructure and need the extra control; most open work replaced it for preferences.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) collapses the reward model and RL loop into one supervised-looking loss on preference pairs (y⁺ chosen, y⁻ rejected):

L = −log σ( β·[ (log π(y⁺|x) − log π_ref(y⁺|x)) − (log π(y⁻|x) − log π_ref(y⁻|x)) ] )

Intuition: increase the policy's log-probability margin of chosen over rejected, measured relative to the reference model, with β controlling how far from the reference you may drift. It is exactly the RLHF objective solved in closed form under the Bradley–Terry preference model — no reward model, no sampling, just pairs and gradient descent. Relatives, one line each: IPO fixes DPO's tendency to over-optimize confident pairs; KTO needs only thumbs-up/down labels, not pairs; ORPO folds preference into SFT with no reference model; SimPO drops the reference too, using length-normalized logprob as the implicit reward.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from DeepSeekMath/R1) is the workhorse of the reasoning era, built for verifiable rewards (unit tests, math answers). The move: sample a group of G answers per prompt, score each, and use the group's own mean (and std) as the baseline — no learned value model at all:

advantage_i = (reward_i − mean(rewards)) / std(rewards) → push up tokens of above-average answers, push down below-average, clip PPO-style, KL to reference

Animation: one GRPO step

One prompt, 8 sampled answers, each scored by a verifier (✓ = reward 1, ✗ = 0, partial credit possible). The dashed line is the group mean — the free baseline. Green bars (above mean) are reinforced, red bars suppressed. Click "Sample new group" repeatedly: as the policy improves, the group mean rises, so the bar to be "above average" rises with it — the baseline adapts for free, which is the whole point of skipping the value model.

6. Axolotl: the practical map

Everything above is a YAML file away. Axolotl is the plan's chosen post-training framework precisely because its config surface is a checklist of this chapter and the systems chapters:

# axolotl config anatomy (annotated) base_model: meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B # §1: start from a pretrained checkpoint datasets: - path: tatsu-lab/alpaca # §1: SFT pairs (or DPO/KTO/GRPO types) type: alpaca sample_packing: true # §6: pack variable-length seqs (below) sequence_len: 4096 adapter: qlora # §4: lora / qlora / (omit = full FT) lora_r: 16 lora_alpha: 32 lora_target_linear: true # adapt all linear layers, not just attn load_in_4bit: true # §4: NF4 frozen base gradient_checkpointing: true # §3: recompute activations flash_attention: true # kernels chapter: IO-aware attention plugins: - axolotl.integrations.liger.LigerPlugin # §7: fused RMSNorm/CE kernels fsdp_config: # §7 + parallelism chapter: shard states ... # (ZeRO-3-style across your GPUs) # rl: dpo | kto | grpo # §5: swap the objective, keep the rig

Sample packing is the quiet 2–4× speedup: instruction datasets are mostly short sequences, and padding every one of them to 4096 burns most of each batch on pad tokens. Packing concatenates multiple examples into each 4096-token row and uses attention masking (or Flash-Attention's varlen mode) so examples can't attend across boundaries.

Animation: padding waste vs sample packing

Eight training examples of realistic (short, varied) lengths. Padded view: each gets its own row to max length — grey is wasted compute. Packed view: the same examples tiled into rows, separator-masked (notched boundaries), with only the final row partially empty. The utilization number is the speedup you don't have to earn.

The plan's hands-on (Step 14): axolotl fetch examples, take a Llama-3-8B QLoRA config, train on your 5080 (~6–10 GB with the settings above), axolotl merge-lora, then serve the merged checkpoint with vLLM and measure TTFT/TPOT — closing the loop with the Inference chapter.

Fused kernels on the training side. Liger Kernel rewrites RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU, and cross-entropy as fused Triton kernels (the Kernels chapter's fusion logic applied to training ops). The flagship is Cut Cross Entropy: the logits tensor (batch × seq × vocab, e.g. 8×4096×128k in BF16 ≈ 8 GB for one activation) is never materialized — the loss and gradient are computed blockwise from the hidden states and the embedding matrix directly. Vocabularies grew 4× in two years; this kernel is why that was affordable.

Long context training splits the sequence dimension across GPUs (Ulysses/ring/context parallelism) because activations at 128k tokens dwarf everything else — mechanics in Parallelism. Checkpointing is the other half of §2's loss-spike playbook: async checkpoints every N minutes, sharded so resume doesn't need the original world size; at thousand-GPU scale, MTBF is hours, and your effective MFU includes restart time.

8. Knowing whether it worked

Three lenses, in rising order of cost and realism. Held-out loss/perplexity is cheap, smooth, and comparable across runs — perfect for ablations, but it measures the pretraining objective, not usefulness. Benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, …) measure capabilities but saturate, leak, and reward format-matching; treat small deltas as noise. LLM-as-judge (MT-Bench, Arena-style pairwise) best matches "is it actually better to talk to", at the price of judge bias — position, verbosity, self-preference — so randomize order and audit samples by hand.

Contamination is the standing threat: benchmark test sets exist in web-scale pretraining data, so a score may measure memorization. Dedup against test sets, use freshly-authored or time-partitioned evals (problems published after the data cutoff), and distrust any result you can't reproduce. The uncomfortable rule of thumb: the more a benchmark matters, the more its signal has decayed.
