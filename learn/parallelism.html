Multi-GPU & Distributed Inference/Training

One GPU is never enough: models outgrow its memory and latency targets outgrow its bandwidth. This chapter covers the collective operations everything is built on, then the four axes of parallelism — tensor, pipeline, data/FSDP, expert — plus sequence parallelism, and how they compose for training and for serving. It is the systems counterpart of Step 15 in the study plan.

1. Why one GPU isn't enough

Two separate walls force distribution:

  • The memory wall. Llama-3-70B in BF16 is ~140 GB of weights before a single token of KV cache — an H100 has 80 GB. Even in FP8 (70 GB) one GPU leaves almost nothing for the cache that batching needs. Training is worse: Adam keeps FP32 master weights plus two moments, ~16 bytes/parameter — 1.1 TB for 70B before activations.
  • The latency wall. Decode speed at batch 1 is bounded by one sweep of the weights through HBM (inference chapter). If 70 GB over 3.35 TB/s ≈ 21 ms/token is too slow, the only fix is to give the token more aggregate bandwidth — i.e., shard the weights across GPUs that read their slices in parallel.

Every technique below is a different answer to "what do we split, and what do we pay in communication?"

2. The collectives everything is built on

Distributed ML is written in a vocabulary of five collective operations, implemented by NCCL on NVIDIA hardware (RCCL on AMD, Gloo on CPU). Know what each moves:

CollectiveEffectBytes on the wire per GPU (data size D, N GPUs)Shows up in
all-reduceevery GPU ends with the elementwise sum of all inputs2·D·(N−1)/N (ring)TP activations, DP gradients
reduce-scattersum, but each GPU keeps only its 1/N shardD·(N−1)/NFSDP/ZeRO gradients
all-gatherevery GPU ends with the concatenation of all shardsD·(N−1)/NFSDP params, sequence parallel
all-to-allGPU i sends its j-th chunk to GPU j (a transpose across devices)D·(N−1)/NMoE token routing, Ulysses
broadcastone GPU's data to allDweight init, checkpoint load

The famous identity: all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather, and the ring algorithm achieves it with each GPU sending only to its neighbor — bandwidth-optimal regardless of N. Watch it happen:

Animation: ring all-reduce on 4 GPUs

Each GPU's vector is split into 4 chunks (columns). Phase 1, reduce-scatter (3 steps): chunks travel clockwise, accumulating partial sums — after step 3, each GPU owns one fully-summed chunk (bright). Phase 2, all-gather (3 steps): the finished chunks travel the ring, replacing stale ones, until everyone has all four sums. Total traffic per GPU: 2·(N−1)/N ≈ 1.5× the data — and only neighbor-to-neighbor links are used, which is why rings love NVLink.

Bandwidth is hierarchical: ~900 GB/s NVLink inside a node (an "NVLink island" of 8 GPUs, or 72 in GB200 NVL72), ~50–100 GB/s InfiniBand/Ethernet between nodes — an order of magnitude cliff. The art of placement is keeping chatty collectives inside the island and only quiet ones crossing it.

3. Tensor parallelism: shard the matrices

Tensor parallelism (Megatron-LM) splits individual weight matrices across GPUs so that every GPU computes a slice of every layer, every token. For a transformer MLP (Z = GeLU(X·A)·B), split A by columns and B by rows:

GPU i: Y_i = GeLU(X · A_i) # column shard: no communication needed, GeLU applies per-shard GPU i: Z_i = Y_i · B_i # row shard: partial sums of the final output all-reduce(Z_1 … Z_N) # one sum makes Z exact on every GPU

The column-then-row pairing is the trick: it defers all communication to a single all-reduce per MLP (and one per attention block — attention is even more natural, since heads are independent and simply dealt out across GPUs, each attending over its own KV shard).

Animation: Megatron MLP on 2 GPUs

X is replicated; A is split by columns (top/bottom halves on GPU 0/1), B by rows. Step through: each GPU computes its half independently until the final partial outputs Z₀ + Z₁ must be all-reduced. Two giant matmuls, one communication. In attention the same picture holds with heads instead of columns.

Costs and limits: TP communicates activations (batch × seq × hidden) twice per layer, every microbatch — the chattiest parallelism there is. That is why TP degree stays within the NVLink island (TP = 8 is the classic choice) and why TP is the tool for the latency wall: TP=4 gives a decode token 4× the aggregate HBM bandwidth, directly cutting TPOT. Beyond the island, the all-reduce over slow links erases the gain.

4. Pipeline parallelism: shard the layers

Pipeline parallelism cuts the model into stages of consecutive layers — GPU 0 holds layers 1–20, GPU 1 holds 21–40, and so on. Communication is tiny (one activation tensor handed to the next stage, point-to-point), so PP crosses nodes happily. The price is the bubble: stages idle while the pipeline fills and drains. Splitting the batch into m microbatches and scheduling one-forward-one-backward (1F1B) keeps memory flat and shrinks the idle fraction to:

bubble fraction = (p − 1) / (m + p − 1) # p stages, m microbatches

Animation: 1F1B pipeline schedule

4 stages (rows) × time (columns). Blue = forward of a microbatch, orange = its backward (2× long), grey = bubble. Drag the slider: more microbatches amortize the same fill/drain cost, and the bubble fraction follows the formula. Interleaved "virtual stages" (each GPU holds several non-contiguous layer chunks) shrink the bubble further by making p effectively smaller per pass — that is Megatron's interleaved schedule.

5. Data parallelism, ZeRO and FSDP: shard the states

Classic data parallelism replicates the whole model on every GPU, feeds each a different slice of the batch, and all-reduces gradients once per step. Compute scales beautifully; memory doesn't — every replica carries the full 16 bytes/parameter of training state. ZeRO's observation: that state is redundant, so shard it and fetch on demand:

StageWhat is shardedMemory per GPU (vs DP)Extra communication
ZeRO-1optimizer states (FP32 master + moments)~4× less statenone beyond DP
ZeRO-2+ gradients~8× lessreduce-scatter instead of all-reduce
ZeRO-3 / FSDP+ parameters themselves~N× lessall-gather each layer's params just-in-time, free after use

Animation: FSDP's gather-compute-free rhythm

4 layers, 4 GPUs, each GPU permanently owns a quarter-shard of every layer (dim bars). To compute layer k, the shards are all-gathered into a full layer (bright), used, and immediately freed — only one full layer lives in memory at a time. Prefetching overlaps the next gather with the current compute. HSDP is the two-level version: FSDP inside each node, plain DP replication across nodes, keeping the heavy gathers on NVLink.

6. Expert parallelism: shard the experts

Mixture-of-Experts layers replace one big MLP with many smaller ones, of which a router activates the top-k per token (DeepSeek-V3: 256 routed experts, 8 active). Since only a fraction of experts fire per token, the natural distribution is one (or a few) experts per GPU — and tokens must physically travel to their experts:

dispatch: all-to-all # each GPU sends each token to the GPU(s) hosting its routed experts compute: every expert runs a dense MLP on the tokens it received combine: all-to-all # results return to the token's home GPU, weighted-summed

Animation: MoE token routing

4 GPUs, one expert each, 4 tokens per GPU colored by which expert the router chose. Step through dispatch (all-to-all scatter), expert compute, and combine (all-to-all gather). Notice the hazard: if the router loves expert 2, GPU 2 drowns while others idle — load balancing is the central MoE systems problem, attacked with auxiliary losses at training time and with EPLB-style expert replication/placement from measured load at serving time. DeepEP is the purpose-built kernel library for these all-to-alls.

7. Sequence and context parallelism

Long contexts blow up activations and KV along the sequence axis, so shard that: each GPU holds a contiguous chunk of the sequence. The problem is attention — every query must see every key. Two solutions:

Ulysses re-shards with all-to-alls: before attention, flip from sequence-sharded to head-sharded (each GPU gets full sequence for a subset of heads, attention is exact and local), then flip back. Cheap while heads ≥ GPUs. Ring attention keeps sequence shards and circulates K/V blocks around a ring, each GPU accumulating partial attention with online-softmax merging as blocks pass by — communication overlaps compute, and context length becomes effectively unbounded (see the ring animation in the attention chapter). Axolotl exposes both (and their combination, USP) for long-context fine-tuning.

8. Composing it all

Training composes all four axes into an N-D grid — e.g. 4096 GPUs = TP 8 (inside nodes) × PP 16 × DP 32, with EP folded into the MoE layers and ZeRO sharding the DP axis. The placement rule of thumb, in order of communication appetite: TP inside the NVLink island, EP and FSDP next, PP and plain DP across nodes.

Inference composes differently, because there is no backward pass and latency bounds everything:

  • TP for latency — shard the weights to multiply decode bandwidth (TPOT ↓).
  • Replicas for throughput — once a TP group meets the latency SLO, scale out with independent copies behind a router; never widen TP further than the SLO requires.
  • EP for MoE — DeepSeek-style wide-EP serving puts experts on many GPUs and feeds them with giant batches.
  • Disaggregation — prefill pools and decode pools with different TP degrees, shipping KV between them (inference chapter §5).

Worked example — Llama-70B, FP8, 4×H100 (TP=4):

weights: 70 GB FP8 / 4 GPUs = 17.5 GB per GPU workspace + misc: ~5 GB per GPU → ~57 GB left for KV cache KV per token (FP8): 2 × 80 layers × 8 kv-heads × 128 dim × 1 B = 160 KB → 40 KB per GPU at TP=4 cache capacity: 57 GB / 40 KB ≈ 1.4 M tokens → ~175 concurrent 8K-context sequences

This is the arithmetic you should be able to do in an interview without notes: weights ÷ TP, subtract workspace, divide what's left by per-token-per-GPU KV. Every term came from this chapter or the KV cache formula.

9. Multi-node reality

Across nodes, topology is destiny: NCCL builds rings and trees out of whatever NVLink, PCIe, and NIC paths exist, and a bad mapping (a TP group spanning two nodes, a ring crossing a slow switch) silently halves throughput — the Multi-node NVLink Tuning Guide in Step 15 is about exactly this. The interconnects themselves are standardizing: UALink as an open NVLink-class scale-up fabric, Ultra Ethernet as the RDMA-style scale-out transport — read both specs as "what has to be true of a network for collectives to be fast."
