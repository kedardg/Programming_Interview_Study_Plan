Deep Learning Fundamentals

From a single neuron to the training dynamics of billion-parameter models: forward and backward passes, the training loop, optimizers, and what changes at scale. The Math chapter's chain rule and gradient descent do all the heavy lifting here.

1. From linear regression to a network

Start with the simplest learned function: linear regression, y = wx + b, fit by minimizing squared error. A neuron is linear regression with many inputs and one twist — a nonlinear activation function applied to the weighted sum: h = σ(w·x + b). The twist is everything. Stack linear layers without nonlinearities and the stack collapses: a composition of linear maps is itself a linear map (that is the "matrices compose" fact from the Math chapter). With nonlinearities between them, stacked layers can bend space, and the universal approximation theorem says even one sufficiently wide hidden layer can approximate any continuous function. Depth is not needed for possibility — it is needed for efficiency: deep networks reuse intermediate features the way programs reuse subroutines, representing in thousands of parameters functions that a shallow net would need exponentially many for.

A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) alternates linear maps and activations: h₁ = σ(W₁x + b₁), h₂ = σ(W₂h₁ + b₂), … Modern transformers still carry this exact block — the "FFN" inside every layer is a 2-layer MLP that holds roughly two-thirds of the model's parameters.

Which activation? The field has converged on a small family. ReLU (max(0, x)) is cheap and avoids the vanishing gradients that killed sigmoid/tanh networks — its gradient is exactly 1 wherever the unit is active — but it can "die" (a unit stuck at 0 gets zero gradient forever). GELU and SiLU/Swish smooth the corner so gradients flow even slightly below zero; they are the defaults in modern LLMs (often in the gated SwiGLU arrangement, which multiplies two projections so the network learns its own per-feature gate).

ReLU's derivative is a clean 0/1 gate; GELU and SiLU are its smooth relatives.

2. The forward and backward pass

Training needs ∂loss/∂w for every weight, and backpropagation computes all of them in one backward sweep, at roughly twice the cost of the forward pass. The algorithm is just the chain rule with bookkeeping: run forward and remember the intermediate activations; then walk backward from the loss, computing each node's gradient from the gradients of the nodes that consumed it. Every layer needs only local knowledge — "given dL/d(my output), produce dL/d(my inputs) and dL/d(my weights)" — which is why autograd frameworks can differentiate arbitrary graphs.

Two facts from this picture reach all the way to systems design later in the track: the stored activations are why training uses so much more memory than inference (and why activation checkpointing trades recompute for memory), and the backward pass's structure — two matmuls per forward matmul — is why training FLOPs ≈ 3× forward FLOPs, the source of the 6ND rule in section 5.

3. The training loop

With gradients in hand, training is a loop: sample a minibatch, forward, loss, backward, optimizer step, repeat. Minibatches (rather than the full dataset) make each step cheap, and their gradient noise is not purely a defect — it regularizes, helping the model find flatter, better-generalizing minima. Batch size is also the main throughput lever on GPUs, which is why the GPU chapter cares so much about it.

The loop's central tension is generalization. Training loss falling is guaranteed (you are optimizing it directly); what you care about is validation loss on held-out data. When the two diverge — train keeps falling, validation turns upward — the model is memorizing rather than learning: overfitting. The classical toolkit:

  • Weight decay — shrink weights toward zero each step; prefers simpler functions.
  • Dropout — randomly zero a fraction of units each forward pass so no unit can depend on a specific partner; at inference, dropout is off. (LLM pretraining mostly skips dropout — with trillions of tokens seen once, memorization is not the binding constraint.)
  • Early stopping — keep the checkpoint where validation loss bottomed out.
  • More data — the regularizer that actually built modern LLMs.

Normalization: why training deep nets is possible at all

As layers train, the distribution of their outputs shifts under the layers above them, destabilizing optimization. Normalization layers re-center and re-scale activations so each layer sees inputs at a predictable scale. BatchNorm normalizes each feature across the minibatch — great for CNNs, but it couples examples in a batch and behaves differently at train and eval time. LayerNorm normalizes across features within each example — batch-independent, so it became the transformer default. RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and just divides by the root-mean-square (one fewer reduction, one fewer parameter): x / RMS(x) · g. It works as well and is cheaper, so Llama-class models use it — and it is simple enough that writing its fused Triton kernel is a Week-6 exercise in the study plan (the Liger RMSNorm notebook).

4. Optimizers

Plain SGD follows the raw minibatch gradient. Its failure mode is the elongated valley from the Math chapter: huge gradients across the valley, tiny ones along it, so the safe learning rate is set by the steep direction and progress along the flat one crawls. Each improvement fixes part of this:

OptimizerUpdate ideaWhat it fixes
SGDw ← w − η g—
+ Momentumv ← βv + g; w ← w − ηvOscillations cancel in v; progress along the valley accumulates
Adammomentum + divide by √(running mean of g²), per weightEach weight gets its own effective step size, so steep and flat directions are rescaled automatically
AdamWAdam, but weight decay applied directly to w, not mixed into gDecay strength no longer entangled with the adaptive rescaling — the LLM default

Adam's price is memory: two FP32 state tensors (m and v) per parameter. With FP32 master weights that is ~16 bytes of optimizer state per parameter — a 70B model needs over a terabyte of optimizer state, which is the single biggest reason training needs the sharding techniques (ZeRO/FSDP) in the Training chapter.

Learning-rate schedules

No single learning rate is right for a whole run. LLM training almost universally uses warmup + cosine decay: ramp linearly from ~0 over the first few hundred/thousand steps (early gradients are huge and poorly scaled — warmup prevents an immediate blow-up, especially with Adam's not-yet-calibrated second moments), then decay smoothly so late training takes fine-grained steps that settle into a good minimum.

Interactive: warmup + cosine schedule

The schedule every Llama-style run uses. Variants you will meet: linear decay, step decay, and WSD (warmup–stable–decay, which holds the plateau and decays only at the end so one long run can be checkpoint-forked into several shorter ones).

5. What changes at scale

Scaling laws are the empirical backbone of modern LLMs: across many orders of magnitude, loss falls as a smooth power law in model size N and training tokens D. The Chinchilla result made it a budgeting rule — for a fixed compute budget, loss is minimized near D ≈ 20 N: a 70B model wants ~1.4T tokens, not the 300B that earlier models used. (Production models now train far past compute-optimal — Llama-3-8B saw 15T tokens — because inference cost rewards over-training a small model: you pay training FLOPs once and serving costs forever.)

The companion rule of thumb prices a run. One token of training costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter — 2 for the forward matmuls, 4 for backward (gradients w.r.t. both activations and weights):

Training FLOPs ≈ 6 · N · D Llama-3-8B: 6 × 8×10⁹ × 15×10¹² ≈ 7.2×10²³ FLOPs On 1,000 H100s at 40% MFU (~400 TFLOP/s each): 7.2e23 / 4e17 ≈ 1.8×10⁶ s ≈ 21 days

That worked example contains the vocabulary of every training-infrastructure conversation: MFU (model FLOPs utilization — achieved useful FLOPs over hardware peak; 35–45% is respectable at scale) and the gap between peak and achieved, which the GPU and Parallelism chapters spend themselves explaining.

Two more realities of large-scale training round out the picture:

  • Instability. Big runs hit loss spikes — sudden jumps from rare bad batches or numerical issues. Defenses: gradient clipping (rescale the global gradient norm to a cap, typically 1.0, so no single step is catastrophic), careful initialization, and skipping/rewinding bad batches.
  • Mixed precision. Compute in BF16 (half the memory and bandwidth, and tensor cores run it at twice FP32 throughput — see Quantization for the formats), but keep FP32 master weights and optimizer state, because a BF16 weight's 8-bit mantissa cannot absorb a tiny gradient update — the add rounds to nothing and learning silently stalls. FP16 needs an extra loss-scaling trick (multiply the loss by ~2¹⁴ so small gradients don't underflow its narrow range); BF16's FP32-sized exponent is exactly why it displaced FP16 for training.
