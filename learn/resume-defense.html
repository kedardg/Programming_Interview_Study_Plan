Defending Your Résumé: every concept behind the bullets, and every question they invite

A résumé bullet is a claim. An interviewer who likes it will spend ten minutes trying to find the seam between what you did and what you wrote. This chapter takes the bullets of a forward-deployed senior data scientist who post-trains open-weight LLMs for a lending and financial-planning domain, and for each one lays out the concepts it rests on, the arithmetic an interviewer can check on a whiteboard, the probing questions with model answers, and the consistency traps that appear when bullets are read side by side. Nothing here names an employer or a product: every bullet is referred to as "the bullet claiming …", which is also how an interviewer will refer to it. Read it with The Interview Journey (the process) and Customization & Evals (the methodology) open beside it.

0. The three-layer answer

Every question about a bullet is answered at three depths, and the interviewer decides how deep to go. Prepare all three for every claim; the failure mode is having only the first.

Layer 1 · the claim

What changed, for whom, by how much, measured how. One sentence, with the unit of the number ("five F1 points on the held-out test split, up from 71.2").

Layer 2 · the mechanism

Why the technique you chose produced that change, and what you compared it against. "SFT on distilled traces taught the format and the derivation steps; DPO on hard negatives fixed the arithmetic sign errors that SFT left."

Layer 3 · the receipts

The numbers an engineer can check: parameter counts, bytes per parameter, tokens, GPU-hours, batch sizes, learning rates, how many pairs, what agreement the labellers reached, what the baseline list converted at.

The chapter is ordered by bullet. Each section ends with a pocket: the three or four numbers to carry into the room, computed below so that you know where they come from rather than memorising them.

1. The bullet claiming +5% on TAT-QA from post-training gpt-oss-20B on 8×A100

This is the bullet an ML interviewer reads first, because it names a public benchmark, a public model, a GPU count, and five techniques (SFT on distilled reasoning traces, LoRA rank sweeps, PPO/DPO, DeepSpeed sharding, vLLM tensor-parallel serving). Each of those five is a door, and the number "5%" is the handle on all of them.

1.1 What TAT-QA actually is

TAT-QA (Zhu et al., ACL 2021) is a question-answering benchmark over hybrid financial-report context: each question comes with a table and the paragraphs that surround it, drawn from real annual reports, and about 16,500 questions across roughly 2,750 contexts. Answers come in four types: a span copied from the table or text, a multi-span, a count, and an arithmetic answer that requires a derivation (sum, difference, ratio, change, average) over cells and numbers in the prose, with a scale (thousands, millions, percent) that must also be predicted. The two metrics are exact match (EM) and a numeracy-aware F1 that tolerates partial multi-span credit and checks both value and scale for numbers. The original TagOp baseline scored in the mid-50s EM; later systems that generate programs or chain-of-thought derivations and execute them, and large generative models with reasoning traces, sit well above that, so an interviewer who knows the benchmark will immediately ask which split (dev is public with labels; the test leaderboard is held out), which metric, and from what starting point.

Trap · "5%" has no unit. Five percentage points (71.2 → 76.2 F1) and five percent relative (71.2 → 74.8) are different claims, and "5%" with no baseline is neither. Fix the bullet so it reads "+5.1 F1 points (71.2 → 76.3) on the TAT-QA dev split, over the same model prompted zero-shot with the same decoding settings" or whatever the true numbers are. If you only have the dev split, say dev; claiming "test" without a leaderboard submission is the kind of thing a careful interviewer will ask you to show.
Trap · what is the comparison? "+5%" over the base model is a different story from "+5%" over the best off-the-shelf competitor, and both are different from "+5%" over the previous production system. Be able to name the baseline in one breath and justify why it is a fair one (same context formatting, same number of shots, same reasoning effort, same max tokens, same scoring script).

1.2 What gpt-oss-20B is, in numbers

gpt-oss-20b is OpenAI's smaller open-weight model (Apache-2.0): a mixture-of-experts transformer with about 21 B total parameters and about 3.6 B active per token, 24 layers, 32 experts per MoE layer with 4 routed per token, grouped-query attention with alternating banded (sliding-window) and full attention, 128k context, and expert weights released in MXFP4 (4-bit microscaling floats with shared block exponents) so the checkpoint is about 12–13 GB on disk. It uses the harmony chat format with separate analysis (reasoning) and final channels and three reasoning-effort levels. The full playbook with memory calculators is in Model Playbooks; the parts that matter for this bullet are:

  • Active vs total parameters. FLOPs per token scale with the 3.6 B active parameters; memory scales with the 21 B total. Training is memory-bound by the whole model and compute-light per token, which is why it is attractive on 8×A100 and why "I trained a 20B model" should be followed by "3.6 B active".
  • Upcast for training. A100s have no FP4 tensor cores. For fine-tuning you dequantize MXFP4 experts to BF16: 21 B × 2 bytes ≈ 42 GB of weights. That one number drives every memory decision below.
  • Where the parameters live. The great majority of parameters are in the expert FFNs; attention and embeddings are a small fraction. A LoRA that targets only attention projections touches little of the model's capacity; one that targets expert projections multiplies adapter count by the number of experts.

1.3 SFT on distilled reasoning traces

Distillation here means sequence-level knowledge distillation: a stronger teacher (a larger model, or the same model at high reasoning effort with many samples) writes step-by-step derivations for the training questions; you keep the traces whose final answer matches the gold label (rejection sampling), optionally filter for length and for derivations that actually reference the right cells, and fine-tune the student with ordinary next-token cross-entropy on the kept traces (the analysis channel followed by the final answer). This is the recipe behind most "reasoning SFT" results since DeepSeek-R1's distilled models, and Customization & Evals covers the loss, the masking of the prompt, and the data-mixture questions.

Questions it invites: how many traces (per question, after filtering), what the acceptance rate of the teacher was (a proxy for how hard the data is), whether you distilled on-policy (student generates, teacher scores) or off-policy (teacher generates, student imitates) and why, whether you masked the prompt tokens from the loss, how long the traces were and whether length correlated with accuracy, and what happened to the arithmetic answers specifically (the type where models fail by sign and scale rather than by retrieval). Have the answer-type breakdown of your +5 points: if most of the gain is on arithmetic, that supports the traces story; if it is on span questions, the gain may be format learning and an interviewer will say so.

1.4 LoRA rank sweeps

A LoRA adapter replaces the update of a weight matrix W (dout × din) with a low-rank product B·A, with A of shape r × din and B of shape dout × r, scaled by α/r. Trainable parameters per adapted matrix are r · (din + dout); a sweep over r ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64, 128} multiplies that by 16× end to end. What a rank sweep usually shows is a plateau: gains from r = 8 to 32, little after, and sometimes degradation at very high rank with a fixed α because the effective learning rate α/r shrinks (hence rsLoRA, which scales by α/√r). The interview questions are about what you held fixed (α, learning rate, target modules, steps, seed) and what you concluded (a rank you can name, with the number that justified it).

Trap · LoRA on a mixture of experts. Which modules did the adapter target? Attention q/k/v/o only is the cheap default; adding expert up/down projections means one adapter per expert per layer (32 × 24 × 2 matrices), and routers are usually left frozen because perturbing them destabilises load balance. If you say "LoRA on all linear layers," expect "including the router?" and have an answer.

1.5 PPO or DPO, and why not GRPO

"PPO/DPO" in a bullet reads as hedging. They are different algorithms with different data requirements, and an interviewer will ask which one produced the number:

PPO (on-policy RL)DPO (offline preference)GRPO / RLVR (the 2025 default for verifiable tasks)
NeedsA reward signal per sample; usually a reward model, or a verifier when the answer is checkablePairs (chosen, rejected) for the same prompt; no reward model at train timeA verifier (exact match / F1 against gold) and a group of samples per prompt

Fit for TAT-QAWorks, but the value model is wasted effort when the reward is a checkerWorks if you can mine good rejected traces (wrong answer, plausible derivation)The natural fit: gold answers make the reward verifiable
Typical question"Why a value model when exact match is the reward?""How did you build the rejected side, and did it drift to long answers?""Why not this?"

A defensible story is: "I ran DPO on pairs mined from the rejection-sampling step (correct trace vs. the highest-likelihood incorrect trace for the same question), which gave most of the gain; I also tried a PPO run with the exact-match reward and a KL penalty, which matched DPO within noise at much higher cost, so I shipped DPO." If you ran only one of the two, the bullet should say that one. The mechanics of all three (clipped objective, GAE, β in DPO, group advantages) are in RL & Robotics and RL Systems; the RLHF bullet below has the worked arithmetic.

1.6 DeepSpeed sharding: the memory arithmetic that decides whether the bullet is consistent

Here is the seam interviewers look for. DeepSpeed ZeRO shards optimizer state (stage 1), gradients (stage 2) and parameters (stage 3) across data-parallel ranks so that a model that does not fit on one GPU can be trained on many. LoRA exists precisely so that you do not need to hold optimizer state for the full model. Mentioning both in the same bullet is not wrong, but you must be able to say which combination you actually ran and why. The calculator below reproduces the arithmetic (details in Parallelism and Training).

Calculator: does it fit? Full fine-tune vs LoRA on N GPUs

Rules: full FT in BF16 with AdamW keeps 2 (weights) + 2 (grads) + 4 (FP32 master) + 8 (two Adam moments) = 16 bytes per parameter; LoRA keeps 2 bytes per frozen parameter plus optimizer state only for the adapters. Activations are approximated as 34·s·b·d bytes per layer with gradient checkpointing off (Korthikanti et al.), and roughly 2·s·b·d with checkpointing on. For an MoE only the routed experts' activations count per token, so the real number is lower than this upper bound.

Reading the default output: full fine-tuning of 21 B parameters needs about 336 GB of states, which does not fit one 80 GB A100 but does fit 8 of them under ZeRO-3 at about 42 GB per GPU before activations. LoRA needs about 42 GB of frozen BF16 weights per GPU, which fits an 80 GB A100 with room for activations and no sharding at all, but does not fit a 40 GB A100, where ZeRO-3 (which shards frozen parameters too) or offloading becomes necessary. So the consistent stories are:

  1. 8×A100-80GB, LoRA, DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 or plain DDP for data parallelism. DeepSpeed is there for the training loop, gradient accumulation and bf16 handling, not because memory forced it. Say "data-parallel across 8 GPUs with DeepSpeed; LoRA meant no parameter sharding was needed."
  2. 8×A100-40GB, LoRA, ZeRO-3. Sharding was required to hold 42 GB of frozen weights. Say that; it is a perfectly good reason and it tells the interviewer you know what ZeRO-3 shards.
  3. Full fine-tune with ZeRO-3, and a LoRA sweep as an ablation. The most expensive story and the one that best justifies "DeepSpeed sharding", but then the bullet should lead with the full fine-tune.
Trap · "8×A100" invites "80 or 40 GB, and what was the per-GPU batch?" Know the memory, the micro-batch, the gradient-accumulation steps, the sequence length, and the wall-clock per epoch. For 3.6 B active parameters at 6·N·D FLOPs per token, 20 M training tokens is ~4.3×1017 FLOPs; at a realistic 40% of 312 TFLOP/s BF16 per A100 that is about an hour on 8 GPUs per epoch, which tells you that the run was memory- and I/O-bound, not compute-bound. If you quote a 12-hour epoch, be ready to explain where the time went (long sequences, MoE dispatch overhead, checkpointing).

1.7 vLLM with tensor parallelism on a model that fits one GPU

Tensor parallelism splits each weight matrix across GPUs and all-reduces the partial results every layer. It is the tool for a model that does not fit on one GPU, or for cutting per-token latency when it does. On A100, vLLM runs gpt-oss-20b through a Triton MXFP4 path or upcast BF16 weights (about 42 GB), so a single 80 GB A100 holds the weights and roughly 30 GB of KV cache. The honest reasons to serve it with TP=2 anyway are: (a) latency, since the decode step is memory-bandwidth-bound and two GPUs read the weights in half the time; (b) KV-cache headroom for long financial-report contexts (128k context × many concurrent requests); (c) 40 GB A100s, where TP=2 is required. The reason not to is throughput per GPU: two replicas with data parallelism usually beat one TP=2 instance on tokens per second per dollar. The arithmetic (per-token KV bytes = 2 · layers · kv_heads · head_dim · bytes) is in Inference and the vLLM command builder in Model Playbooks.

Pocket for bullet 1. TAT-QA: ~16.5k Qs, hybrid table+text, EM and numeracy F1, four answer types. gpt-oss-20b: 21 B total / 3.6 B active, 32 experts top-4, MXFP4 ≈ 13 GB on disk, BF16 ≈ 42 GB. Full FT = 16 B/param → 336 GB → ZeRO-3 across 8×80 GB. LoRA params = r·(din+dout) per matrix. Your baseline, your split, your metric, your rank, and the per-answer-type breakdown of the +5.

2. The bullet claiming a post-training data loop: vLLM baselines across five models, an LLM-as-judge materiality rubric, and synthetic human-verified DPO pairs

This bullet is about methodology, so the questions are about rigour: were the baselines fair, is the judge trustworthy, and what does "human-verified synthetic" mean.

2.1 A fair baseline table

Five models served through vLLM is a reasonable baseline sweep (for example a mix of open models at 8–30 B and the model you intend to fine-tune). What makes it fair is that everything except the model is held fixed, and the interview question is "what did you hold fixed?" The answer is a table you should be able to sketch from memory:

Held fixedWhy it matters
Prompt template, few-shot examples, context truncation ruleTable serialisation alone moves TAT-QA scores by several points
Decoding: temperature, top-p, max new tokens, reasoning effort, stop sequencesGreedy vs sampled, and a 512-token cap on a model that reasons for 800 tokens, both change the number
Scoring script and answer normalisationScale handling ("in millions"), units, rounding; use the official evaluator
Precision and kernel (BF16 vs FP8 vs AWQ) and vLLM versionQuantized serving can cost a point; version changes change sampling defaults
Seed and number of runsReport mean ± std over ≥3 seeds when sampling; a 1-point gap at one seed is noise

Also carry: cost per million tokens and tokens per second per model, because a forward-deployed engineer is asked "why not the biggest model?" and the answer is a latency or cost number, not a preference. See Deployment for the load-testing side.

2.2 LLM-as-judge with a materiality rubric

In accounting, a misstatement is material if it could change the decision of someone relying on the statement. A materiality rubric for a judge model turns that into graded criteria: a wrong sign or wrong scale on a headline figure is a critical error, a rounding difference below a threshold is immaterial, a missing caveat is minor, and so on. The judge is prompted with the question, the gold or reference answer, the candidate answer and the rubric, and asked for a score or a pairwise preference with a rationale. The concepts an interviewer will probe (all in Customization & Evals):

  • Calibration against humans. On a labelled subset, what was the agreement (Cohen's κ, or pairwise accuracy) between the judge and human graders? A judge that agrees with humans 85% of the time is a tool; one you never checked is a liability.
  • Position bias and length bias. Pairwise judges prefer the first answer and the longer answer. Swap order and average; penalise or control length.
  • Self-preference. A judge from the same family as the policy favours its own style. Use a different family, or an ensemble, or anchor to the reference answer.
  • Pointwise vs pairwise. Pointwise scores are easier to aggregate; pairwise is more reliable for close calls. Which did you use for the DPO pairs?
  • Verifiable where possible. For TAT-QA-like questions the final number is checkable; use the checker for the final answer and reserve the judge for the derivation and the explanation.

2.3 "Synthetic, human-verified" preference pairs

Synthetic pairs are generated by a model; human-verified means a person checked them. Both can be true, but the phrase invites "verified how, by whom, how many?" A defensible protocol: generate candidate pairs (chosen = a rejection-sampled correct trace; rejected = an incorrect trace for the same question, or a correct answer with a materially wrong derivation, or a deliberately corrupted scale), have the judge pre-filter, then have domain reviewers confirm a random sample or the full set with a stated inter-annotator agreement, and drop any pair where reviewers disagreed. Know the counts: pairs generated, pairs kept, fraction human-reviewed, agreement. Then the DPO facts: the loss is −log σ(β · [log π(yw)/πref(yw) − log π(yl)/πref(yl)]), β (usually 0.05–0.5) controls how far the policy may move from the reference, DPO is known to drift toward longer outputs unless pairs are length-matched, and a chosen/rejected pair that differs only in the final number teaches arithmetic while one that differs in style teaches style.

Trap · the loop. "Data loop" implies iteration: generate → train → evaluate → mine new failures → generate. Say how many rounds you ran and what changed between rounds (which failure categories shrank). One round is a pipeline, not a loop.
Pocket for bullet 2. Five models, one table of held-fixed settings, judge agreement with humans as a number, counts for pairs generated/kept/reviewed, β, number of loop rounds, and the one failure category that the loop fixed.

3. The bullet claiming 4× deal conversion from a PageRank and Personalized PageRank lead list over news graphs plus CRM data

This is the bullet most likely to be examined by a non-ML interviewer, because "4×" is a business number, and by an ML interviewer, because PageRank is a whiteboard algorithm. Prepare both.

3.1 PageRank, properly

Model a graph of n nodes (companies, people, events, articles) with adjacency A. A random surfer at node i follows a uniformly random out-edge with probability d (the damping factor, classically 0.85) and jumps to a random node with probability 1 − d. The stationary distribution of that walk is the PageRank vector p, which satisfies

p = (1 − d) · v + d · Mᵀ p,   where Mij = 1 / outdeg(i) if i → j, and v is the jump distribution

Global PageRank uses v = uniform (1/n everywhere). Personalized PageRank (PPR) uses v concentrated on a seed set (your existing customers from the CRM, the deals that converted last year, the sectors you cover): the surfer teleports back to the seeds, so p becomes a measure of proximity to the seeds through the graph, which is exactly what a lead list wants. Computation is power iteration: start from p = v, apply the update until ‖pt+1 − pt‖1 falls below a tolerance; convergence is geometric at rate d, so about 50–100 iterations for d = 0.85 at 1e-6. Dangling nodes (no out-edges) leak mass; the standard fix redistributes their mass by v. The matrix is sparse, so each iteration costs O(edges), which is why this scales to millions of nodes without any ML infrastructure.

Animation: power iteration on a small news-and-CRM graph

Node size is current score. Edges are co-mentions in news (grey) and CRM relationships (green). With personalization on, the surfer teleports back to the seed accounts, and the ranking becomes "who is close to our existing book"; with it off, it becomes "who is central in the news", which is a different lead list. Watch which node wins under each setting.

3.2 Building the graph

The questions here are data-engineering questions. Nodes: companies (resolved to a canonical identifier so that "Acme Corp" and "ACME Corporation" are one node; entity resolution is the hard part), people, and events (fundraises, acquisitions, management changes). Edges: co-mention within an article or within a sentence, weighted by count and decayed by article age; relationship edges from the CRM (existing borrower, referral source, lost deal). Directed or undirected: co-mention is undirected, so symmetrise or use the undirected variant; CRM relationships may be directed. Edge weights go into M as weights divided by weighted out-degree. Things to say unprompted: how you handled hubs (a news wire mentioned with everyone; down-weight by inverse document frequency or drop), time decay (half-life in weeks), and how often the graph is rebuilt.

3.3 Evaluating a ranked list, and defending "4×"

A ranked lead list is evaluated like a retrieval system: precision@k (of the top k leads, how many became deals), recall@k (of the deals that closed, how many were in the top k), MRR or NDCG if you care about order, and lift relative to the previous list. "4× conversion" needs: the baseline (the old list's conversion rate over the same window and the same team), the window (quarters, since deal cycles are long), the denominator (leads contacted, not leads generated), and attribution (was the team also changed, was the market different, was there an A/B split or a before/after comparison). A before/after over a market upswing is not 4×. The strongest form of the claim is an interleaved or randomised test: half the outreach from each list, conversion measured per list. If you ran before/after, say so and say what you controlled for; an interviewer trusts a smaller, well-attributed number more than a large unattributed one.

Trap · why not a model? The reason PageRank beats a learned ranker here is usually label scarcity: a few hundred closed deals is not enough to train a ranker but is enough to seed PPR. Say that, then say what you would do with more labels (learning-to-rank with PPR score as a feature).
Pocket for bullet 3. p = (1−d)v + dMᵀp; d = 0.85; power iteration O(edges) per step, ~geometric convergence at rate d; PPR = v on seeds; dangling fix; precision@k and lift; the old list's conversion rate; the window; the test design.

4. The bullet claiming schema normalization with retrieve-then-select, SFT then DPO on hard negatives and synthetic distractors

Schema normalization maps fields from heterogeneous sources (a borrower's spreadsheet, a PDF table, a vendor's export) onto a canonical schema: "Total Rev (k$)" → revenue_total_usd_thousands. The classic formulation is schema matching; the LLM formulation is a two-stage pipeline:

  1. Retrieve. Embed the source field (name, sample values, surrounding header context) and the canonical fields with a bi-encoder; take the top-k canonical candidates by cosine similarity. This bounds the LLM's choice set and makes the task tractable for thousands of canonical fields.
  2. Select. Prompt the LLM with the source field, its sample values and the k candidates; it outputs one candidate (or "none"). SFT trains this selection on labelled mappings; the loss is cross-entropy on the chosen candidate's tokens.
  3. DPO on hard negatives. The rejected side of each pair is a hard negative: a candidate the retriever ranked highly that is wrong (revenue_net for a gross revenue field), or a synthetic distractor constructed to be confusable (same words, different unit or period). This teaches the model the distinctions that SFT's positive-only signal leaves ambiguous, which is the same reason dense retrievers are trained with hard negatives (DPR, Karpukhin et al.).

Metrics: top-1 accuracy of the full pipeline, recall@k of the retriever (if the right field is not in the top k, the selector cannot win; this bounds the system), and a confusion breakdown by error type (unit, period, gross/net, entity). Questions: why not a cross-encoder instead of a generative selector (answer: the LLM can also output "none" and a justification, and handles value-level evidence), how you prevented the selector from copying the first candidate (shuffle candidate order in training), how you chose k, and how DPO changed the error distribution relative to SFT alone.

Trap · SFT → DPO appears twice. Bullets 1 and 4 both say "SFT then DPO on hard negatives". That is fine (it is a reusable recipe) if you present it as such: "the same two-stage recipe, applied to two tasks; in the QA task the negatives were wrong derivations, in the schema task they were confusable fields." If you cannot explain how the negatives differed, the two bullets look like one bullet written twice.
Pocket for bullet 4. Retriever recall@k bounds the system; selector top-1; hard negatives from the retriever's own top-k; distractors by unit/period perturbation; candidate-order shuffling; the error-type breakdown before and after DPO.

5. The bullet claiming RLHF end to end: a LoRA reward model, clipped PPO, GAE, and a KL penalty to a frozen reference

This is the bullet that gets the most whiteboard time, because every term in it is a formula. It is also the bullet most at risk of contradicting bullet 1, which also mentions PPO.

5.1 Reward model

A Bradley–Terry reward model takes a prompt and a response and outputs a scalar r. On pairs (yw, yl) it is trained with loss −log σ(r(yw) − r(yl)). A LoRA reward model is the policy's base model with a LoRA adapter and a scalar head: it saves memory (the frozen base can be shared with the policy and the reference, so three of the four PPO models share one set of weights), and it is the standard trick for fitting PPO on a small cluster. Know: pair count, accuracy on a held-out pair set (70–80% is typical; higher suggests easy pairs), and how you guarded against reward hacking (length correlation of r, adversarial probes).

5.2 Clipped PPO, GAE, and the KL term

Per token, with ratio ρt = πθ(at|st) / πold(at|st) and advantage Ât, the clipped objective is

LCLIP = Et[ min( ρt Ât, clip(ρt, 1−ε, 1+ε) Ât ) ], ε ≈ 0.2

The reward for a response is the reward model's scalar on the final token, with a per-token penalty −β · log(πθ/πref) that keeps the policy near the frozen SFT reference (β ≈ 0.01–0.1; a KL that grows past a few nats per sequence is the first sign of hacking). The value model predicts expected return per token, and GAE turns rewards and values into advantages with a bias–variance knob λ:

δt = rt + γ V(st+1) − V(st),   Ât = Σl≥0 (γλ)l δt+l

With λ = 1 the advantage is the Monte-Carlo return minus the baseline (unbiased, high variance); with λ = 0 it is the one-step TD error (low variance, biased by V). In LLM RLHF γ is usually 1 and λ 0.95. The canvas below runs the recursion on a toy 12-token sequence so that you can say what λ does rather than recite it. Full derivations are in RL & Robotics; the systems side (four models, rollout vs training engines, weight sync) is in RL Systems.

5.3 Questions, and the consistency fix

Q. You list PPO in the TAT-QA bullet and "RLHF end to end" as a separate bullet. Same system?
A. Decide now. Either the RLHF bullet is the PPO run from bullet 1 (then merge them: one bullet for the TAT-QA result, one sub-clause saying it was done with the RLHF stack you built), or RLHF was built for a different task (then name the task and its metric). Two bullets that both say "PPO" with no task in the second one look like padding.
Q. Four models on 8×A100: how did they fit?
A. Shared frozen base in BF16 (42 GB) with three LoRA adapters (policy, value head, reward) switched at forward time; reference = base with adapters disabled; rollouts generated with vLLM on a subset of GPUs and weights synced each step, or with HF generate at lower throughput. Say which, and the rollout-to-train ratio of wall-clock.
Q. How did you know it was working?
A. Reward going up while KL stays bounded and a held-out, judge-free metric (exact match, human spot checks) also improves; reward up with KL exploding or length exploding is hacking.
Q. Why PPO and not DPO or GRPO?
A. PPO is the right answer when you have a learned reward model and no verifier and want on-policy samples; GRPO when the reward is verifiable; DPO when you have pairs and a small budget. If your answer is "because the tutorial used PPO", say instead what you would choose now and why.
Pocket for bullet 5. BT loss −log σ(rw − rl); clipped ratio with ε = 0.2; per-token KL β; δt and the (γλ)l sum; λ = 0.95, γ = 1; four models and the shared-base trick; RM accuracy; the KL-per-sequence you watched.

6. The bullet claiming >60% faster per-record processing from a multi-agent medical-record review system with RAG, MCP servers, and drift and hallucination guardrails

This bullet changes domain (from finance to clinical records), which an interviewer will notice; have the one-sentence context ready (a different client or an earlier project, and that the stack carried over). The technical content is an agent system, covered at depth in Agents and System Design.

  • Multi-agent review. Typically an orchestrator that splits a record into sections, specialist workers (medications, diagnoses, timeline, billing codes) that each produce structured findings with citations, and a reconciler that merges and flags conflicts. The questions: why multiple agents rather than one long-context call (parallelism, bounded context per worker, auditable intermediate outputs), how workers communicate (shared state vs message passing), and what the failure mode of the orchestrator was.
  • RAG over long records. Chunking strategy (by section, by encounter, with overlap), embedding model, hybrid lexical + dense retrieval for drug names and codes, re-ranking, and the citation format that lets a reviewer click back to the page. The number to carry is retrieval recall on a labelled set of "fact → page" pairs.
  • MCP servers. The Model Context Protocol standardises how a model calls tools and reads resources: a server exposes tools (functions with JSON schemas), resources (documents) and prompts; the client (the agent runtime) lists and invokes them over JSON-RPC. Saying "MCP servers" means you wrapped the record store, the coding database, and the guardrail checks as servers so that any agent runtime could use them; be ready for "what did each server expose and why MCP rather than plain function calling" (answer: decoupling, reuse across runtimes, permissioning per server).
  • Hallucination guardrails. Every extracted claim must cite a span; a verifier (NLI model or a second LLM call) checks that the span entails the claim; unsupported claims are dropped or flagged. Metric: unsupported-claim rate on an audited sample, before and after.
  • Drift guardrails. Input drift (record formats or vocabulary change; monitor embedding-distribution distance and OOV rates), model drift (provider updates a hosted model; pin versions, run a golden set nightly), and prompt drift (changes to prompts or tools go through the same golden set). Say which you monitored and what the alert threshold was.
  • Privacy. Clinical records are protected data: de-identification before any third-party model, access logging, and data residency. An interviewer in healthcare will ask before anything else.
Trap · ">60% faster" than what? Per-record wall-clock of the new system against the previous one (a human reviewer, a single-agent pipeline, a rules engine)? Measured over how many records? And the quality side: did accuracy on an audited set hold? Speed without a quality number reads as a system that skips work. Carry both: "median review time 42 → 16 minutes over 300 records, with unsupported-claim rate under 2% on a 100-record audit".
Pocket for bullet 6. Orchestrator/workers/reconciler; chunking and hybrid retrieval with recall on a labelled set; MCP tools/resources/prompts over JSON-RPC; citation-entailment check and its unsupported-claim rate; three kinds of drift and one monitor each; the baseline and sample size behind 60%.

7. The bullet claiming end-to-end ownership of AI products for an origination and financial-planning domain

"Owning end to end" is tested by asking for the pieces. The forward-deployed definition is: discovery (what decision the user makes, and what a wrong answer costs: that is where the materiality rubric came from), data (access, lineage, labelling budget), modelling (the ladder from prompting to RAG to fine-tuning, and why you stopped where you did), evaluation (offline metrics that predict the user's outcome, and the online metric that confirmed it), serving (latency and cost envelopes, vLLM, load tests), monitoring (drift, regressions, feedback capture), and the people (who used it, how adoption was measured). Prepare one story per piece and a one-line answer to "what would you do differently", because that question is the actual test of ownership.
